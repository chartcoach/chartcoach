## Reveal presentation elements in stages rather than all at once <!-- role: advice -->

Gradually reveal labels, symbols, and objects to establish the intended viewing order, and avoid revealing multiple important items simultaneously.

## Staged state changes act as attentional cues and reduce ambiguity about what to inspect next <!-- role: reason -->

When multiple elements appear together, viewers’ fixations can be scattered and the viewing order becomes unpredictable; staged reveals create discrete attentional events that guide inspection.

**Mechanism:** A visual state change (appearance/reveal) captures attention; sequencing state changes serializes attention so viewers process elements in the intended order.

**Evidence:** Eye tracking showed that newly revealed elements (e.g., a revealed label or symbol) attracted attention reliably, while simultaneous initial elements produced less consistent fixation order; redesign that added staged captions and cues improved proposition recall [@faradayDesigningEffectiveMultimedia1997].

**Notes:** Staged reveals can also be used to emphasize what is important, not just what is next.

## Where staged reveals are most useful <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand a multi-step explanation with multiple labeled parts.
- **Task:** Identify entities and follow cause/effect across steps.
- **Data:** Several co-present objects/labels competing for attention.
- **Chart Setting:** Short, time-compressed sequences where attention must be directed quickly.
- **Audience:** Low domain-knowledge viewers or first-time learners.
- **Success Criterion:** Viewers attend to and later recall each key proposition in sequence.

## When simultaneous reveals can be acceptable <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** All revealed elements are meant to be treated as a single unit with no required internal order. **Why:** Forced sequencing can add unnecessary time without improving comprehension.

## Tradeoffs of staged reveals <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** More steps can lengthen runtime and increase authoring complexity. **Risk:** Over-sequencing may feel artificial if the message is simple. **Mitigation:** Reserve staged reveals for key elements that must be noticed or remembered.

## Common mistakes in reveal sequencing <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Revealing several labeled objects at the beginning to “set the scene.” **Why it fails:** Viewers may not establish a consistent order and may neglect uncued elements.
- **Mistake:** Revealing a label long before its corresponding action. **Why it fails:** The label may be forgotten or not integrated with the later visual change.

## Quick checks for reveal sequencing quality <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Users recall later animated items but miss early static context (initial state, baseline condition). **Quick Check:** Step through the timeline and count how many new elements appear at each moment; if multiple important items appear together, expect competition. **Stronger Test:** Use immediate free recall and see whether propositions tied to early simultaneous reveals are omitted.

## Alternatives when you cannot stage reveals <!-- role: fix -->

- Add a speech cue aligned to the most important element in a crowded moment.
- Use a symbol cue to indicate the intended next target among simultaneous elements.
- Replace simultaneous reveals with one persistent overview followed by zoomed or highlighted focus segments.
- Split the content into two shorter sequences instead of one dense sequence.
