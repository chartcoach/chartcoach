## Show consequences and actions tied to the viewer’s world <!-- role: advice -->

Translate the data into concrete real-life outcomes and specific actions that could change those outcomes. Make the actions and impacts local, personal, or decision-linked whenever possible.

## Why tangible impacts and actions increase engagement <!-- role: reason -->

Abstract numbers become meaningful when viewers can map them to lived experience and plausible choices, which increases attention, emotional relevance, and perceived agency. Showing where decisions can influence outcomes also shifts interpretation from inevitability to avoidability, making the message feel less fatalistic and more actionable.

**Mechanism:** Concrete impacts reduce cognitive distance by anchoring values to recognizable situations, while actionable guidance increases perceived control and motivation to engage rather than disengage.

**Evidence:** Viewers criticized climate visualizations that lacked actionable guidance and preferred tangible outcomes or region-specific impacts; solution-oriented visuals were perceived as more motivating and as clarifying where action could influence the future [@schuster_being_2024]. Captions (especially titles) that highlighted avoidable outcomes through policy action shifted negative interpretations toward more hopeful, solution-focused readings [@koesten_what_2023]. Editorial framing that targets emotional resonance and balances urgency with fatigue was used to stimulate change in coverage of issues like climate change and COVID-19 [@gregory_data_2024].

**Notes:** “Action” can be personal, organizational, or policy-level, but it should be specific enough that a viewer can name what changes and who can change it.

## When to use tangible consequences and actions <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand what the numbers mean for real life and what choices could change outcomes.
- **Task:** Interpret scenarios, evaluate interventions, or decide what to support or do next.
- **Data:** Forecasts, scenarios, risk metrics, impacts over time, or comparisons between “with action” and “without action” outcomes.
- **Chart Setting:** Public-facing communication, journalism, education, dashboards for non-specialists, or any setting where attention and motivation are at risk.
- **Audience:** Mixed literacy audiences, non-experts, or expert audiences seeking decision leverage points.
- **Success Criterion:** Viewers can restate a concrete consequence and at least one feasible action (and who can take it) without external explanation.

## When not to follow it <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The visualization is for exploratory analysis where outcomes and interventions are not yet known or are outside the analyst’s remit. **Why:** Adding premature “actions” can bias exploration and falsely imply causal certainty or endorsement.

## Tradeoffs and risks <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Space and simplicity, because consequences and actions often require annotation, scoping, or scenario framing. **Risk:** Overstating certainty, implying causality, or nudging viewers toward a single “approved” choice. **Mitigation:** Be explicit about assumptions, uncertainty, and the decision level (personal vs organizational vs policy) without turning the chart into a manifesto.

## Common ways this goes wrong <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Showing only global aggregates with no local or personal mapping. **Why it fails:** Viewers cannot connect the numbers to lived experience, so the message stays abstract and easy to ignore.
- **Mistake:** Presenting impacts as doom-only with no leverage points. **Why it fails:** It encourages disengagement and fatigue rather than informed action.
- **Mistake:** Listing generic tips (“be greener”) disconnected from the chart’s variables. **Why it fails:** The guidance feels performative and does not explain how actions would move the displayed outcomes.

## Quick tests <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** A viewer can describe the trend but cannot say what it changes in real life or what could influence it. **Quick Check:** Ask, “What happens, to whom, where, and what can change it?” and see if the chart answers in one sentence. **Stronger Test:** Run a short think-aloud with 3–5 target viewers and check whether they independently name a concrete impact and a plausible action aligned with the data.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Add an impact translation that ties values to concrete outcomes (people affected, local conditions, costs, time-to-effect) using plain-language annotations.
- Include at least one explicit lever with directionality (what action changes which metric, and in what direction) and distinguish personal, organizational, and policy actions.
- Reframe titles and captions to emphasize avoidable outcomes and decision relevance rather than inevitability.
- If the chart cannot support action/impact mapping without clutter, split it into a small “what it means” panel (impacts) and a separate “what changes it” panel (levers or scenarios).
