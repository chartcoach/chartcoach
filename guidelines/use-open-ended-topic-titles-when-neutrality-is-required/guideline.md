## Prefer a topic-style title when a neutral stance is required <!-- role: advice -->

When you are required to be neutral, title the visualization by stating the topic and scope rather than asserting an argument or selecting a single supporting comparison.

## Why open-ended titles reduce explicit framing cues <!-- role: reason -->

Open-ended titles foreground what the visualization is about without directing viewers toward a particular interpretation or policy stance.

**Mechanism:** Removing evaluative claims and selective comparisons reduces the immediate cue that pushes viewers toward a specific conclusion.

**Evidence:** In title composition, open-ended frames dominated default and neutral titles, while slanted (supporting/non-supporting) titles rarely used open-ended framing; in viewing studies, slanted titles shifted the perceived main message [@kongFramesSlantsTitles2018].

**Notes:** Even “neutral” instructions can still produce slant, so neutrality should be verified rather than assumed.

## When this applies to your chart/title decisions <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Get oriented to what the chart covers without being steered toward a stance.
- **Task:** Initial comprehension, overview, or reference.
- **Data:** Controversial issues where multiple framings are plausible.
- **Chart Setting:** Journalism, public policy communications, education, or platforms requiring impartial presentation.
- **Audience:** Mixed-attitude audiences who may be sensitive to advocacy framing.
- **Success Criterion:** Readers can describe the chart’s contents without inferring a side from the title alone.

## When not to follow it <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The purpose is explicitly persuasive/advocacy communication with a declared stance. **Why:** A topic-only title may undercut the intended rhetorical goal.

## Tradeoffs and risks <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Topic titles may be less memorable and less compelling than argumentative titles. **Risk:** Readers may miss what is noteworthy if no key comparison is surfaced. **Mitigation:** Use a neutral title plus a separate explanatory caption that presents multiple noteworthy points.

## Common failure modes <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Calling a title “neutral” while embedding a selective comparison that favors one side. **Why it fails:** Selective emphasis functions as framing even without overtly emotional language.

## Quick tests <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** A reader can infer which policy position the publisher supports from the title alone. **Quick Check:** Remove the chart and ask if the title sounds like a conclusion rather than a description. **Stronger Test:** Ask multiple readers with differing attitudes to rate whether the title signals a side.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Rewrite the title to state only the topic, population, and time range.
- Move interpretive language (if needed) into a caption that acknowledges the alternative reading.
- Add a subtitle that lists both variables so the title does not privilege one.
- Present multiple titles (or allow viewers to compare titles) when publishing in highly contentious contexts.
