## Assume relative risk reduction will increase perceived effectiveness and persuasiveness versus absolute formats <!-- role: advice -->

Treat relative risk reduction as a format that will typically make an intervention seem more effective and more compelling than absolute risk reduction or number needed to treat, even when the underlying evidence is identical.

## Why relative formats systematically change perceived magnitude <!-- role: reason -->

Relative metrics express change as a proportion, which tends to feel larger than the corresponding absolute change, and this shifts judgments of effectiveness and willingness to adopt an intervention. Because the underlying effect is the same, the format can act as a framing lever rather than a purely informational choice.

**Mechanism:** Relative summaries highlight proportional improvement, which increases perceived effect size and can move intention-to-act without improving objective understanding.

**Evidence:** Compared with absolute risk reduction, relative risk reduction had little or no difference in understanding on average but increased perceived effectiveness and persuasiveness (moderate effect sizes) [@aklUsingAlternativeStatistical2011]. Compared with number needed to treat, relative risk reduction improved understanding, increased perceived effectiveness, and increased persuasiveness [@aklUsingAlternativeStatistical2011].

**Notes:** The reviewed outcomes are mostly surrogate outcomes (perception and hypothetical choices), and no included comparisons assessed actual behavior.

## When this expectation matters <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Evaluate or communicate intervention benefits.
- **Task:** Choose a numerical summary format for benefits in a message, leaflet, abstract, or decision aid.
- **Data:** Two-group outcome risks that can be summarized as relative risk reduction, absolute risk reduction, and number needed to treat.
- **Chart Setting:** Any setting where format choice could influence uptake (for example, patient-facing materials or clinician summaries).
- **Audience:** Consumers or clinicians; effects appear similar across these groups in the review.
- **Success Criterion:** Communication that supports informed choices rather than inadvertent persuasion.

## When the persuasion shift may be less relevant <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The purpose is explicitly advocacy and the communication is not intended to support value-consistent individual decision-making. **Why:** In that use case, increased persuasiveness is not a distortion but a desired outcome.

## Tradeoffs of treating relative risk reduction as persuasive framing <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** You may avoid a concise, familiar metric that some audiences expect. **Risk:** Overcorrecting can reduce engagement if readers find the presentation too technical. **Mitigation:** Keep summaries short but include both absolute and relative components.

## Common mistakes about relative risk reduction’s effect <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Assuming “more persuasive” implies “better understood.” **Why it fails:** Understanding and persuasiveness did not move together reliably; relative formats can persuade without improving comprehension.
- **Mistake:** Using relative risk reduction to compare interventions across different baseline risks without showing absolute risks. **Why it fails:** Relative metrics alone prevent fair magnitude comparison when baseline risks differ.

## Quick checks for unintended persuasion <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Stakeholders consistently choose the intervention more often when shown relative risk reduction than when shown absolute formats for the same scenario. **Quick Check:** Pilot-test two versions (relative-first vs absolute-first) and compare perceived effectiveness ratings. **Stronger Test:** Measure whether choices change while objective understanding stays flat.

## What to do instead when you want neutral communication <!-- role: fix -->

- Present absolute risk reduction or event rates as the primary summary and include relative risk reduction as secondary context.
- Add a short “out of N people” frequency translation of the absolute effect to anchor magnitude.
- Include both benefit and harm information in absolute terms to support balanced comparison.
