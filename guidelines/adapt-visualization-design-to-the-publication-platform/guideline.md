## Adapt framing and format to the outlet’s constraints <!-- role: advice -->

Design the visualization specifically for the platform or publication outlet it will appear in, including its available space, interaction model, and typical viewing time. Create platform-tailored versions when the same story must live in multiple places.

## Platform-fit improves comprehension and engagement <!-- role: reason -->

A visualization competes with different attention patterns and technical constraints across outlets, so a format that works in one medium can become unreadable or unpersuasive in another. Platform-fit reduces cognitive overhead by aligning message density, typography, and pacing with how audiences actually encounter the content in that context.

**Mechanism:** When complexity, layout, and annotation match the medium (e.g., mobile scrolling vs. print reading), viewers can parse the main message faster, avoid missing key context, and are more likely to engage long enough to understand the point.

**Evidence:** Practitioners report that framing and format should change with the publication medium, favoring simpler, emotionally resonant treatments for social media and more analytical ones for reports or news contexts [@schuster_being_2024]. Visual producers describe tailoring formats to platform expectations, such as concise standalone graphics for mobile and more complex layouts for print [@koesten_what_2023]. Social-media-specific design choices—simplifying to one message, limiting elements, using bolder typography/color, and producing platform-native variants like Instagram cards or TikToks—are emphasized as necessary to capture attention, with noted audience differences by age and consumption habits [@schuster_who_2023].

**Notes:** “Adapted” includes both content scope (single takeaway vs. multi-faceted analysis) and presentation (typography, aspect ratio, animation, and annotation).

## Situations that trigger platform-specific adaptation <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand and remember the main takeaway quickly, or scrutinize evidence in depth depending on outlet.
- **Task:** Summarize, contextualize, persuade, or support analysis with appropriate depth.
- **Data:** Any type; especially data that tempts dense encoding or multi-panel explanation.
- **Chart Setting:** Social feeds, mobile-first pages, slide decks, print spreads, news articles, reports, or platform-native video/animation.
- **Audience:** Mixed literacy levels; potentially distinct sub-audiences by platform (e.g., quick-scan readers vs. long-form readers).
- **Success Criterion:** The intended message is legible and interpretable in the viewing conditions of the outlet (time, device, attention, and interaction constraints).

## When not to optimize for the outlet <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** A standardized format is required for compliance, auditing, or cross-report comparability across time or teams. **Why:** Platform-driven changes can undermine consistency and make comparisons or governance reviews unreliable.

## Tradeoffs of platform-tailored design <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Producing multiple versions increases design and review time and can require extra tooling or workflows. **Risk:** Over-optimizing for attention (e.g., extreme simplification or spectacle) can reduce nuance or invite misinterpretation. **Mitigation:** Maintain a stable “source of truth” version and treat platform variants as derived presentations.

## Common ways this goes wrong <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Reposting a print-optimized or report-optimized graphic to social without changes. **Why it fails:** Text becomes illegible, the narrative is too dense for quick scanning, and key context is lost in constrained layouts.
- **Mistake:** Forcing all insights into a single social card or short clip. **Why it fails:** Important caveats and definitions disappear, turning an analytical message into an oversimplified claim.
- **Mistake:** Using platform-native effects (animations, bright colors, huge type) without a clear message hierarchy. **Why it fails:** Salience competes with comprehension, so attention rises while understanding drops.

## Fast checks for platform fit <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** The main takeaway cannot be understood within the typical attention window of the outlet (e.g., a few seconds in a feed) or becomes unreadable on the target device. **Quick Check:** View it in the real container (phone, feed preview, print size) and confirm the message and key labels are legible without zooming or extra explanation. **Stronger Test:** Run a brief pilot with representative users on the actual platform and ask them to state the takeaway and any uncertainties after a single viewing.

## Practical ways to adapt without losing substance <!-- role: fix -->

- Create separate platform variants that share the same underlying numbers but change aspect ratio, typography scale, and annotation density to match the outlet.
- Reduce each platform version to one primary message and move secondary details into captions, callouts, or follow-up panels where the medium supports it.
- Use outlet-appropriate packaging, such as carousel-style sequences for stepwise explanation on social or multi-panel layouts with richer legends and footnotes for print/report formats.
- Provide a linked or downloadable “full detail” companion for outlets that encourage quick scanning, so depth remains available without overloading the primary artifact.
