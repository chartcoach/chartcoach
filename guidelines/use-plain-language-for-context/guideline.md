## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

Use plain, concrete language in captions, annotations, labels, and titles. Avoid jargon, abstract phrasing, and unexplained technical terms.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

Plain language reduces interpretation effort and prevents readers—especially non-experts—from getting stuck on terminology instead of the message.

- **The Principle:** Cognitive load and shared vocabulary
- **The Evidence:** Lay viewers struggle with technical and high-level wording in contextual text and scenario descriptions [@schuster_being_2024]. Captions with more scientific vocabulary were harder to translate into clear takeaways than newer, plainer captions [@koesten_what_2023]. Practitioners emphasize simplifying language in annotations, labels, and titles as essential for clear communication [@schuster_who_2023].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

This advice is designed for moments where text is doing explanatory work beyond basic labeling.

- **User Goal:** Understand what the chart is showing and what to conclude from it (especially for scenarios, uncertainty, and methodology notes)
- **Data Type:** Any, especially complex topics (e.g., climate projections, uncertainty ranges, processed/derived metrics)
- **Audience:** General public, mixed audiences, or any setting where expertise is unknown

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

Use more technical language only when precision for experts is the primary requirement.

- **Scenario:** A specialist audience expects domain terminology (e.g., peer-reviewed figures, regulatory filings for experts, internal team analysis among specialists)
- **Reason:** Replacing established terms can reduce accuracy, create ambiguity, or conflict with standardized definitions

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

Using plain language can trade off precision and brevity.

- **The Sacrifice:** Short, technically exact terms may need longer explanations or parenthetical definitions
- **The Risk:** Over-simplifying can remove important nuance (e.g., what “uncertainty” specifically means or how data were processed)

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

These anti-patterns make context harder to understand without improving rigor.

- **The Wrong Fix:** Swapping in jargon to sound authoritative (e.g., “stochastic variability,” “downscaled ensembles”) without definitions
- **Why it fails:** Readers cannot map the terms to meaning, so they abandon the caption or misinterpret it [@schuster_being_2024]
- **The Wrong Fix:** Using abstract, high-level descriptions instead of concrete takeaways (e.g., “indicates heterogeneity across spatiotemporal domains”)
- **Why it fails:** It adds cognitive work and blocks narrative comprehension [@koesten_what_2023]
- **The Wrong Fix:** Explaining process before impact (long methodology-first captions)
- **Why it fails:** Readers miss the point because the framing and “so what” is buried [@schuster_who_2023]

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

- **Visual Sign:** Captions or annotations contain acronyms, discipline-specific terms, or dense abstract nouns; the key takeaway is not obvious on first pass
- **The Test:** Give the chart (with caption) to someone outside the domain and ask them to restate the message in one sentence. If they can’t, or they repeat jargon without understanding, rewrite in plainer language.

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

- **Quick Fix:** Replace jargon with everyday words and add short definitions for unavoidable terms (e.g., “uncertainty (the plausible range)”).
- **Best Fix:** Rewrite context in a “takeaway-first” structure: (1) what to notice, (2) what it means, (3) what to be careful about (e.g., uncertainty or assumptions), using concrete language and minimal required terminology.
