## Do not present relative risk reduction alone; include baseline risk or absolute risk change <!-- role: advice -->

When presenting a relative risk reduction, also present the baseline risk or the absolute risk reduction so the reader can see the absolute magnitude of benefit.

## Why pairing relative and absolute information reduces misinterpretation <!-- role: reason -->

Relative formats can inflate perceived impact because they express proportional change while hiding the starting risk, which makes the effect feel similar across very different baseline risks. Adding baseline risk or absolute change anchors interpretation to the real-world magnitude that supports fair benefit–harm comparisons.

**Mechanism:** Relative terms amplify perceived effect size by focusing attention on proportional change and away from absolute event rates.

**Evidence:** Relative risk reduction was perceived as larger and was more persuasive than absolute risk reduction and number needed to treat across included intervention scenarios, while understanding was not consistently improved versus absolute risk reduction [@aklUsingAlternativeStatistical2011].

**Notes:** The review primarily assessed understanding, perception, and persuasiveness in hypothetical scenarios rather than observed real-world behavior.

## When this rule applies to treatment effect communication <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Decide whether to start, recommend, or fund an intervention.
- **Task:** Judge the magnitude of benefit from an intervention effect summary.
- **Data:** Comparative risks (control vs intervention) that allow computation of relative risk reduction and absolute risk reduction.
- **Chart Setting:** Clinical summaries, patient decision aids, press releases, guideline tables, or study abstracts.
- **Audience:** Health consumers and health professionals making choices from brief effect summaries.
- **Success Criterion:** Decisions based on an accurate sense of absolute benefit magnitude.

## When presenting relative risk reduction alone may be acceptable <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The baseline risk and absolute risks are already explicitly and prominently displayed in the same visual unit, and the relative risk reduction is clearly secondary. **Why:** The anchoring information is already present, so the relative figure is less likely to dominate interpretation.

## Tradeoffs of always pairing relative and absolute information <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Additional numbers increase cognitive load and space requirements. **Risk:** Readers may focus on only one metric and ignore the other. **Mitigation:** Keep the baseline risk and absolute change adjacent to the relative figure and expressed in the same time horizon.

## Common mistakes when pairing relative and absolute metrics <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Providing relative risk reduction with no baseline risk and expecting readers to infer absolute benefit. **Why it fails:** The same relative reduction can correspond to trivial or large absolute changes, causing overestimation when baseline risk is low.
- **Mistake:** Mixing time horizons (for example, baseline risk over 10 years but relative reduction “per year”). **Why it fails:** The absolute magnitude becomes incomparable and misleading.

## Quick checks for balanced treatment effect summaries <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** A large share of readers describe the intervention as “highly effective” even when absolute risk reduction is small. **Quick Check:** Confirm that any relative risk reduction statement is paired with baseline risk or absolute risk reduction in the same sentence or table row. **Stronger Test:** Ask users to estimate how many people benefit out of a fixed number (for example, out of 1000) after reading the summary.

## What to do instead of relative-only reporting <!-- role: fix -->

- Add baseline risk and absolute risk reduction next to the relative risk reduction using the same population and time period.
- Present event rates for both groups (“risk reduced from X to Y”) so absolute differences are visible.
- Replace relative-only text with a short table row that includes control risk, intervention risk, and absolute difference.
