## Make the denominator visible in risk graphics when accuracy matters <!-- role: advice -->

When the goal is accurate probability judgment, use graphics that depict the numerator in the context of the full denominator (a part-to-whole display) rather than showing the numerator alone. Ensure the display makes “out of how many” visually clear.

## Why denominator context improves probability judgment <!-- role: reason -->

Risk visuals that show only numerators can increase perceived risk salience and encourage avoidance behaviors, but they can also bias magnitude judgments by removing the reference frame. Part-to-whole displays support correct interpretation by preserving the ratio structure.

**Mechanism:** Showing the whole establishes a stable reference that supports proportional reasoning and reduces overemphasis on the counted “bad outcomes.”

**Evidence:** Risk graphs depicting numerators alone emphasize risks and can promote risk-related behavior changes, while graphs depicting numerators in context of denominators promote accurate judgments [@anckerRethinkingHealthNumeracy2007].

**Notes:** This is a design choice that depends on whether the success criterion is accuracy or motivational impact.

## Where part-to-whole risk graphics fit best <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand how likely an outcome is and compare risks across options.
- **Task:** Judge magnitude and differences between probabilities.
- **Data:** Event probabilities expressed as percentages, frequencies, or ratios.
- **Chart Setting:** Decision aids, informed consent, comparative risk summaries.
- **Audience:** Mixed numeracy; users who may misinterpret isolated magnitudes.
- **Success Criterion:** Accurate reading of probabilities and correct comparisons across options.

## When not to prefer part-to-whole graphics <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The primary goal is to maximize risk avoidance behavior rather than probability accuracy. **Why:** Numerator-only emphasis may be more motivating in some behavior-change contexts.

## Tradeoffs of denominator-explicit graphics <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** More visual complexity and potentially less immediate emotional impact. **Risk:** Users may find denser visuals less approachable without textual guidance. **Mitigation:** Add brief explanatory text that states the conclusion the graphic is meant to support.

## Common failures in risk graphic selection <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Choosing a visually simple risk bar that encodes only the numerator because it “looks clean.” **Why it fails:** Clean appearance can come at the cost of removing denominator context needed for accurate interpretation.

## Quick tests for denominator comprehension <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Users can repeat the numerator (“10%”) but cannot explain the reference set (“10 out of 100”). **Quick Check:** Ask users to describe what the whole represents and how many outcomes occur within it. **Stronger Test:** Test accuracy on comparison questions across two risks using numerator-only versus part-to-whole displays.

## What to do instead if the chosen graphic is numerator-only <!-- role: fix -->

- Add an explicit denominator frame (e.g., a full 100% bar or a labeled “out of 100 people” container).
- Pair the graphic with a frequency statement that uses a consistent denominator.
- Provide both a numerator-only and a part-to-whole view, clearly labeled for “motivation” versus “accuracy.”
- Add a short text interpretation adjacent to the graphic stating the intended comparison.
