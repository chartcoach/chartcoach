## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->
Represent comparative medical risks and benefits using 100-unit pictographs (icon arrays) rather than statistical text or tables.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->
Visualizing probability as discrete units in a matrix improves user understanding.
*   **The Principle:** Dual Coding Theory. Processing congruent visual and verbal information creates stronger memory representations and integration.
*   **The Evidence:** In a study of 4,685 parents, [@tait_effect_2010] found that pictographs resulted in significantly higher "gist" (general meaning) and "verbatim" (exact number) understanding compared to numerical text or tables. This benefit persisted regardless of the parents' numeracy (mathematical literacy) levels.

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->
This format is specifically effective for informed consent and risk communication.
*   **User Goal:** Understanding the likelihood of side effects or benefits of a medical treatment.
*   **Data Type:** Discrete probabilities (counts out of 100).
*   **Audience:** Lay audiences (e.g., parents) with varying levels of education and numeracy.

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->
*   **Scenario:** When the data requires precise comparison of very small decimals not visible on a 100-unit grid (e.g., 0.001%).
*   **Reason:** A standard 100-unit grid cannot represent fractions of a single percent without distortion or zooming.

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->
*   **The Sacrifice:** Space. A 10x10 grid takes up significantly more layout room than a single sentence or table row.
*   **The Bias:** [@tait_effect_2010] noted that parents viewing pictographs perceived risks as lower and benefits as higher compared to text/tables, which may influence decision-making (making them more likely to consent).

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->
*   **The Wrong Fix:** Using bar graphs or pie charts for risk.
*   **Why it fails:** While not tested against these specific types in this paper, the authors note that pictographs are generally superior to other abstract charts for communicating discrete frequency relationships to laypeople.

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->
*   **Visual Sign:** Are you using percentages in text (e.g., "25%") or rows of numbers?
*   **The Test:** Ask a lay user to identify "how many people out of 100" would experience the effect. If they struggle with the text/table, switch to a pictograph.

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->
*   **Quick Fix:** Add a text explanation converting percentages to frequencies (e.g., "25 out of 100").
*   **Best Fix:** Convert the percentage into a 10x10 matrix of icons, coloring the affected number of units (e.g., 25 blue blocks, 75 grey blocks).
