## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->
Do not shy away from high visual density or "chart junk" if your goal is memorability; utilize elaborate visual elements rather than stripping the chart down to its bare minimum.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->
Research by [@borkin_what_2013] indicates that visualizations with a "bad" data-ink ratio (lots of non-data ink) and high visual density are more memorable than minimalist, "clean" charts. This contradicts the conventional wisdom of Edward Tufte regarding retention. The researchers suggest that "visual difficulties" might force the viewer to expend more cognitive effort, or that the extra visual details provide more "hooks" for memory storage.

*   **The Principle:** Cognitive Engagement / Visual Hooks
*   **The Evidence:** [@borkin_what_2013] (Section 7.2, Fig 5 & 6)

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->
Apply this when designing for venues where the visualization needs to stand out and be remembered among many others.

*   **User Goal:** Retaining the image in memory over time.
*   **Context:** Infographics, news media, or marketing materials.
*   **Audience:** Viewers who are browsing rather than analyzing.

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->
*   **Scenario:** Rapid decision-making environments (e.g., dashboards, control rooms).
*   **Reason:** While dense charts are memorable, they take longer to process (4.8 minutes for 120 images in the study). High density may slow down immediate data extraction.

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->
*   **The Sacrifice:** Simplicity and potentially immediate readability.
*   **The Risk:** The paper notes that a memorable visualization is not necessarily a *comprehensible* one. You risk confusing the user about the data values in exchange for them remembering the image.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->
*   **The Wrong Fix:** Adding random clutter or noise that obscures the data.
*   **Why it fails:** The density should ideally support the narrative or theme (contextual redundancy), not random noise which might just annoy the user.

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->
*   **Visual Sign:** Is there significant white space? Is every pixel strictly dedicated to data?
*   **The Test:** Assess the "Visual Density" on a scale of Low to High. If it is "Low" (minimalist), it is statistically less likely to be remembered than a "High" density image.

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->
*   **Quick Fix:** Add background textures, detailed axes, or annotated text that provides context.
*   **Best Fix:** Design the visualization as part of a larger illustrative narrative (like a poster or infographic) rather than a standalone statistical plot.
