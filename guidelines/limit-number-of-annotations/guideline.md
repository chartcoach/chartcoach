## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

Use annotation to point viewers to the most important comparisons, but keep callouts to a small number of points.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

- **The Principle:** Language can guide attention effectively, but too many callouts compete for attention and overwhelm processing.
- **The Evidence:** The paper recommends annotation as guidance while warning that only a limited number of items can be called out without overwhelming the viewer [@zacksDesigningGraphsDecisionMakers2020].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understanding the intended takeaway quickly while still being able to verify by inspecting the data.
- **Data Type:** Charts with many possible takeaways where you need to prioritize.
- **Audience:** General audiences.

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

- **Scenario:** Instructional material explicitly teaching how to read a chart (step-by-step).
- **Reason:** Teaching contexts may justify more guided text, but this is different from decision communication [@zacksDesigningGraphsDecisionMakers2020].

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

- **The Sacrifice:** You cannot highlight every interesting detail.
- **The Risk:** Un-annotated secondary insights may be missed.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **The Wrong Fix:** Annotating everything that looks notable.
- **Why it fails:** Competing callouts remove hierarchy and increase cognitive load [@zacksDesigningGraphsDecisionMakers2020].

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

- **Visual Sign:** The chart looks like a wall of labels; viewers don’t know where to start.
- **The Test:** Hide the data and look only at callouts—if there are too many to read in a few seconds, you likely over-annotated.

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

- **Quick Fix:** Keep only the 1–3 callouts tied to the decision question.
- **Best Fix:** Use a layered approach: one primary annotated view plus optional supplementary views for secondary points [@zacksDesigningGraphsDecisionMakers2020].
