## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

Do not use relative risk reduction (RRR) as the sole or primary summary statistic in decision-support materials when neutrality is required, because it is more persuasive than absolute formats.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

RRR systematically increases perceived effectiveness and willingness to adopt an intervention compared with ARR and NNT, even when understanding changes little, which can bias choices in hypothetical decision contexts.

- **The Principle:** Format-driven persuasion bias
- **The Evidence:** In [@aklUsingAlternativeStatistical2011], RRR vs ARR increased persuasiveness (SMD 0.66) and RRR vs NNT increased persuasiveness (SMD 0.65).

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Make an informed choice aligned with personal values (not simply maximize uptake).
- **Data Type:** Intervention effect summaries (benefits/harms) where multiple equivalent effect metrics could be shown.
- **Audience:** Both consumers and professionals, since overall differences between these groups were not consistent/large in [@aklUsingAlternativeStatistical2011].

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

- **Scenario:** Materials explicitly intended to persuade adoption (e.g., advocacy messaging) rather than support value-consistent decisions.
- **Reason:** The rule is about neutrality; [@aklUsingAlternativeStatistical2011] documents that RRR is more persuasive.

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

- **The Sacrifice:** You may lose a format that is compelling and commonly used.
- **The Risk:** If you remove RRR entirely, some audiences may miss a familiar comparative metric.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **The Wrong Fix:** Assuming “more persuasive” implies “better understood.”
- **Why it fails:** [@aklUsingAlternativeStatistical2011] found little/no difference in understanding for RRR vs ARR on average while persuasiveness increased.

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

- **Visual Sign:** The headline statistic is RRR (e.g., “cuts risk by 50%”) and absolute effects are absent or minimized.
- **The Test:** Ask whether a reader can compare benefits and harms using absolute magnitudes from the display; if not, the design is likely persuasion-weighted.

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

- **Quick Fix:** Reorder so absolute effects (ARR and/or event rates) appear first, with RRR secondary.
- **Best Fix:** Provide event rates for both groups plus ARR (and optionally NNT), and explicitly label RRR as relative (echoing appropriateness concerns discussed in [@aklUsingAlternativeStatistical2011]).
