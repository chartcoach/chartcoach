## Signal what to attend to and how to structure the lesson <!-- role: advice -->

Include cues that guide learners toward essential words and visual elements and help them organize the explanation.

## Why signaling reduces incidental processing <!-- role: reason -->

In dense or confusing multimedia, learners may allocate limited capacity to nonessential details or fail to see how parts relate, increasing incidental processing and reducing capacity for building coherent mental models. Signals steer selection and organization processes toward goal-relevant information so essential processing is more likely to succeed within capacity limits.

**Mechanism:** Cues constrain attention and structure-building, decreasing wasted processing on irrelevant or poorly organized elements.

**Evidence:** Transfer improved for a signaled narrated animation compared with an unsignaled version in an experimental comparison, consistent with a signaling effect [@mayerNineWaysReduce2003].

**Notes:** Signals can target selecting words, selecting images, organizing words, and organizing images.

## When learners face dense or confusing multimedia <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Identify key steps and relationships in an explanation.
- **Task:** Select relevant elements and organize them into a coherent structure.
- **Data:** Many details, side facts, or visually complex graphics.
- **Chart Setting:** Narrated animation with potentially distracting or confusing content.
- **Audience:** Learners likely to be unsure what matters most.
- **Success Criterion:** Higher transfer with the same underlying content.

## When signaling is less necessary <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The presentation is already highly concise and clearly structured with minimal ambiguity about what is essential. **Why:** Additional cues may offer little benefit and can add visual clutter.

## Tradeoffs of signaling <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Additional design effort and potential screen clutter. **Risk:** Over-signaling can distract or make the experience feel heavy-handed. **Mitigation:** Keep signals focused on the essential structure and content.

## Common signaling misuses <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Adding decorative highlights that do not map to the lesson’s essential structure. **Why it fails:** It can increase incidental processing rather than reduce it.

## Checks for whether cues are working <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Learners focus on nonessential details and miss the causal chain. **Quick Check:** Ask a learner to point out the most important steps; if they choose peripheral details, signals are insufficient or misleading. **Stronger Test:** Compare transfer performance with and without signaling while keeping content constant.

## Alternatives if you cannot add signals <!-- role: fix -->

- Weed extraneous material so fewer distractions compete for attention.
- Segment the lesson so learners have time to organize and integrate each chunk.
- Align related text and graphics to reduce the need for scanning.
- Synchronize corresponding narration and visuals to reduce memory holding demands.
