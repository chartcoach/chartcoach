## Treat titles as part of the visualization’s message, not as a neutral label <!-- role: advice -->

Assume your title will meaningfully shape what viewers think the visualization “says,” and design/evaluate it as part of the visualization rather than as a decorative label.

## Why titles can dominate the perceived message <!-- role: reason -->

Visualization titles can function like headlines: they focus attention on one aspect of the data and prompt viewers to interpret the same chart as supporting different conclusions.

**Mechanism:** A slanted title cues which variable, comparison, or trend to attend to, causing viewers to encode the title’s framing as the chart’s “main message.”

**Evidence:** Viewers’ recalled “main message” significantly depended on whether they saw a supporting vs non-supporting title, with many viewers deriving opposing messages from the same visualization [@kongFramesSlantsTitles2018].

**Notes:** Viewers often reproduce the title’s message in recall even when they report not remembering the title.

## When this applies to your chart/title decisions <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand the takeaway or implication of a chart about a debated policy or contentious issue.
- **Task:** Summarize the “main message,” make sense of what the data implies, or decide what to believe.
- **Data:** Multivariate displays where different variables can support different sides (e.g., absolute vs per-capita; dollars vs percent).
- **Chart Setting:** Standalone charts (e.g., social media embeds) or any context where the title is highly visible.
- **Audience:** General audiences who may rely on the title to decide what the chart means.
- **Success Criterion:** Viewers’ takeaway reflects the data structure rather than only the title’s framing.

## When not to follow it <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The “title” is not visible at viewing time (e.g., removed in the embed or replaced by a platform label). **Why:** It cannot frame interpretation if it is not seen.

## Tradeoffs and risks <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Treating titles as consequential increases review time and editorial effort. **Risk:** Over-correcting toward neutrality can reduce clarity about what the chart is meant to convey. **Mitigation:** Separate “what it shows” from “what it implies” so clarity does not require a slant.

## Common failure modes <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Writing the title last as a purely descriptive afterthought. **Why it fails:** Titles can still steer interpretation even if the author intends them to be neutral.

## Quick tests <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Different readers summarize different “main messages” that track the title wording more than the plotted relationships. **Quick Check:** Swap the title to emphasize a different variable and see if your own takeaway flips. **Stronger Test:** Ask a few readers to write the main message after a short viewing and compare their summaries across alternative titles.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Write at least two alternative titles that emphasize different variables/trends and check whether each produces a different plausible takeaway.
- Separate a neutral “what the chart contains” title from any interpretive claim by moving interpretation to a caption or body text.
- Explicitly name the variable used in any claim so the title cannot be read as applying to the other variable.
- Provide access to multiple plausible framings (e.g., alternate titles or a viewer-composed title step) when publishing in contexts where titles strongly guide reading.
