## Write the main point directly on the chart <!-- role: advice -->

Annotate the chart with short sentences that state the specific patterns or conclusions you want readers to notice. Write these as plain-language takeaways, even if a careful reader could discover them without help.

## Making key insights explicit reduces missed or wrong interpretations <!-- role: reason -->

When a visualization leaves the “so what” entirely implicit, readers must spend time searching, inferring, and deciding what matters; many will stop early or form a different takeaway than intended. Writing the intended insights on the chart makes the reading path shorter and more reliable by directing attention to the relevant features and reducing the chance of misinterpretation [@muth_readers_time_2017].

**Mechanism:** Explicit annotations turn a discovery task (“find what matters”) into a recognition task (“confirm what matters”), which reduces exploration time and ambiguity.

**Evidence:** Adding written takeaways on the chart helps readers understand the intended points quickly and reduces the chance they miss or misread the message in the data [@muth_readers_time_2017].

**Notes:** This is especially valuable when the chart is embedded in a broader story and readers may skim rather than analyze.

## Use this when readers need to understand your intended message quickly <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand the key message of the chart without deep exploration.
- **Task:** Identify the main pattern(s), peaks/troughs, changes, or comparisons the author considers important.
- **Data:** Any dataset where multiple patterns are visible but only some are central to the narrative.
- **Chart Setting:** News, reports, dashboards, presentations, or mobile contexts where attention is limited.
- **Audience:** Mixed or non-expert audiences; readers who may skim.
- **Success Criterion:** Faster comprehension of the intended takeaway with fewer wrong inferences [@muth_readers_time_2017].

## When not to do it <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The purpose is open-ended exploration where prescribing a single takeaway would narrow interpretation too much. **Why:** Strong takeaway annotations can make readers feel there is “nothing left to discover,” reducing exploratory value [@muth_readers_time_2017].

## Tradeoffs you accept <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** You spend more time writing and placing annotations and may use more space in the chart. **Risk:** Over-annotation can feel didactic and can crowd the graphic, especially on small screens [@muth_readers_time_2017]. **Mitigation:** Keep annotations short and focused on the few most important points.

## What people often do wrong <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Publishing the chart with no on-chart explanation and assuming readers will infer the intended message from the data alone. **Why it fails:** Readers may miss the key point, take longer than they’re willing to, or draw a different conclusion than intended [@muth_readers_time_2017].

## Fast ways to verify it worked <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** A reader must stare, hover, or read surrounding text to figure out what they should notice. **Quick Check:** Hide the article text and ask, “Does the chart itself tell me what matters within a few seconds?” **Stronger Test:** Ask a colleague to state the chart’s takeaway after a brief glance; compare their answer to your intended message [@muth_readers_time_2017].

## What to do instead if it’s not working <!-- role: fix -->

- Write one short annotation for the primary insight and remove secondary commentary until the chart reads cleanly.
- Replace vague labels with concrete statements about the pattern (for example, “usually peaks around…” rather than just naming the axis or series).
- If space is tight, prioritize annotating the highest-value moment(s) in the data and delete lower-importance callouts.
- If the story needs multiple takeaways, split into separate charts rather than forcing everything into one view [@muth_readers_time_2017].
