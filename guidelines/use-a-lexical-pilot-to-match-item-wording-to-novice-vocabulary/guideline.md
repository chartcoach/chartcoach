## Use a lexical pilot to match item wording to novice vocabulary <!-- role: advice -->

Before final item writing, run a pilot where non-expert participants describe what they learn from charts in their own words, and use that vocabulary to draft and revise item stems.

## Why lexical alignment reduces construct-irrelevant difficulty <!-- role: reason -->

If test items use jargon or unfamiliar phrasing, performance can reflect language comprehension rather than visualization literacy. Using vocabulary and phrasing observed from the target population reduces this confound and improves item clarity.

**Mechanism:** Lexical alignment reduces extraneous language-processing load, keeping the cognitive demand focused on reading the visualization.

**Evidence:** VLAT collected thousands of participant-written statements about tasks and used the resulting vocabulary and phrases as reference material when writing and revising items to match the target population’s lexical level [@leeVLATDevelopmentVisualization2017].

**Notes:** Lexical alignment complements, but does not replace, expert review for task-construct alignment.

## When to apply this <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Write items that measure visualization reading, not vocabulary knowledge.
- **Task:** Draft item stems and response options for non-expert test takers.
- **Data:** Any; lexical risk increases with specialized attributes and contexts.
- **Chart Setting:** Standardized tests, surveys, or training quizzes.
- **Audience:** Non-expert adults, native-language specific groups.
- **Success Criterion:** Items are understood consistently with minimal clarification.

## When not to depend on a lexical pilot alone <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The assessment intentionally targets professional terminology comprehension (e.g., domain training). **Why:** Simplifying language may remove part of the intended construct.

## Tradeoffs <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Extra time and recruitment effort to run the pilot. **Risk:** Pilot vocabulary may reflect one locale or subgroup and not generalize. **Mitigation:** Sample broadly from the intended population and avoid overly colloquial phrasing.

## Common mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Using expert-authored technical wording because it is “more precise,” despite being unfamiliar to novices. **Why it fails:** It adds language barriers unrelated to visualization reading.

## Quick checks <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Participants ask for definitions of words in stems or misinterpret what the question is asking. **Quick Check:** Ask a few target users to paraphrase each item stem without answering it. **Stronger Test:** Track item-level completion time spikes that suggest language friction rather than visual reasoning difficulty.

## What to do if wording is still causing confusion <!-- role: fix -->

- Replace jargon with plain-language equivalents drawn from pilot responses.
- Shorten stems so the key task request is explicit and early in the sentence.
- Standardize phrasing across items for the same task category to reduce switching costs.
- Remove or redefine ambiguous comparative terms and ensure only one interpretation fits the chart.
