## Use imagery intentionally when you want a clearer value message <!-- role: advice -->

Use strong visual embellishment when your goal includes communicating a specific stance or value message, not just reporting numbers. Assume readers will infer more messaging from embellished charts than from plain ones.

## Why embellishment amplifies perceived messaging <!-- role: reason -->

Evocative imagery adds semantic and emotional cues that can frame the data, making readers more likely to infer intent beyond neutral presentation. This can improve clarity of the intended message, but it also increases the chance of perceived bias.

**Mechanism:** Non-data visuals provide interpretive cues that shape how viewers summarize the chart’s “point,” beyond describing categories and trends.

**Evidence:** Value-message descriptions were significantly better (and more frequently articulated) for embellished charts than plain charts during chart description, immediate recall, and long-term recall [@batemanUsefulJunkEffects2010a].

**Notes:** This effect occurred even when other comprehension components (subject, categories, trend) were similar during on-screen description [@batemanUsefulJunkEffects2010a].

## When message-framing is part of the goal <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand not only what changed, but what the chart “is trying to say.”
- **Task:** Summarize the intended message or stance implied by the graphic.
- **Data:** Any dataset where a narrative framing is being communicated alongside quantities.
- **Chart Setting:** Editorial, presentation, or mass-media style contexts where framing is expected.
- **Audience:** General audiences who may rely on visual cues for gist.
- **Success Criterion:** Readers correctly identify the intended message and remember it later.

## When not to amplify value message <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** You must minimize rhetorical framing and avoid implying judgment. **Why:** Embellishment increases the likelihood that viewers perceive a value message, which may be inappropriate for neutral reporting [@batemanUsefulJunkEffects2010a].

## Tradeoffs and risks <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Some neutrality and perceived objectivity. **Risk:** Readers may accept the visual framing as part of the “facts,” increasing the impact of bias. **Mitigation:** Be explicit about intent and ensure the framing matches the underlying data.

## Common failure modes <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Using emotionally loaded imagery when the communication goal is neutral briefing. **Why it fails:** The imagery can shift the takeaway toward judgment rather than accurate understanding.

## Quick tests <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Different readers infer conflicting “messages” that go beyond the data. **Quick Check:** Ask readers what message they think the chart communicates; high variance suggests unintended framing. **Stronger Test:** Compare message recall after a multi-week delay to see whether the framing persists [@batemanUsefulJunkEffects2010a].

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Use a plain chart when the goal is neutral description rather than persuasion.
- Remove or soften imagery that implies positive/negative judgment while keeping the same data depiction.
- Separate narrative framing into accompanying text instead of embedding it in the graphic.
- Include a clearly worded title that states the intended takeaway directly if you must avoid visual rhetoric.
