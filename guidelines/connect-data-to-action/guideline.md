## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

Explicitly link data visualizations to real-world consequences and meaningful actions. Do not present abstract metrics without showing how they relate to daily life or specific choices.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

Abstract data often feels distant to lay audiences. Connecting numbers to personal relevance or actionable solutions increases engagement and motivation.

*   **The Principle:** **Tangibility and Agency.** Viewers struggle to connect with large-scale abstractions. Localizing data (e.g., specific regional impacts) or visualizing the outcome of potential solutions makes the data feel "real." Furthermore, framing data around solutions rather than just problems prevents paralysis.
*   **The Evidence:** Interviews show that both experts and lay viewers criticize visualizations that lack actionable guidance. Lay viewers specifically prefer scenarios with tangible outcomes, such as local temperature rise [@schuster_being_2024]. Additionally, captions that highlight avoidable outcomes through policy action can shift a viewer's interpretation from negative to hopeful [@koesten_what_2023].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

Apply this when the goal is persuasion, education, or behavior change, particularly regarding complex global issues.

*   **User Goal:** To understand "How does this affect me?" or "What can I do?"
*   **Data Type:** Abstract, large-scale phenomena (e.g., Climate Change, Epidemiology/COVID-19, Economic shifts).
*   **Audience:** The general public or non-experts who need to bridge the gap between data and personal reality.

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

Avoid this approach when the primary goal is neutral observation or raw scientific analysis.

*   **Scenario:** Scientific reporting for peer review or exploratory data analysis tools.
*   **Reason:** Adding "actionable" framing can introduce perceived bias or obscure the raw data needed for independent analysis.

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

Connecting data to action requires editorial judgment, which changes the nature of the visualization.

*   **The Sacrifice:** Pure neutrality. You are guiding the viewer toward a specific conclusion or behavior.
*   **The Risk:** Audience fatigue. If every visualization demands high emotional engagement or urgent action, viewers may disengage entirely to protect themselves [@gregory_data_2024].

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

Designers often confuse "scaring" the audience with "engaging" them.

*   **The Wrong Fix:** Showing catastrophic data (Doom-scrolling) without offering a path forward.
*   **Why it fails:** Without a visualized solution or action, negative data creates anxiety rather than motivation, leading to avoidance [@koesten_what_2023].

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

*   **Visual Sign:** Does the chart only show a steep line going up or down?
*   **The Test:** Ask, "If I am a viewer seeing this chart, do I know what step to take next?" If the answer is "No," the rule is broken.

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

*   **Quick Fix:** Rewrite the title and captions to focus on the solution or the specific consequence, rather than the metric itself [@koesten_what_2023].
*   **Best Fix:** Visualize scenarios. Show two futures: one where action is taken and one where it is not, illustrating the tangible difference (e.g., showing specific local impacts) [@schuster_being_2024].
