## Make the most important text the most visually prominent <!-- role: advice -->

Use size, weight, and contrast so that the key message is read first and supporting details recede. Keep secondary text smaller, lighter, and less prominent.

## Visual prominence creates an intended reading path <!-- role: reason -->

Readers don’t read charts linearly; they are pulled toward high-contrast, large, bold elements first. A clear hierarchy ensures attention lands on the intended message before details, reducing confusion and improving retention of the main point.

**Mechanism:** Strong typographic contrast directs attention and establishes priority, helping readers allocate limited attention efficiently.

**Evidence:** Using font sizes, styles, and colors to emphasize what matters most helps control what readers see first, while small, light text can hold secondary information [@muth_text_in_data_visualizations_2022].

**Notes:** A practical approach is to start with de-emphasized text and then selectively promote only what must not be missed.

## Apply when the chart includes multiple text elements <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Grasp the takeaway quickly, then consult details as needed.
- **Task:** Scan, orient, and interpret with minimal confusion.
- **Data:** Any; especially explanatory graphics with titles, descriptions, labels, and notes.
- **Chart Setting:** Static charts or story graphics where text competes for attention.
- **Audience:** Broad audiences and skimmers.
- **Success Criterion:** Readers can identify the main message without reading everything.

## When equal emphasis may be acceptable <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** All text elements are equally critical and must be read in full to avoid misunderstanding. **Why:** De-emphasizing any part would hide essential context.

## Trade neutrality for guidance <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Less uniform visual appearance across text elements. **Risk:** Over-emphasis can make the chart feel shouty or biased. **Mitigation:** Promote only one primary element and keep supporting text legible but subdued.

## Common hierarchy mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Styling many text elements as bold or high-contrast. **Why it fails:** If everything is emphasized, nothing stands out and the reading order collapses.

## Quick checks <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Your eyes don’t know where to start, or you read details before the main point. **Quick Check:** Squint at the chart; the most important text should remain the most noticeable. **Stronger Test:** Show the chart for a few seconds and ask what the viewer remembers; if they recall a detail instead of the main message, hierarchy may be off.

## Fixes that restore hierarchy <!-- role: fix -->

- Reduce contrast and weight of sources, notes, and secondary explanations.
- Increase prominence of the main takeaway text (often the title) while keeping it concise.
- Use bold within annotations for emphasis instead of adding more font sizes.
- Remove nonessential text so remaining elements can have a clear priority.
