## Make it easy to take a more active role in shared decisions for African American veterans <!-- role: advice -->

When designing materials for mixed-race veteran clinics, include explicit affordances for patients to take a more active role, anticipating that African American veterans may want more involvement than they currently experience.

## Why this can reduce dissatisfaction with decision roles <!-- role: reason -->

If a subgroup is more likely to be dissatisfied with their current involvement and wants more activity, materials that only support passive receipt of recommendations can amplify dissatisfaction and reduce perceived partnership.

**Mechanism:** Participation affordances (ways to ask, choose, and state preferences) reduce friction between preferred and actual roles.

**Evidence:** African American veterans more often preferred a more active role than the one they actually played compared with White veterans, and Whites more often reported satisfaction with their current role [@rodriguezHealthLiteracyNumeracy2013]. Racial groups did not differ in their usual or preferred role categories at the coarse level, but did differ in the mismatch between preferred and actual roles [@rodriguezHealthLiteracyNumeracy2013].

**Notes:** This is about aligning materials with involvement preferences, not about trust (which did not differ by race in this sample).

## When this applies in clinic decision tools <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Increase patient involvement to match preference during care decisions.
- **Task:** Move from passive receipt to collaborative or more active participation.
- **Data:** Any information used to choose among options.
- **Chart Setting:** Visit-based shared decision-making tools and take-home decision aids.
- **Audience:** Clinics with substantial African American veteran populations.
- **Success Criterion:** Fewer users report wanting “more involvement than I currently have” after using the tool.

## When not to follow it <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The decision is urgent and must be made immediately with limited discussion time. **Why:** The workflow constraint, not the material, limits participation.

## Tradeoffs and risks <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** More space/time in the artifact to support participation. **Risk:** Too many prompts can overwhelm users who prefer passive roles. **Mitigation:** Keep participation elements optional and clearly separable from the main explanation.

## Common failure modes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Provide only explanatory content without a way to express preferences or questions. **Why it fails:** African American veterans more often wanted a more active role than their current one, so explanation alone may not address dissatisfaction [@rodriguezHealthLiteracyNumeracy2013].
- **Mistake:** Assume race differences will appear only as lower trust and focus solely on reassurance. **Why it fails:** Trust did not differ by race in this sample, while mismatch in decision-role satisfaction did [@rodriguezHealthLiteracyNumeracy2013].

## Quick tests <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Users say “I still don’t know what I’m supposed to do/say here” during a decision conversation. **Quick Check:** Ask whether the artifact helps them take the role they want in the conversation. **Stronger Test:** Compare preferred-vs-actual role mismatch reports before and after introducing the material in a clinic with a substantial African American veteran population.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Add explicit prompts for patients to state preferences, concerns, or questions during the decision.
- Provide a simple way to indicate a choice or leaning that can be discussed with the clinician.
- Include a short section that clarifies what parts of the decision the patient can influence.
- Test whether African American veterans report improved satisfaction with their involvement after using the material.
