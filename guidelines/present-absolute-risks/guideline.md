## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->
Display risk information as absolute risks (e.g., "Risk reduced from 4% to 2%") rather than relative risks (e.g., "Risk reduced by 50%") or Number Needed to Treat (NNT).

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->
Relative risk creates a framing effect where changes appear larger and treatments seem more effective than they actually are. NNT is the most difficult format for patients to understand.
*   **The Principle:** Denominator Neglect / Framing Bias.
*   **The Evidence:** Research consistently shows that treatments are viewed more favorably and risks appear larger when presented as relative risks compared to absolute risks [@fagerlin_helping_2011].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->
*   **User Goal:** Comparing the efficacy of treatment options.
*   **Data Type:** Clinical trial results or risk reduction statistics.
*   **Audience:** Patients, medical students, and physicians (all are susceptible to this bias).

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->
*   **Scenario:** Comparative risk information (comparing an individual to the "average person").
*   **Reason:** While potentially biasing, knowing how one compares to a population average can be persuasive and informative for screening behaviors, though the authors advise caution [@fagerlin_helping_2011].

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->
*   **The Sacrifice:** The "persuasiveness" of the data. Interventions will look less impressive in absolute terms (e.g., a 1% absolute drop vs. a 50% relative drop).
*   **The Risk:** Patients may undervalue a treatment that is clinically significant but numerically small in absolute terms.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->
*   **The Wrong Fix:** Using Number Needed to Treat (NNT) as a "simplified" metric.
*   **Why it fails:** Studies show NNT is consistently the hardest format for patients to comprehend and should never be the sole method of presentation [@fagerlin_helping_2011].

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->
*   **Visual Sign:** Do the charts or labels use big percentages like "50% reduction" without showing the underlying base rates (e.g., 2 in 100 vs 1 in 100)?
*   **The Test:** Look for the denominator. If the graphic implies a reduction ratio without showing the total population count, it is likely using relative risk.

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->
*   **Quick Fix:** Add the baseline numbers to the text (e.g., "50% reduction (from 4% to 2%)").
*   **Best Fix:** Visualise the absolute frequencies (e.g., show 4 people affected in the control group and 2 people affected in the treatment group out of 100).
