## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

Choose a data visualization only when it strengthens the story’s message; otherwise use illustration, photography, or no visual.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

A visual should match the story’s communicative job. When the format doesn’t fit the topic or the point being made, readers spend attention decoding the graphic instead of understanding the message, and the visual can dilute or distort the takeaway.

- **The Principle:** Format–message fit (choose visuals by topic affordances and communicative purpose)
- **The Evidence:** SciAm editors reported that graphics can strengthen or weaken an article depending on alignment, and that some topics are better served by photos or bespoke illustration than by data visualization [@gregory_data_2024].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

This advice is designed for decisions made during story planning and visual commissioning.

- **User Goal:** Understanding the central takeaway quickly (not necessarily analyzing data)
- **Data Type:** Any—especially when data is thin, uncertain, or secondary to the narrative
- **Audience:** Broad/general readers who may not want to “work” to decode a chart

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

- **Scenario:** The claim hinges on quantification (comparisons, trends, distributions, uncertainty).
- **Reason:** Without a chart, the story’s evidence is harder to verify or is easier to misinterpret.
- **Scenario:** Readers need to explore values (interactive lookup, filtering, detailed breakdowns).
- **Reason:** Photography/illustration cannot support precise reading or exploration.

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

- **The Sacrifice:** Less “data-forward” framing and fewer opportunities for analytical detail.
- **The Risk:** A photo/illustration may feel less evidentiary, and “no image” may reduce scannability or shareability.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **The Wrong Fix:** Forcing a chart into a story because data exists (“chart for chart’s sake”).
- **Why it fails:** The graphic competes with the narrative and can weaken the message if it doesn’t match the topic’s natural visual evidence [@gregory_data_2024].
- **The Wrong Fix:** Using a generic stock chart/infographic style to signal “science” or “authority.”
- **Why it fails:** It implies precision or analytical support that the story may not actually provide.

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

- **Visual Sign:** The chart repeats what the text already says, feels tangential, or requires long explanation before it makes sense.
- **The Test:** Remove the visualization and ask: does the story lose evidence or clarity? If not, switch formats (photo/illustration) or omit the visual.

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

- **Quick Fix:** Replace the chart with a relevant photo, a simple explanatory illustration, or a single key-number callout if the main value is emphasis rather than analysis.
- **Best Fix:** Re-clarify the story’s communicative purpose, then redesign the visual approach to match it—commission a bespoke illustration for conceptual explanation, use photography for inherently visual subjects, or rebuild the chart only if it is essential evidence and directly supports the article’s core message [@gregory_data_2024].
