## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->
Do not rely on visual aids alone for audiences with **low graph literacy**. If the audience has poor graph skills, consider using alternative formats (like analogies) or providing training, as standard visual aids may fail.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->
While visual aids generally help, their effectiveness is moderated by the viewer's ability to interpret graphical information. Users with low graph literacy struggle to associate visual patterns (like blocks of icons) with meaningful data interpretations.

*   **The Principle:** **Graph Literacy Moderation**. The ability to make inferences from data displays varies significantly between individuals.
*   **The Evidence:** In one study, participants with low graph literacy improved their accuracy from 48% (no aid) to 64% (with icon arrays), whereas high graph literacy participants improved from 51% to 87%. The gap indicates visuals are less effective for the low-literacy group [@garcia-retamero_using_2012].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->
*   **User Goal:** Medical decision making or public health announcements.
*   **Audience:** Broad public samples, elderly populations, or groups where graph literacy has not been assessed.

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->
*   **Scenario:** The audience is expert (e.g., medical professionals).
*   **Reason:** Experts may have specific biases, but basic graph literacy is generally assumed, allowing for more complex or high-density visualizations.

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->
*   **The Sacrifice:** Standardization. You may need to create multiple versions of a risk communication tool (one visual, one analogy-based) to reach all audiences.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->
*   **The Wrong Fix:** Adding more complex interactivity or "richer" visuals to help understanding.
*   **Why it fails:** Complexity often exacerbates the problem for low graph literacy users who struggle with basic pattern-to-meaning associations [@garcia-retamero_using_2012].

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->
*   **Visual Sign:** Is the chart the only way to understand the risk?
*   **The Test:** Can a user explain the chart's meaning without reading the caption? If they have low graph literacy, they might see "black dots" but not understand they represent "people who died."

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->
*   **Quick Fix:** Always accompany the graphic with a clear, plain-language textual explanation (e.g., "This picture shows that 5 out of 100 people died").
*   **Best Fix:** For specifically low-literacy groups, use analogies or specially designed formats rather than standard statistical graphs [@garcia-retamero_using_2012].
