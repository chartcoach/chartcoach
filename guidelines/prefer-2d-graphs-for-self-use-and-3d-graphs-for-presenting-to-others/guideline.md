## Match 2D vs 3D choice to self-use vs presentation-to-others <!-- role: advice -->

Prefer 2D graph designs when the graph is for your own understanding, and consider 3D volume designs more when the graph is intended to be shown to others.

## Why audience context shifts dimensionality preferences <!-- role: reason -->

People may treat depth cues as a rhetorical or communicative device, favoring simpler displays for personal comprehension and more embellished displays when communicating externally.

**Mechanism:** Visual embellishments can increase perceived salience and “presentation value,” which may be judged as helpful for communication contexts.

**Evidence:** Across scenario-based selections, 2D (simple/area) graphs were chosen more for “self” scenarios, while 3D (volume/surface) graphs were chosen more for “others” scenarios; in a forced-choice test, preference differences for presenting to others were smaller (not significant) compared with strong 2D preference for self-use [@levyGratuitousGraphicsPutting1996].

**Notes:** The interaction between dimensionality and self-vs-others scenario was statistically reliable in the forced-choice study.

## When this applies <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Either understand data personally or communicate it to an audience.
- **Task:** Self: sensemaking; Others: explanation and communication.
- **Data:** 2D quantitative data with optional depth cues.
- **Chart Setting:** Slides, handouts, or reports intended for audiences.
- **Audience:** Self vs group; mixed graph literacy.
- **Success Criterion:** Self: clarity for analysis; Others: communicative effectiveness and engagement.

## When not to follow it <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The presentation’s success depends on immediate, accurate reading rather than salience or recall. **Why:** Viewers preferred 2D for immediate-use scenarios even when other contexts sometimes favored 3D [@levyGratuitousGraphicsPutting1996].

## Tradeoffs and risks <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Using 3D for audiences may trade away some straightforwardness relative to 2D. **Risk:** Audience members may focus on appearance over content. **Mitigation:** Keep the underlying encoding consistent and avoid unnecessary 3D variants when clarity is paramount.

## Common failure modes <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Assuming that what is preferred for presenting to others is also preferred for personal analysis. **Why it fails:** Preferences diverged between “self” and “others” scenarios, with 2D favored for self-use and 3D chosen more for others [@levyGratuitousGraphicsPutting1996].

## Quick tests <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** You choose a style based on what looks best in slides, but it feels less usable for your own analysis. **Quick Check:** If it’s primarily for your own understanding, default to 2D. **Stronger Test:** Show both versions to a few audience members and ask which they feel better communicates the message.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Use a 2D area or simple design for analysis and working drafts.
- If presenting to others, test whether a 3D volume design improves recall or engagement without harming comprehension.
- If communicative clarity is the concern, switch chart type (line vs bar) to match the task rather than relying on dimensional embellishment.
