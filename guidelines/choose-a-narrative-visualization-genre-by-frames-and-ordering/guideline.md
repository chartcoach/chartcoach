## Choose genre by frames and ordering <!-- role: advice -->

Choose the narrative visualization genre by deciding how many distinct visual scenes (frames) you need and how strictly you want to order them. Use the seven genres as building blocks and combine them when a single genre cannot carry both the structure and the content.

## Frames and ordering define narrative genre <!-- role: reason -->

Narrative visualization forms differ primarily by whether they multiplex scenes in space or time and by whether they impose a linear path, suggest a loose path, or allow random access. Matching the genre to the needed scene structure reduces disorientation and clarifies what the reader should do next.

**Mechanism:** When the number of scenes and their ordering match the intended narrative flow, the viewer can maintain orientation and follow the story progression without having to infer structure from dense graphical content.

**Evidence:** Narrative visualization examples cluster into seven genres (magazine style, annotated chart, partitioned poster, flow chart, comic strip, slide show, film/video/animation) that vary mainly by number of frames and ordering; genres can be combined as building blocks to form more complex narratives [@segelNarrativeVisualizationTelling2010].

**Notes:** The same underlying chart (e.g., an annotated graph) can appear inside different genres (e.g., slideshow) to change pacing and control.

## When frames and ordering drive the choice <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand an argument or explanation drawn from data.
- **Task:** Follow a sequence of observations or compare related sub-stories.
- **Data:** Often multivariate or too dense to present “all at once” without guidance.
- **Chart Setting:** Web or presentation medium where content can be split into spatial panels or temporal steps.
- **Audience:** General readers who benefit from explicit structure cues.
- **Success Criterion:** Viewers can tell what to look at next and how sections relate.

## When not to force a genre choice <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The primary goal is open-ended analysis with no prescribed narrative path. **Why:** Imposing a genre-defined ordering can constrain exploration and shift the experience away from reader-driven discovery.

## Tradeoffs of committing to a genre early <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Flexibility to let readers wander freely through all possible views. **Risk:** Over-structuring can make the experience feel like a fixed presentation rather than an exploratory visualization. **Mitigation:** Combine genres or reserve an exploration phase after the structured portion.

## Common genre-selection mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Treating “interactive chart” as a genre and skipping decisions about frames and ordering. **Why it fails:** Readers must infer narrative structure from interaction alone, which can weaken comprehension and orientation.
- **Mistake:** Using a partitioned poster when a linear sequence is required. **Why it fails:** Loose ordering can make causal or temporal arguments hard to follow.

## Quick checks for genre fit <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Viewers ask “Where do I start?” or “What should I look at next?” **Quick Check:** Count the distinct scenes you need and state whether the story must be linear, loosely guided, or free-form. **Stronger Test:** Ask a small set of readers to describe the intended path after a brief scan.

## What to do instead when the genre doesn’t fit <!-- role: fix -->

- Combine a partitioned poster layout with flow-chart cues (arrows, trails, ordered sections) to add a suggested path.
- Embed annotated charts inside a slideshow to control pacing while keeping the underlying encoding stable.
- Switch to a slideshow or video format when you need strict linear sequencing and controlled transitions.
- Add an exploration phase after the structured narrative if readers need to verify or probe beyond the presented story.
