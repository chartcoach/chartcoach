## Match the visual format to the story’s communicative job <!-- role: advice -->

Use a data visualization only if it clarifies the story’s point better than an illustration, photograph, or no image. Choose the visual format that most directly supports what readers should understand or feel.

## Visuals help when they align with topic affordances and message <!-- role: reason -->

A visual earns its space when it reduces ambiguity and cognitive effort for the intended takeaway; the wrong format adds noise, invites the wrong inferences, or competes with the narrative. Matching format to the topic’s “best evidence” (measured quantities vs. literal appearance vs. conceptual depiction) makes the message easier to interpret and harder to misread.

**Mechanism:** When the depiction matches the kind of claim being made, readers spend less effort translating the visual and more effort understanding the point; mismatched formats shift attention to irrelevant details or imply unsupported precision.

**Evidence:** Editorial workflows that plan visuals around topic affordances rely on photos or bespoke illustration for subjects where imagery carries the meaning, and reserve data graphics for cases where quantitative structure is the story. Misaligned data graphics can weaken comprehension and emphasis, while aligned graphics strengthen the message and fit the narrative intent [@gregory_data_2024].

**Notes:** “No image” can be a valid choice when a visual would be decorative, misleading, or redundant with the text.

## When the choice is between data graphics, imagery, or none <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand the central claim of an article or section quickly and accurately.
- **Task:** Grasp magnitude, change, comparison, composition, or evidence strength; or instead recognize a subject, place, organism, or artifact.
- **Data:** Optional; may be absent, sparse, highly uncertain, or not central to the claim.
- **Chart Setting:** Editorial or explanatory content where visuals compete for limited attention and space (print, web, mobile, social).
- **Audience:** General readers with varying data literacy; mixed accessibility needs.
- **Success Criterion:** The chosen visual increases comprehension or impact without introducing confusion, false precision, or distraction.

## When not to force a data visualization <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The story’s key value is literal appearance, texture, or direct documentary evidence (or the story has no meaningful quantitative structure). **Why:** A chart can imply measurement, precision, or comparisons that are not the point and can distract from what readers need to see.

## Tradeoffs of choosing the “right” format <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** A non-chart visual may reduce opportunities for precise quantitative comparison. **Risk:** Skipping a chart can under-support a claim that actually needs numeric evidence, reducing perceived credibility. **Mitigation:** Ensure the text still provides the necessary quantitative specifics (or add a small, tightly focused graphic) if the claim depends on numbers.

## Common ways this goes wrong <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Adding a chart because data exists, even when the story’s takeaway is not quantitative. **Why it fails:** The graphic competes with the narrative and can steer readers toward irrelevant comparisons.

## Quick ways to validate the choice <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Readers would ask “What am I supposed to learn from this chart/image?” **Quick Check:** Write the intended one-sentence takeaway of the visual; if the sentence is clearer than the visual, the visual format is likely wrong or unnecessary. **Stronger Test:** Show two versions (e.g., chart vs. photo/illustration vs. none) to a small set of target readers and ask what they learned in one sentence; keep the option that yields the most consistent takeaway.

## Practical alternatives when a chart isn’t the best fit <!-- role: fix -->

- Use a photograph when the key evidence is what something looks like or that it exists in a specific place or condition.
- Use a bespoke illustration when you need to explain a mechanism, depict an unseen process, or combine elements that cannot be photographed together.
- Use a minimal text-only callout (or a small annotated number) when a single value or fact supports the point without needing a full graphic.
- Remove the visual entirely when it does not add new information beyond the prose.
