## Use motion onset to steer attention to the next visual element <!-- role: advice -->

Use the onset of motion to pull viewers’ gaze to the intended object and use the motion path to guide where they look next.

## Motion captures attention and the motion path biases subsequent fixations <!-- role: reason -->

In dynamic visuals, attention is strongly drawn to newly moving objects, and viewers tend to track the moving object, which creates a predictable viewing order and often leaves the eye near the motion’s end point.

**Mechanism:** Motion onset triggers an attentional shift; tracking keeps attention “locked” to the moving object and carries fixation toward the trajectory end.

**Evidence:** In eye tracking of an 18-second instructional animation, all subjects rapidly aligned to a newly appearing moving object and then tracked its path, and fixations tended to end near the path endpoint [@faradayDesigningEffectiveMultimedia1997].

**Notes:** Motion can be used as a deliberate “pointer” when the next concept is spatially distinct.

## When motion is an appropriate attentional cue <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Follow an explanation with a correct viewing sequence.
- **Task:** Identify the next object/action in a stepwise process.
- **Data:** Procedural or causal sequences (objects and actions over time).
- **Chart Setting:** Animated or time-based multimedia with simultaneous text/labels and visuals.
- **Audience:** Especially helpful for low domain-knowledge viewers who need guidance on what to inspect.
- **Success Criterion:** Viewers attend to the intended object at the intended time and recall key propositions.

## When not to use motion as the primary cue <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The moving object is not central to the message at that moment. **Why:** Viewers may track it and miss other information that is more important.

## Tradeoffs of attention steering via motion <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** You may reduce viewers’ opportunity to read or inspect static elements while motion is ongoing. **Risk:** Motion can dominate attention and suppress processing of labels or other visuals. **Mitigation:** Plan pauses or sequencing so motion does not overlap with critical reading.

## Common mistakes with motion-based guidance <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Adding motion for decorative effect. **Why it fails:** Viewers may follow the motion and ignore the information that actually carries the explanation.
- **Mistake:** Making motion too fast to track. **Why it fails:** Attention may shift without sustained tracking, reducing comprehension of the action.

## Quick checks for motion-based attention control <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Viewers can describe the moving item but miss adjacent labels or state changes. **Quick Check:** Watch the animation yourself and note whether your eyes are pulled away from the concept being explained in the audio. **Stronger Test:** Run a brief recall test after a single viewing and check whether propositions tied to non-moving elements are missed.

## What to do instead when motion would distract <!-- role: fix -->

- Pause motion while key labels or captions must be read.
- Replace continuous motion with a brief state change (reveal/highlight) to cue attention without prolonged tracking.
- Re-time the animation so motion begins only after viewers have had time to process the current label/caption.
- Use a symbol cue (e.g., arrow) to direct gaze without requiring tracking.
