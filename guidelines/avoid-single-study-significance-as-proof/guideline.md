## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

Do not claim a relationship is true solely because one study reports p < 0.05.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

The paper argues that reliance on “formal statistical significance” from isolated studies yields a high nonreplication rate; PPV depends on power and pre-study odds, not just α. A single significant finding can easily be false, especially in low-R settings.

- **The Principle:** Significance is not confirmation
- **The Evidence:** [@ioannidisWhyMostPublished2005]

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Decide whether to accept a claimed association/intervention effect
- **Data Type:** Any study reporting a statistically significant relationship
- **Audience:** Researchers, clinicians, decision-makers reading literature

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

- **Scenario:** Very high pre-study odds and high power with minimal bias (e.g., well-conducted confirmatory trials)
- **Reason:** PPV can be high in these cases, though single studies still warrant caution. [@ioannidisWhyMostPublished2005]

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

- **The Sacrifice:** Slower acceptance of new claims
- **The Risk:** Potentially delays recognition of true effects. [@ioannidisWhyMostPublished2005]

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **The Wrong Fix:** “Statistically significant” equals “clinically/biologically real”
- **Why it fails:** PPV can remain below 50% in many realistic research settings. [@ioannidisWhyMostPublished2005]

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

- **Visual Sign:** Conclusions use the word “proved”/“demonstrated” based primarily on p < 0.05
- **The Test:** Ask: “If R is low or power is low, would PPV plausibly drop below 50%?”

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

- **Quick Fix:** Reword claims to be provisional and discuss power, R, and potential bias
- **Best Fix:** Seek/require replication or synthesize total evidence rather than highlighting one significant result. [@ioannidisWhyMostPublished2005]
