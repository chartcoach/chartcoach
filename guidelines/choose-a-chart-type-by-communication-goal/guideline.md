## Choose chart families by your communication goal <!-- role: advice -->

Pick the chart type by first naming what you want to show: change over time, shares, absolute numbers, correlations, flows, or geographic patterns. Then choose within that family based on how many categories and time points you have.

## Why goal-first chart choice improves clarity <!-- role: reason -->

A goal-first approach narrows the decision to chart types that match the viewer’s expected reading strategy, reducing confusion about what comparisons are intended.

**Mechanism:** When a chart’s form matches the intended task (trend, part-to-whole, ranking, association, movement, place), readers can apply familiar perceptual shortcuts and spend less effort guessing the chart’s purpose.

**Evidence:** Chart type selection becomes simpler and more accurate when it is constrained by the intended message (e.g., time change vs. shares vs. relationships), allowing many irrelevant chart types to be ignored [@muth_chart_types_guide_2025].

**Notes:** This guideline is about selecting a chart family; later choices (layout, labeling, color) still matter.

## Situations that call for goal-first chart selection <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Choose a chart type that a mainstream audience can interpret with minimal effort.
- **Task:** Communicate one primary comparison: trend, share, rank, association, flow, or spatial pattern.
- **Data:** Any dataset where the same values could be plausibly charted multiple ways.
- **Chart Setting:** Reporting, dashboards, articles, presentations, or posters with limited reader time.
- **Audience:** General readers or mixed-expertise audiences.
- **Success Criterion:** Readers correctly infer what to compare and what the chart is “about.”

## When not to follow this goal-first framing <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** You intentionally want to challenge readers with a less intuitive form for novelty or metaphor rather than fast comprehension. **Why:** The chart’s purpose may be to attract attention or convey a vibe, not maximize immediate readability [@muth_chart_types_guide_2025].

## Tradeoffs of goal-first selection <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** You may default to familiar “boring” charts more often. **Risk:** You might underuse niche forms that are well-suited for specific expert tasks. **Mitigation:** Treat the goal-first choice as a first filter, then consider whether a more specialized chart is worth the learning curve.

## Common ways this goes wrong <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Picking a chart because it looks exciting without stating the chart’s intended message. **Why it fails:** Readers may not know what to compare or may miss the main statement [@muth_chart_types_guide_2025].

## Quick tests for goal-first selection <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Viewers ask, “What am I supposed to look at?” or misread the chart as showing a different kind of comparison (e.g., shares vs. totals). **Quick Check:** Write a one-sentence main statement and name the task word in it (trend/share/rank/relationship/flow/place). **Stronger Test:** Show the chart to a colleague for five seconds and ask what comparison it invites.

## What to do instead if the goal is unclear <!-- role: fix -->

- Write a single-sentence chart message and underline the key comparison word (change, share, total, relationship, flow, location).
- Split the content into separate charts if you have multiple competing goals.
- Replace the chart with a simpler form that matches the primary goal more directly.
- Add a title that explicitly states the intended comparison when the chart could be interpreted multiple ways.
