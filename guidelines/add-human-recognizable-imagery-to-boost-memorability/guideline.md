## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

Include a human-recognizable pictorial element (photo, cartoon, or pictogram of an object/person) when you need the visualization itself to be memorable.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

Adding recognizable imagery provides a strong, easily encoded semantic cue that supports later recognition of the visualization as an image.

- **The Principle:** Semantic cueing via recognizable objects
- **The Evidence:** Visualizations containing pictorial elements had much higher memorability scores than those without (mean d′ 1.93 vs 1.14) in a large-scale memory-game experiment [@borkinWhatMakesVisualization2013a].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Remembering the visualization later (recognition/recall)
- **Data Type:** Any, especially when the chart’s form is otherwise visually generic
- **Audience:** Broad or mixed audiences (e.g., public-facing communication)

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

- **Scenario:** You need to measure or compare memorability effects of non-pictorial design choices.
- **Reason:** Pictorial elements have a strong main effect that can dominate other factors and confound comparisons [@borkinWhatMakesVisualization2013a].

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

- **The Sacrifice:** More non-data elements (“chart junk”) and less minimalist appearance.
- **The Risk:** Viewers may remember the pictorial element more than other parts of the visualization [@borkinWhatMakesVisualization2013a].

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **The Wrong Fix:** Adding any decoration and assuming it will help equally.
- **Why it fails:** The paper’s strongest effect is specifically from human-recognizable objects; other clutter can increase density but may not provide the same semantic cue [@borkinWhatMakesVisualization2013a].

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

- **Visual Sign:** The visualization contains only abstract marks (bars/lines/points) with no recognizable objects.
- **The Test:** Show it briefly (≈1s) and ask someone what they remember; if they can’t name a concrete object/figure, you likely lack a strong pictorial cue [@borkinWhatMakesVisualization2013a].

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

- **Quick Fix:** Add a small, recognizable pictogram or photo tied to the topic.
- **Best Fix:** Integrate the pictorial element so it clearly relates to the message rather than appearing as unrelated decoration [@borkinWhatMakesVisualization2013a].
