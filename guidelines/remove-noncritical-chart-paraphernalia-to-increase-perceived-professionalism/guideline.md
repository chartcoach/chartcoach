## Remove non-critical chart paraphernalia in explanatory charts <!-- role: advice -->

Remove non-critical chart elements such as heavy borders, background shading, dense gridlines, tick marks, and redundant mark styling when the goal is to communicate data in a professional-looking way.

## Why decluttering increases professionalism judgments <!-- role: reason -->

Decluttering reduces the salience of “template” decoration relative to the data, which shifts viewers’ impression from “spreadsheet output” toward “intentional communication,” increasing perceived professionalism even when it does not strongly change what viewers remember.

**Mechanism:** Lower visual noise and fewer competing elements makes the presentation feel more deliberate and less messy, which people translate into a professionalism judgment.

**Evidence:** Across six topics, decluttered designs were rated significantly more professional than cluttered designs, while decluttering alone did not significantly increase focus-relevant recall in redrawings or written conclusions [@ajaniDeclutterFocusEmpirically2022a].

**Notes:** The declutter manipulation bundled multiple removals at once (e.g., backgrounds, borders, gridlines, excess colors), so this guideline is about the bundle rather than any single element.

## When decluttering for professionalism applies <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Present data in a way that looks appropriate for a professional environment.
- **Task:** Quick audience impression (“does this look professional?”) alongside basic comprehension.
- **Data:** Typical business/reporting datasets across categorical and temporal topics.
- **Chart Setting:** Static charts in slides, handouts, or brief on-screen exposure.
- **Audience:** General audiences who are familiar with common spreadsheet chart defaults.
- **Success Criterion:** Higher professionalism ratings without needing to change the data content.

## When not to follow it <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The audience must read precise values that rely on reference structure you would remove (e.g., exact alignment to intermediate scale values). **Why:** Removing reference cues can make precision tasks harder even if the chart looks cleaner.

## Tradeoffs and risks <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Some reference scaffolding and immediate familiarity with default spreadsheet aesthetics. **Risk:** Over-minimal designs may be perceived as too sparse or “missing information” by some viewers. **Mitigation:** Keep only the structural elements that directly support the intended reading of the data.

## Common failure modes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Removing many elements but leaving other visually dominant clutter (e.g., heavy borders or dense background styling). **Why it fails:** The remaining clutter still drives the “messy/template” impression.
- **Mistake:** Treating decluttering as sufficient to make the key message memorable. **Why it fails:** Decluttering alone did not reliably increase focus-relevant recall compared to cluttered designs.

## Quick tests <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Viewers describe the chart as messy, childish, or dominated by formatting rather than data. **Quick Check:** Squint test—if decoration stands out as much as or more than the marks that encode the values, it is likely over-cluttered. **Stronger Test:** Ask a small sample to rate professionalism on a 1–5 scale while holding the data constant across versions.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Remove or soften borders, backgrounds, and dense gridlines while keeping the data marks and essential labels.
- Replace separate legends with direct labels when feasible to reduce lookup overhead.
- Preserve only the minimal reference structure needed for the intended reading (e.g., axes and labels).
- If precision reading is critical, keep lightweight reference cues but reduce their contrast and visual dominance.
