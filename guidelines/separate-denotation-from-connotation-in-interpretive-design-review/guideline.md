## Evaluate both literal readings and implied associations <!-- role: advice -->

For each major element (chart type, metaphor, labels, layout), separately describe what it explicitly shows and what it implicitly suggests through conventions and cultural associations.

## Why conventions and codes create framing beyond the data <!-- role: reason -->

Viewers interpret narrative visualizations using internalized viewing codes: expectations tied to chart forms, reading habits, perceptual salience, and cultural associations. These codes can generate connotations—secondary meanings—that steer interpretation even when the denoted (literal) data mapping is accurate.

**Mechanism:** Denotation communicates direct data-to-mark meaning, while connotation activates learned associations (for example, expectations attached to a chart type or metaphor), shifting how viewers characterize what they see.

**Evidence:** The paper distinguishes denotation from connotation and introduces “viewing codes” (visual, textual, cultural, perceptual, aesthetic, and individual) as extra-representational factors that shape interpretation of narrative visualizations [@hullmanVisualizationRhetoricFraming2011a]. It also describes how narrative examples can precisely depict data while simultaneously implying evaluative meanings through metaphors and culturally loaded associations [@hullmanVisualizationRhetoricFraming2011a].

**Notes:** Connoted meanings may vary across audiences with different experiences and norms.

## When denotation vs connotation review applies <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand what the visualization implies and how different audiences might read it.
- **Task:** Message review, editorial sign-off, or risk assessment for misinterpretation.
- **Data:** Any, especially politically or socially sensitive topics.
- **Chart Setting:** Narrative visualization with metaphor, stylistic cues, or playful framing.
- **Audience:** Broad audiences with diverse cultural and domain backgrounds.
- **Success Criterion:** The implied story is consistent with what the chart can support, across likely audience segments.

## When not to follow it <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The audience is tightly controlled and shares strong conventions (for example, an internal team trained on the house style). **Why:** Connotation risk is reduced when viewing codes are homogeneous.

## Tradeoffs and risks <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Time for interpretive review and audience consideration. **Risk:** Over-sanitizing stylistic choices can reduce engagement. **Mitigation:** Keep the review focused on high-impact connotations that would reverse or strongly intensify the takeaway.

## Common failure modes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Assuming that accurate encoding guarantees neutral interpretation. **Why it fails:** Connotation can add evaluative meaning that changes what viewers think the data “means.”
- **Mistake:** Using a metaphor or pun without considering subgroup differences in familiarity. **Why it fails:** Some viewers will miss the intended layer; others may import stronger associations than intended.

## Quick tests <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Viewers describe the visualization using evaluative language that is not present in the data description.\
**Quick Check:** Write two sentences: “This shows …” (denotation) and “This suggests …” (connotation).\
**Stronger Test:** Ask people from different backgrounds to paraphrase the message and compare divergence.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Add clarifying annotation that anchors the intended interpretation when metaphors are strong.
- Replace culturally loaded metaphors with more literal phrasing when misread risk is high.
- Provide alternative views or definitions when conventions could trigger an unintended frame.
- Align title and annotations with what the data can support to prevent connotation from overpowering denotation.
