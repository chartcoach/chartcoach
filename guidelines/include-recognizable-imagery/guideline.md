## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->
Embed human-recognizable objects—such as photographs, pictograms, cartoons, or logos—into your visualization design.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->
Visualizations that contain "pictograms" (a broad category including photos and cartoons) have significantly higher memorability scores than those that do not. According to [@borkin_what_2013], humans are evolutionarily adapted to segment and recognize natural objects and scenes. Providing these visual hooks allows the image to "stick" in memory more effectively than abstract geometric shapes alone.

*   **The Principle:** Intrinsic Memorability of Scenes
*   **The Evidence:** [@borkin_what_2013] (Section 7.2, Fig 1)

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->
This advice applies when the primary goal is long-term retention of the visual image itself, particularly in public-facing media.

*   **User Goal:** Engagement and recall (e.g., "Did I see this infographic before?").
*   **Data Type:** Qualitative or simple quantitative data suitable for infographics.
*   **Audience:** General public, news readers, or magazine audiences where capturing attention is competitive.

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->
Avoid this rule when strict data precision and immediate comprehension are prioritized over recall, or when the imagery distracts from the data trend.

*   **Scenario:** Scientific analysis or high-density data exploration.
*   **Reason:** While memorable, the paper notes that memorability does not equate to comprehension. Excessive imagery may act as a distractor in analytical contexts.

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->
*   **The Sacrifice:** "Data-ink ratio" (minimalism). You are adding elements that do not directly represent data values.
*   **The Risk:** You may create a "chart junk" heavy design that purists argue reduces data clarity, even if it increases image retention.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->
*   **The Wrong Fix:** Using generic, abstract shapes or clip art that doesn't relate to the content.
*   **Why it fails:** The study highlights "human recognizable objects" specifically; abstract patterns do not trigger the same recognition/memory pathways as faces, people, or physical objects.

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->
*   **Visual Sign:** Does the chart consist entirely of geometric lines, bars, and text?
*   **The Test:** Ask a viewer to describe the image after looking away. If they only describe the *type* of chart (e.g., "a bar chart") rather than a specific image (e.g., "the chart with the apple tree"), it lacks memorable imagery.

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->
*   **Quick Fix:** Add a relevant icon or logo to the title or annotation area.
*   **Best Fix:** Integrate the data encoding with the imagery (e.g., using an Isotype design where icons represent units of data).
