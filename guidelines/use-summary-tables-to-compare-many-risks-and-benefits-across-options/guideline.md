## Provide a concise summary table when there are many attributes <!-- role: advice -->

When a decision involves numerous risks and benefits, include a summary table that lists the key outcomes and their statistics for each option in one place.

## Tables reduce cognitive load and support side-by-side comparison <!-- role: reason -->

Spreading attributes across pages or a long conversation increases forgetting and makes comparison harder; a compact table helps people hold the relevant information together.

**Mechanism:** A single aligned view reduces memory demands and enables direct option-by-option comparison.

**Evidence:** Adding a summary table increased interest in seeking more information and discussing chemoprevention with a clinician compared with materials without a summary table [@fagerlinHelpingPatientsDecide2011].

**Notes:** Effects on knowledge may be limited, but tables can still improve engagement and comparison behavior.

## When a summary table is appropriate <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Compare options across multiple outcomes (benefits and harms).
- **Task:** Attribute-by-attribute comparison.
- **Data:** Several risk/benefit statistics per option.
- **Chart Setting:** Multi-page brochures, web decision aids, or end-of-visit takeaways.
- **Audience:** Patients who may struggle to remember earlier details.
- **Success Criterion:** Users can correctly identify which option is better on the outcomes they care about.

## When not to follow it <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The decision truly hinges on only one or two outcomes. **Why:** A table can add unnecessary complexity and distract from the key tradeoff.

## Tradeoffs and risks <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Requires space and careful layout. **Risk:** Overly dense tables can overwhelm and become unreadable. **Mitigation:** Limit the table to decision-critical outcomes and keep units consistent.

## Common failure modes <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Including every possible outcome in the table for completeness. **Why it fails:** Excess detail can distract from the information needed for the decision.

## Quick tests <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Users cannot locate the number they need or confuse units across rows. **Quick Check:** Time how long it takes to answer a simple comparison question using the table. **Stronger Test:** Ask users to explain which option they prefer and cite the table cell(s) they used.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Limit rows to the most decision-relevant risks and benefits for the intended audience.
- Align options as columns and outcomes as rows so comparisons are direct.
- Use the same denominator and time horizon in all cells.
- Place the table at the end of the material as a recap for the full set of attributes.
