## Avoid using number needed to treat as the sole format for risk reduction when comprehension is critical <!-- role: advice -->

Do not rely on number needed to treat as the only summary of benefit when the audience must accurately understand the magnitude of effect; accompany it with an absolute risk reduction or event rates.

## Why number needed to treat can reduce understanding and perceived magnitude <!-- role: reason -->

Number needed to treat requires inversion and interpretation of a “how many to help one” framing, which can be harder to translate into personal or population impact than direct risk differences or relative reductions. This can lower understanding and can make benefits feel smaller compared with other equivalent formats.

**Mechanism:** The NNT representation pushes readers to convert a reciprocal into an effect magnitude, increasing mental computation and ambiguity about what happens to the many who do not “count” as the one.

**Evidence:** Relative risk reduction was better understood than number needed to treat and perceived as larger, and absolute risk reduction was also better understood than number needed to treat and perceived as larger [@aklUsingAlternativeStatistical2011]. Persuasiveness differed for relative risk reduction versus number needed to treat, but not meaningfully for absolute risk reduction versus number needed to treat [@aklUsingAlternativeStatistical2011].

**Notes:** Understanding outcomes for these comparisons were sometimes based on single studies, so precision can be limited even when effects are statistically clear.

## When this applies <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand intervention benefit magnitude well enough to choose among options.
- **Task:** Interpret a risk reduction statement without doing additional calculations.
- **Data:** Treatment/control risks where NNT is one possible summary.
- **Chart Setting:** Patient decision aids, medication brochures, clinician-facing summaries where fast comprehension matters.
- **Audience:** Mixed numeracy audiences, including consumers and clinicians.
- **Success Criterion:** Higher objective understanding and calibrated perception of effect size.

## When NNT alone may be sufficient <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The audience is trained, expects NNT, and the decision context already provides absolute event rates elsewhere. **Why:** The missing interpretive anchor is supplied, reducing the cognitive burden of NNT alone.

## Tradeoffs of deprioritizing NNT <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** You may lose a compact single-number summary some clinical audiences value. **Risk:** Adding absolute risks can increase message length and reduce recall of the headline number. **Mitigation:** Keep NNT but add a one-line absolute translation in the same time horizon.

## Common mistakes with NNT communication <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Presenting NNT without stating the time period (“NNT = 50” with no duration). **Why it fails:** NNT is uninterpretable without a time horizon.
- **Mistake:** Using NNT without baseline risk context and expecting readers to infer whether the effect is small or large. **Why it fails:** Perceived magnitude shifts downward and comprehension drops relative to absolute or relative formats.

## Quick checks for NNT usability <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Readers cannot explain what happens out of a fixed group (for example, out of 1000 treated) after seeing only NNT. **Quick Check:** Ensure any NNT is paired with a time horizon and an absolute risk statement. **Stronger Test:** Ask users to translate NNT into an absolute “x fewer events out of y people” estimate.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Present event rates for control and intervention groups (“from X to Y”) as the primary statement.
- Add the absolute risk reduction as a difference (“Y% lower” or “Z fewer per 1000”) next to any NNT.
- Use a consistent-denominator frequency translation to show how many benefit in a defined group over the stated time.
