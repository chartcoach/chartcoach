## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->
Present data in tables rather than charts when attempting to persuade an audience that holds strong, negative pre-existing beliefs about the topic.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->
While visualization is generally powerful, it can backfire with polarized audiences. Skeptics often view charts as "manipulated" or "cherry-picked" to fit a narrative.
*   **The Principle:** **Objectivity and Scrutiny.** Tables appear more objective to skeptics because they present "raw numbers." This format allows users to scrutinize the data logic themselves (Elaboration Likelihood Model's central route) rather than feeling forced into a conclusion by a visual trend.
*   **The Evidence:** [@pandey_persuasive_2014] found a "reverse trend" for Negatively Polarized participants: tables outperformed charts. For example, in one study (Video Games), 92.30% of polarized participants were persuaded by tables versus only 58.33% by charts. Qualitative feedback indicated that skeptics felt "data is easily manipulated" in charts but felt tables let them "see the raw numbers."

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->
*   **User Goal:** Changing the mind of someone who strongly opposes the argument.
*   **Audience:** "Negatively Polarized" users—those whose initial attitude is strongly against the message being presented (e.g., convincing a tax-hike opponent that tax hikes create jobs).
*   **Data Type:** Controversial statistical evidence that challenges core beliefs.

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->
*   **Scenario:** The audience is neutral, undecided, or already agrees with you.
*   **Reason:** For these groups, tables are significantly less persuasive than charts. The cognitive load of a table may reduce the impact of the message if the user is not motivated to scrutinize the data [@pandey_persuasive_2014].

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->
*   **The Sacrifice:** You lose the "vividness" and immediate pattern recognition of charts.
*   **The Risk:** The message may be less memorable or harder to understand for those who are not motivated to read the numbers.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->
*   **The Wrong Fix:** "Dumbing down" the data into a simple chart to make it easier for a hostile audience to understand.
*   **Why it fails:** Hostile audiences interpret the simplification as manipulation. Feedback in [@pandey_persuasive_2014] showed that skeptics often rejected charts because they felt they didn't "represent the whole picture."

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->
*   **Visual Sign:** Are you using a highly stylized chart to present controversial data to a hostile crowd?
*   **The Test:** Ask yourself: "If someone wanted to claim this data is fake, does the design give them ammunition?" (e.g., truncated axes, complex encodings). If yes, switch to a table.

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->
*   **Quick Fix:** Add a detailed data table below the chart.
*   **Best Fix:** For the skeptical segment of your audience, replace the visualization with a clean, well-structured table of the underlying statistics to leverage the perception of objectivity.
