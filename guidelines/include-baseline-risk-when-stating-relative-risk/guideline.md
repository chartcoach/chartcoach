## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

Whenever you state a relative risk (e.g., “2× higher,” “10% reduction”), also state the baseline risk and the resulting absolute risk.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

Relative risks without base rates can inflate perceived risk magnitude; adding the baseline anchors interpretation and reduces overestimation.

- **The Principle:** Base-rate anchoring for interpreting comparative risk
- **The Evidence:** [@lipkusNumericVerbalVisual2007]

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand effect size of an exposure or treatment
- **Data Type:** Comparative risk statements (relative risk, percent reduction)
- **Audience:** Patients and the general public

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

- **Scenario:** You truly cannot estimate baseline risk for the target population.
- **Reason:** Presenting a speculative baseline can mislead more than it helps. [@lipkusNumericVerbalVisual2007]

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

- **The Sacrifice:** Extra space and explanation.
- **The Risk:** If the baseline is for a different population than the reader, it can mis-calibrate perceived personal risk.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **The Wrong Fix:** Reporting only “10× higher risk” or “14% reduction.”
- **Why it fails:** People cannot infer absolute magnitude and may overestimate the threat or benefit. [@lipkusNumericVerbalVisual2007]

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

- **Visual Sign:** A comparative claim appears with no “from X to Y” numbers.
- **The Test:** Ask “Could someone compute the absolute risk change from what’s shown?” If not, it fails.

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

- **Quick Fix:** Add “baseline risk = …” and “new risk = …” immediately after the relative statement.
- **Best Fix:** Show baseline vs comparator side-by-side using the same denominator (e.g., “1 out of 100” vs “10 out of 100”), then optionally restate the relative factor. [@lipkusNumericVerbalVisual2007]
