## Consider graph literacy as a constraint when using icon arrays <!-- role: advice -->

Before adopting icon arrays as the primary aid for communicating treatment risk reduction, evaluate whether the intended audience can correctly interpret simple graphs. If graph literacy is expected to be low, plan supplementary supports rather than assuming the icon array will solve misunderstanding.

## Why graph literacy moderates the effectiveness of icon arrays <!-- role: reason -->

Icon arrays reduce denominator neglect by making ratios visible, but that benefit depends on the viewer’s ability to decode the graphical representation. When viewers cannot reliably map icons to quantities and groups, the visual channel provides less usable information.

**Mechanism:** Higher graph literacy enables more accurate extraction of the relevant proportional relations from icon arrays, increasing the corrective effect on denominator neglect.

**Evidence:** When numeric information was supplemented with icon arrays, accuracy in estimating treatment risk reduction increased more for participants with higher graph literacy than for those with lower graph literacy, indicating a moderation effect [@garcia-retameroUsingVisualAids2012].

**Notes:** This moderation was observed even when icon arrays generally improved performance in unequal-denominator conditions.

## When graph literacy is likely to vary <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand risk reduction from a visual + numeric presentation.
- **Task:** Read a visual display and infer relative risk reduction across groups.
- **Data:** Frequencies/ratios shown both numerically and as icon arrays.
- **Chart Setting:** Patient education, online explainers, decision aids used by broad populations.
- **Audience:** Heterogeneous audiences where graph-reading skill is unknown or uneven.
- **Success Criterion:** The visual aid improves accuracy across the audience, not only among graph-literate subgroups.

## When to deprioritize graph literacy concerns <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The audience is known to have high graph literacy (e.g., trained professionals in a setting where graph interpretation is routine). **Why:** The moderation risk is smaller when graph comprehension is reliably strong.

## Tradeoffs of designing around graph literacy <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Additional research or testing effort and potentially more explanatory content. **Risk:** Over-simplifying visuals to accommodate low graph literacy can reduce information density. **Mitigation:** Validate comprehension with representative users before deployment.

## Common mistakes when accounting for graph literacy <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Assuming that icon arrays are universally self-explanatory and need no comprehension check. **Why it fails:** Lower graph literacy reduces the gain from icon arrays, so the intended debiasing effect may not materialize.

## Quick checks for graph-literacy dependence <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Users misinterpret what each icon represents or cannot state totals for each group from the display. **Quick Check:** Ask a few users to explain what one icon means and what the total number of icons represents. **Stronger Test:** Run a brief comprehension test and confirm that accuracy improves with the visual across low- and high-skill users.

## What to do instead when graph literacy is low <!-- role: fix -->

- Add minimal explanatory text that teaches how to read the icon array in the specific message.
- Simplify the visual mapping so totals and affected subsets are easier to identify.
- Provide alternative or additional representations that reduce dependence on graph decoding.
- Pilot-test multiple formats with the target audience and select the one that improves proportional judgments most reliably.
