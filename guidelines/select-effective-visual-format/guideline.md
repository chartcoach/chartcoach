## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

Determine whether a data visualization is the most effective medium for the story before beginning design. actively consider if photography, bespoke illustration, or text alone would communicate the narrative better.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

Visuals must align with the communicative purpose of the story to add value; otherwise, they risk becoming distractions. Not every topic benefits from quantitative abstraction.

*   **The Principle:** Topic Affordance. Different subjects naturally "afford" or lend themselves to different types of representation. For example, emotive subjects often require photography, while abstract concepts may require diagrams.
*   **The Evidence:** Research into editorial practices shows that efficient teams plan visual approaches based on these affordances. Editors note that data graphics can weaken a story if they do not align with the core message, whereas specific topics (like wildlife or space exploration) are often better served by photography or illustration [@gregory_data_2024].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

Apply this decision-making process during the editorial planning or storyboarding phase, before data preparation begins.

*   **User Goal:** Telling a cohesive story or explaining a specific phenomenon.
*   **Data Type:** High-level concepts, emotive subjects, or sparse datasets.
*   **Audience:** General audiences who need narrative cohesion rather than raw data access.

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

*   **Scenario:** Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA).
*   **Reason:** When the goal is to discover the story within the data, you must visualize everything regardless of narrative fit. You cannot judge the "best format" until you know what the data says.
*   **Scenario:** Dashboards and Monitoring.
*   **Reason:** In operational contexts, the format is often pre-determined by the need for consistent metric tracking, overriding narrative format choices.

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

*   **The Sacrifice:** Visual Consistency. Mixing photos, illustrations, and charts requires a stronger art direction strategy than using a single template library.
*   **The Risk:** "Wall of Text." If you decide a chart isn't necessary, you risk losing readers who scan articles for visual breaks. You must replace the rejected chart with another engaging visual element.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

*   **The Wrong Fix:** Visualizing for the sake of having a visual.
*   **Why it fails:** This leads to charts that merely repeat the text (e.g., a big number chart that just duplicates the headline) or abstract charts that strip the emotion from a human-centric story.
*   **The Wrong Fix:** Using generic stock photography.
*   **Why it fails:** While a chart might not be perfect, a generic photo is often worse. If a chart is rejected, the alternative visual must still be specific to the story.

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

*   **Visual Sign:** A chart containing only one or two data points that are already written in the paragraph immediately preceding it.
*   **The Test:** Read the story without the chart. Is any information lost? If no, the chart is likely decorative rather than informative. Conversely, ask if a photograph of the subject would provoke a stronger emotional response than the bar chart you are designing.

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

*   **Quick Fix:** If the chart adds no value, remove it and use pull-quotes or bold typography to break up the text.
*   **Best Fix:** Commission or source a photograph, diagram, or illustration that captures the qualitative essence of the topic (e.g., using a telescope image for a space story instead of a star-count chart).
