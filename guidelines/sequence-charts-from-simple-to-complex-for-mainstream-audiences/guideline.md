## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

When telling a multi-chart story, begin with simple charts and statements, then escalate to more complex chart types only after readers have context.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

Explain the principle at work here. Connect the rule to human perception or clear communication.

- **The Principle:** Progressive disclosure reduces overwhelm by building understanding step-by-step before introducing harder encodings.
- **The Evidence:** The post states scatter plots and 2D histograms are complicated for many readers and suggests introducing a topic with simple visualizations first, then increasing complexity; it gives a three-step example sequence (line → arrow plot → scatter) [@muth_chart_types_guide_2025].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

This advice is designed for specific moments.

- **User Goal:** Explaining a complex topic to a mainstream audience without losing them.
- **Data Type:** Topics that can be narrated across multiple related views (e.g., trend, then ranking of changes, then relationship analysis).
- **Audience:** Mainstream/general readers, including those who may stop reading before the end [@muth_chart_types_guide_2025].

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

List the specific scenarios where you should ignore this rule.

- **Scenario:** Your audience already expects technical/statistical graphics.
- **Reason:** The post’s warning is specifically about mainstream readers finding scatter/2D histograms overwhelming; expert contexts may tolerate jumping in [@muth_chart_types_guide_2025].

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

Be honest about the downsides.

- **The Sacrifice:** Requires multiple charts (more production and editorial space) instead of one.
- **The Risk:** Readers may stop before reaching the most nuanced chart; you must ensure early charts carry the core takeaway [@muth_chart_types_guide_2025].

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

Describe the common anti-patterns or "lazy fixes" people use that don't actually solve the problem.

- **The Wrong Fix:** Leading with the most complex chart because it contains “everything.”
- **Why it fails:** Many readers will disengage; the post advises starting simpler to avoid overwhelm [@muth_chart_types_guide_2025].

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

- **Visual Sign:** The first chart requires significant explanation before it becomes interpretable.
- **The Test:** Show the first chart to a non-expert; if they can’t restate the main message quickly, start with a simpler view [@muth_chart_types_guide_2025].

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

- **Quick Fix:** Add an introductory chart that makes a single clear statement (e.g., a simple line).
- **Best Fix:** Rebuild the narrative as a sequence from simple to complex (e.g., trend → comparison of changes → relationship), mirroring the post’s suggested progression [@muth_chart_types_guide_2025].
