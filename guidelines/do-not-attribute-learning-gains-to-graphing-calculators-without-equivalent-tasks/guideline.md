## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

When studying the effect of a graphing calculator, keep the mathematical tasks and learning opportunities equivalent across conditions; vary only access to the tool.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

Changing both the tool and the curriculum confounds causal claims: any differences can come from different mathematics being taught/attempted rather than the device itself. Mesa’s quasi-experimental setup (same problems, controlled access) is explicitly motivated by this validity problem in prior comparative studies [@mesaSolvingProblemsFunctions2008].

- **The Principle:** Control confounds by isolating the independent variable (tool access).
- **The Evidence:** [@mesaSolvingProblemsFunctions2008]

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Attribute differences in strategy, success, or time-on-task to technology rather than task differences.
- **Data Type:** Classroom/experimental comparisons of outcomes (achievement, strategy, discourse) with vs. without calculators.
- **Audience:** Education researchers, curriculum evaluators, instructional designers running pilots.

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

- **Scenario:** You are evaluating a full instructional package where technology and task redesign are inseparable (i.e., the intervention is “tech + curriculum” by definition).
- **Reason:** The causal question is no longer “tool effect” but “program effect,” so holding tasks constant is not the aim.

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

- **The Sacrifice:** Less ecological validity; the “no-tool” condition may feel artificial.
- **The Risk:** Participants may behave differently under controlled access than in real classrooms, complicating generalization.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **The Wrong Fix:** Compare a technology-rich unit to “normal teaching” and attribute gains to the calculator.
- **Why it fails:** Differences may reflect different content emphasis, representations, or activity structure—not the tool [@mesaSolvingProblemsFunctions2008].

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

- **Visual Sign:** The control condition uses different tasks, different representations, or different mathematical goals than the experimental condition.
- **The Test:** Make a side-by-side mapping of (a) problem prompts, (b) targeted concepts, and (c) permitted representations; if they differ materially, you cannot attribute effects to the calculator [@mesaSolvingProblemsFunctions2008].

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

- **Quick Fix:** Re-run with the same problems in both conditions and only toggle calculator access (as Mesa did) [@mesaSolvingProblemsFunctions2008].
- **Best Fix:** Use a crossover or counterbalanced design so each group experiences both conditions on comparable tasks, reducing group differences [@mesaSolvingProblemsFunctions2008].
