## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

Avoid using the stereotypical pink-for-women and blue-for-men pairing to encode gender categories; choose a different, non-stereotyped color pair instead. [@muth_gendercolor_2018]

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

Explain the principle at work here. Connect the rule to human perception or clear communication.

- **The Principle:** Color carries cultural meaning, not just visual contrast; stereotyped palettes can communicate unintended claims.
- **The Evidence:** The post argues that pink/blue “come with the whole gender stereotype baggage” and that using them “endorse[s] gender stereotypes,” which can be counter to the intent of gender-(pay)-gap reporting. [@muth_gendercolor_2018]

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

This advice is designed for specific moments.

- **User Goal:** Understand differences between genders (often disparities like gaps) without editorial framing that reinforces stereotypes.
- **Data Type:** Categorical groups labeled as men/women (or similar sex/gender categories) in any chart type.
- **Audience:** Broad/public audiences, especially in news, reporting, and communications where cultural associations are quickly activated. [@muth_gendercolor_2018]

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

List the specific scenarios where you should ignore this rule.

- **Scenario:** You deliberately need instant decoding from widely recognized conventions and are willing to accept the stereotype baggage to remove labeling overhead.
- **Reason:** The post notes an argument for pink/blue: the strong association can make charts faster to decipher and can sometimes eliminate the need for a separate legend. [@muth_gendercolor_2018]

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

Be honest about the downsides.

- **The Sacrifice:** You may lose the “immediate recognition” that some readers get from pink/blue conventions.
- **The Risk:** Some readers may need a legend/labels to understand the mapping, and the chart can take longer to read at first glance. [@muth_gendercolor_2018]

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

Describe the common anti-patterns or "lazy fixes" people use that don't actually solve the problem.

- **The Wrong Fix:** Keeping pink and blue but “just flipping them” (pink=men, blue=women) to appear subversive.
- **Why it fails:** The post warns readers may not check the legend and may misinterpret the chart through the stereotype lens (e.g., inferring women are doing better if pink appears larger/stronger), especially if the newsroom has used the stereotypical mapping before. [@muth_gendercolor_2018]

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

- **Visual Sign:** Your gender categories are encoded with a pink-vs-blue palette (or close variants) and rely on the reader’s assumptions rather than clear labeling.
- **The Test:** Show the chart briefly to someone and ask “which color is women/men?”—if they answer based on stereotype (without reading) or get it wrong, the mapping is doing unintended work. [@muth_gendercolor_2018]

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

- **Quick Fix:** Swap to a clearly non-stereotyped pair (e.g., any distinct hues that are not pink/blue-coded) and add/keep a legend if needed. [@muth_gendercolor_2018]
- **Best Fix:** Choose a pair with an explicit rationale that supports the message (e.g., The Telegraph’s purple/green inspired by suffrage colors) so the palette communicates intent rather than default stereotypes. [@muth_gendercolor_2018]
