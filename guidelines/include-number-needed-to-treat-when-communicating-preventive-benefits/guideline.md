## Include number needed to treat for preventive outcomes <!-- role: advice -->

When presenting preventive treatment effects, include the number needed to treat (NNT) for a specified time horizon alongside risk reductions.

## NNT makes the practical magnitude of benefit harder to misread <!-- role: reason -->

NNT reframes effect size into a concrete “how many treated to prevent one event” quantity, which incorporates baseline risk and discourages overinterpretation that can occur with percentage-based summaries.

**Mechanism:** Converting benefit into treated-person counts shifts attention from proportional change to practical yield, reducing perceived effect size when absolute event rates are low.

**Evidence:** For the same myocardial infarction endpoint, physicians rated lipid-lowering drugs as less effective and were less inclined to start treatment when results were presented as NNT compared with relative risk reduction, with NNT producing the largest shift toward conservative ratings [@bucherInfluenceMethodReporting1994].

**Notes:** In the study design, NNT allowed within-physician comparison because the same endpoint was presented both as risk reduction and as NNT.

## Contexts where effect size must translate into prescribing decisions <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Decide whether to initiate drug therapy for primary prevention.
- **Task:** Convert trial outcomes into an actionable treatment threshold.
- **Data:** Preventive endpoints with modest absolute event reductions over a fixed follow-up period.
- **Chart Setting:** Trial abstracts, guideline summaries, drug information sheets, or decision aids using text/tables.
- **Audience:** Clinicians interpreting evidence for prescribing.
- **Success Criterion:** Decisions reflect the practical magnitude of benefit and are less sensitive to framing.

## Exceptions: When not to follow it <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The time horizon or event definition cannot be stated clearly. **Why:** NNT without a clearly specified timeframe and endpoint can be misleading and hard to compare.

## Costs: Tradeoffs and risks <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Extra explanation and space to define timeframe and endpoint for NNT. **Risk:** Readers may treat NNT as universally comparable across different follow-up durations if the timeframe is not prominent. **Mitigation:** Always state NNT with the exact endpoint and follow-up period in the same phrase.

## Mistakes: Common failure modes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Giving NNT without the follow-up duration. **Why it fails:** The quantity becomes ambiguous and invites invalid comparisons across studies or endpoints.
- **Mistake:** Using RRR alone even when NNT is available. **Why it fails:** In this study, RRR-only framing increased perceived effectiveness and inclination to treat relative to NNT framing for the same outcome [@bucherInfluenceMethodReporting1994].

## Check: Quick tests <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Readers can repeat the percent reduction but cannot answer “how many need treatment to prevent one event, and over what time.”\
**Quick Check:** Verify every NNT includes both the endpoint and a time horizon in the same sentence or table cell.\
**Stronger Test:** Ask a small set of intended readers to explain the expected benefit per 1000 treated over the stated period; confusion indicates missing or unclear NNT context.

## Fix: What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Add an NNT line for key preventive endpoints, explicitly tied to the endpoint and follow-up period.
- Present NNT adjacent to ARR/RRR for the same endpoint so readers can triangulate magnitude.
- If NNT cannot be computed or communicated clearly, present ARR with a population denominator and timeframe instead.
- Use consistent denominators and time horizons across endpoints to support comparison.
