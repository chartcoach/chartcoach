## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->
When communicating medical risk reductions, do not rely on numbers alone. Accompany numerical data—even transparent formats like Absolute Risk Reduction (ARR)—with icon arrays.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->
Many individuals, particularly older adults and those with low numeracy skills, struggle to process elementary probability expressions.
*   **The Principle:** Visual assistance. Icon arrays provide a concrete representation of the "part-to-whole" relationship that numbers can obscure.
*   **The Evidence:** In experiments comparing students and older adults, adding icon arrays to numerical descriptions significantly increased the accuracy of risk understanding. This accuracy boost occurred even when the numerical data was already presented in a "transparent" format (Absolute Risk Reduction) rather than a misleading one (Relative Risk Reduction) [@galesic_using_2009].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->
This advice is critical for communicating quantitative health information.
*   **User Goal:** Helping patients understand the true likelihood of side effects or treatment benefits.
*   **Audience:** Broad audiences, specifically older adults (60+) or those with lower educational backgrounds/numeracy skills.
*   **Data Type:** Binary outcomes (e.g., stroke vs. no stroke) within a specific population.

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->
*   **Scenario:** Highly time-constrained, expert-only environments.
*   **Reason:** While not explicitly tested against experts in this paper, the primary benefit is framed around overcoming low numeracy. If the audience is comprised of statisticians who do not need visual aids to parse probabilities, the space may be better used elsewhere.

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->
*   **The Sacrifice:** Screen or page real estate. Icon arrays (especially those with 1,000 units) take up significantly more space than a text string like "8%."
*   **The Risk:** If the icons are too small or "messy" (e.g., detailed faces scaled down), they may become illegible. The study used simple circles to avoid this.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->
*   **The Wrong Fix:** Using Relative Risk Reduction (RRR) numbers alone (e.g., "38% lower risk").
*   **Why it fails:** This format is notoriously difficult for laypeople to interpret correctly compared to Absolute Risk Reduction (ARR). However, even ARR is improved by adding icons [@galesic_using_2009].

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->
*   **Visual Sign:** Is the risk communicated solely through text (e.g., "1 in 100") or percentages?
*   **The Test:** Ask a layperson to explain the "number of people affected out of 1,000." If they struggle, you need visual support.

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->
*   **Quick Fix:** Add a static grid of dots representing the population (e.g., 100 or 1,000 units) with the affected portion highlighted.
*   **Best Fix:** Integrate an icon array showing both the baseline risk and the risk with treatment side-by-side to visualize the reduction.
