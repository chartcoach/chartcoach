## Build shared decision-making visuals to support a collaborative role across literacy levels <!-- role: advice -->

Design shared decision-making materials so that both low and high health literacy users can participate collaboratively, without assuming that limited health literacy implies a preference for passivity.

## Why this supports participation without stereotyping <!-- role: reason -->

If preferences for decision roles do not differ by health literacy, then segmenting shared decision tools by literacy level risks misaligning the interaction style with users’ actual preferences and may reduce engagement.

**Mechanism:** Collaboration-oriented materials (that make the decision structure visible and discussable) can serve users who want shared control regardless of literacy level.

**Evidence:** Veterans with adequate versus inadequate health literacy did not differ in their usual role in decision making or in the role they preferred [@rodriguezHealthLiteracyNumeracy2013]. Overall, most participants preferred a collaborative role even though many reported a passive usual role [@rodriguezHealthLiteracyNumeracy2013].

**Notes:** This guideline concerns role preference and interaction framing, not the reading level of the content.

## When this applies in decision support <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Participate with a clinician in choosing among care options.
- **Task:** Clarify values, compare options, and agree on a plan.
- **Data:** Option attributes, benefits/harms, probabilities, tradeoffs.
- **Chart Setting:** Shared decision-making aids used during or around visits.
- **Audience:** Veterans with mixed health literacy levels.
- **Success Criterion:** Users can engage in collaborative discussion regardless of health literacy level.

## When not to follow it <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The setting is explicitly clinician-directed decision making with no patient input permitted (rare). **Why:** A collaborative framing would be incompatible with the actual workflow.

## Tradeoffs and risks <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Some efficiency if materials prompt more discussion. **Risk:** Collaboration framing without clear takeaways can feel vague. **Mitigation:** Keep the decision points explicit so collaboration is structured.

## Common failure modes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Label low-literacy users as “passive” and give them non-participatory materials. **Why it fails:** Preferences for decision-making role did not differ by health literacy group in this sample [@rodriguezHealthLiteracyNumeracy2013].
- **Mistake:** Treat the “usual role” as the “preferred role.” **Why it fails:** Many veterans reported a passive usual role but preferred collaboration [@rodriguezHealthLiteracyNumeracy2013].

## Quick tests <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Users say they want more say but the material guides them to accept a decision without discussion. **Quick Check:** Ask users whether the artifact helps them ask questions and compare options with the clinician. **Stronger Test:** Observe a brief simulated encounter and code whether the artifact prompts collaborative exchanges.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Make the decision points explicit so patients can see where their input matters.
- Include prompts that help patients articulate preferences or questions in the visit.
- Present options in a way that supports side-by-side discussion rather than a single “correct” choice.
- Validate that the same artifact supports both users with lower and higher health literacy in brief observations.
