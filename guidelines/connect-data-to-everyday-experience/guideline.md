## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

Connect the data to familiar, everyday experiences (places, routines, costs, timeframes, local events) so viewers can map the numbers onto their own lives.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

Personal relevance reduces abstraction and invites viewers to interpret the chart through concrete lived contexts, which can increase attention, empathy, motivation, and follow-up information seeking.

- **The Principle:** Emotional resonance through personal anchoring
- **The Evidence:** Viewers of crisis maps often assessed local risk and reflected on responsibility when the map connected to their own context [@koesten_encountering_2025]. Lay audiences asked for climate visuals to show what the data means for their lives; detached charts felt uninteresting, while relatable framing felt motivating [@schuster_being_2024]. Familiar local references and recognizable timeframes can trigger emotional reactions and further inquiry [@koesten_what_2023].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

Use this when the goal is to motivate action, increase concern, or help people interpret implications rather than just read values.

- **User Goal:** Understand “what this means for me/us” and decide whether to care, act, or learn more
- **Data Type:** Risk, impact, exposure, or trend data where consequences are situational (e.g., climate, health, safety, community outcomes)
- **Audience:** General public or mixed audiences, especially non-experts encountering the topic casually

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

- **Scenario:** High-stakes decisions requiring strict neutrality (e.g., regulatory reporting, compliance dashboards)
- **Reason:** Personal hooks can be perceived as persuasive framing and may undermine perceived objectivity or fairness.
- **Scenario:** Audiences with highly diverse contexts where “everyday” references won’t generalize (e.g., global audiences with different baselines, costs, seasons)
- **Reason:** A single reference point can mislead or alienate people whose lived experience differs.

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

- **The Sacrifice:** Space and simplicity—contextual explanations, examples, and annotations take room and add reading time.
- **The Risk:** Over-personalization can bias interpretation, prompt unhelpful anxiety, or distract from the core message if the “relatable” element becomes the story.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **The Wrong Fix:** Adding a generic human-interest photo or a vague tagline (“This affects us all”) without linking to the data.
- **Why it fails:** It signals relevance but doesn’t help viewers translate values into lived meaning, so the chart still feels abstract.
- **The Wrong Fix:** Using sensational anecdotes or extreme scenarios as the primary framing.
- **Why it fails:** It can distort perceived typicality and reduce trust in the visualization’s intent.

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

- **Visual Sign:** The chart shows values and trends, but a viewer can’t answer “So what changes for my area, routine, or decisions?” without outside knowledge.
- **The Test:** Ask 2–3 target viewers to complete this prompt in one sentence after 10 seconds: “This means that in my life/where I live, \_\_\_\_\_\_.” If they can’t, the connection is missing.

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

- **Quick Fix:** Add one concrete anchor near the main takeaway (e.g., “In a typical summer in City X…” / “Equivalent to Y days of…” / “Within a 15-minute walk of…”), and label the relevant region/timeframe.
- **Best Fix:** Reframe the visualization around lived context: localize by default (place-based views), provide “nearest to you” comparisons, and annotate implications (who/where/when is affected), while keeping the underlying data accessible and clearly sourced.
