## Use tables (not charts) for strongly negatively polarized audiences <!-- role: advice -->

Use tables rather than charts when your target audience is strongly opposed to the advocated position and you are trying to move their attitude in your direction. Keep all explanatory text constant and vary only the data format.

## Why tables can outperform charts among strong opponents <!-- role: reason -->

When viewers are strongly opposed, the more rhetorical “vivid” feel of charts may provoke greater resistance or skepticism, while tables may be perceived as more neutral or less manipulative, increasing receptivity to the same evidence.

**Mechanism:** Initial negative attitudes can lead viewers to scrutinize and resist persuasive cues; a less visually emphatic representation may reduce reactive counter-arguing.

**Evidence:** Across three topic experiments, negatively polarized participants showed higher persuasion likelihood with tables than charts in all topics (aggregated p ≈ 0.00063) [@pandeyPersuasivePowerData2014]. Aggregated attitude change also favored tables over charts for negatively polarized participants (mean difference for charts minus tables ≈ -1.063; p ≈ 0.001), though topic-level uncertainty was higher due to smaller subgroup sizes [@pandeyPersuasivePowerData2014].

**Notes:** The effect was observed despite identical narrative framing and identical underlying data, differing only in table vs chart presentation.

## When this applies: persuasion attempts against prior opposition <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Reconsider a debated claim they initially disagree with.
- **Task:** Update an attitude rating after reading evidence.
- **Data:** Statistical evidence supporting a causal or comparative claim.
- **Chart Setting:** Narrative message with multiple evidence blocks; tables vs charts as the manipulated factor.
- **Audience:** People with strong negative initial attitudes toward the advocated claim.
- **Success Criterion:** Increased fraction of positive attitude change within the negatively polarized segment.

## When not to follow it: neutral/weakly polarized audiences <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The audience starts neutral or only weakly polarized. **Why:** In that segment, charts consistently increased persuasion likelihood and (in aggregate) increased attitude change compared with tables [@pandeyPersuasivePowerData2014].

## Tradeoffs of using tables for persuasion <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Tables can make patterns harder to see quickly, which may reduce persuasion among undecided readers. **Risk:** Readers may not extract the intended trends without effort, leading to no change. **Mitigation:** Use attention checks or comprehension probes during testing to confirm the table communicates the intended evidence.

## Common failure modes when using tables to persuade opponents <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Treating the “tables work better for opponents” result as universally topic-independent. **Why it fails:** Topic appeared to modulate strength of the effect and subgroup samples were smaller, increasing uncertainty [@pandeyPersuasivePowerData2014].
- **Mistake:** Ignoring segmentation and reporting only overall persuasion rates. **Why it fails:** Aggregated results can hide reversals by initial attitude (Simpson’s-paradox-like masking) [@pandeyPersuasivePowerData2014].

## Quick checks for this guideline <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Strong opponents show equal or lower positive attitude change with tables than with charts for the same evidence. **Quick Check:** Pre-segment participants by initial attitude buckets and compare (+) change rates for tables vs charts within the negative bucket. **Stronger Test:** Run a randomized controlled comparison (tables vs charts) and analyze effects separately for negative, neutral, and positive initial attitudes.

## What to do instead if tables don’t move opponents <!-- role: fix -->

- Measure and address skepticism drivers by collecting open-ended explanations for lack of change (e.g., distrust of data or logic).
- Test additional presentation variants while keeping the narrative constant to isolate what reduces resistance in your context.
- Add a control condition with no evidence to determine whether the data itself is helping at all for this audience.
- Replicate across multiple topics to check whether your topic amplifies or suppresses the table advantage.
