## Explain what the graph shows and what conclusion to draw <!-- role: advice -->

Provide a clear explanation of what each risk graph means and explicitly state the key conclusion the viewer should take from it.

## Why explanations and stated conclusions improve comprehension and persuasion <!-- role: reason -->

Graphs can be unfamiliar or cognitively demanding, especially in medical contexts. Explanations and explicit conclusions guide attention to the relevant features and reduce misinterpretation.

**Mechanism:** Instruction helps viewers map graphical elements to their meanings and reduces reliance on superficial cues, improving comprehension and the likelihood that the intended message is taken.

**Evidence:** Verbal instruction improved understanding of survival curve comparisons, and providing verbal explanations of conclusions made a complex genetic-risk graphic more comprehensible and persuasive than a caption alone. [@lipkusNumericVerbalVisual2007]

**Notes:** Auditory explanations can also support accessibility for readers with visual impairments.

## When this applies <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand what a risk graphic implies for decisions or actions.
- **Task:** Interpret a graph; extract a key takeaway; compare treatments over time.
- **Data:** Risks displayed graphically; may include survival/mortality curves or complex visuals.
- **Chart Setting:** Clinical encounters, patient decision aids, public health materials.
- **Audience:** People with limited graph literacy or limited time.
- **Success Criterion:** Correct interpretation of the intended takeaway and reduced confusion.

## When not to follow it <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The graphic is a simple, universally familiar comparison with explicit labels and no plausible alternative reading. **Why:** Extra explanation may be redundant and consume space.

## Tradeoffs and risks <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Space and attention devoted to explanation reduces room for additional data. **Risk:** Overly directive conclusions can reduce deliberation about tradeoffs. **Mitigation:** Keep the conclusion factual and tie it to the displayed quantities.

## Common failure modes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Relying on a caption alone for a complex or unfamiliar risk graph. **Why it fails:** Viewers may not know how to translate the visual into the intended inference.

## Quick tests <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Readers ask what the axes or regions “mean” or draw different conclusions from the same graphic. **Quick Check:** Remove the explanation and see if a pilot reader can restate the takeaway accurately; if not, the explanation is needed. **Stronger Test:** Use a brief comprehension quiz (one question per graph) in a small user test.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Add a short plain-language explanation of what each visual element represents.
- State the key conclusion as a single sentence adjacent to the graphic.
- Provide spoken or audio-supported explanation in settings where visual access is limited.
- Replace the graphic with a simpler display if explanation must become long to prevent misunderstanding.
