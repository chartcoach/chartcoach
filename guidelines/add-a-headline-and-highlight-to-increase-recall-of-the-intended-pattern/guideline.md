## Add an explicit message headline and highlight the relevant data subset <!-- role: advice -->

Add a one-sentence headline that states the intended takeaway and use a distinct highlight color to emphasize only the data marks that support that takeaway.

## Why headlines plus highlighting shape what people remember <!-- role: reason -->

Focusing cues constrain attention toward a specific pattern among many plausible patterns, so the remembered “story” becomes more aligned with the highlighted interpretation rather than drifting to whatever each viewer personally finds salient.

**Mechanism:** A written takeaway primes what to look for, and a unique color reduces search and competition among marks, increasing the chance that the viewer encodes the intended relation as the main memory trace.

**Evidence:** Across six topics, focused (decluttered+focused) designs led to higher focus-relevant recall than cluttered and decluttered designs in both redraw-from-memory outcomes and typed free-response conclusions, with focused designs producing roughly 2.5–3× higher odds of recalling a focus-relevant conclusion than non-focused designs [@ajaniDeclutterFocusEmpirically2022a].

**Notes:** The tested focus manipulation combined both annotation (headline) and highlight color, so the evidence supports the combination rather than isolating each component.

## When focusing for recall applies <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Ensure the audience leaves with a specific takeaway from a chart.
- **Task:** Remember and later restate the main conclusion; reproduce the gist of the pattern from memory.
- **Data:** Multivariate charts where multiple different conclusions could reasonably be drawn.
- **Chart Setting:** Brief viewing time (e.g., presentation slides) with limited opportunity for extended inspection.
- **Audience:** General audiences who may not share the presenter’s “curse of knowledge.”
- **Success Criterion:** Higher alignment between what the designer intends and what viewers later recall.

## When not to follow it <!-- role: exceptions -->

- **Break it when:** The goal is open-ended exploration or group sensemaking where multiple interpretations should remain equally available. **Why:** Focus cues bias attention toward one interpretation and can suppress other useful readings.
- **Break it when:** A neutral tone is required and the audience may interpret emphasis as agenda-driven persuasion. **Why:** Some viewers may see strong emphasis and annotation as “pushy,” which can reduce perceived neutrality.

## Tradeoffs and risks <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Space for annotation and design effort to choose a single message. **Risk:** Highlighting can over-narrow attention so secondary context is missed. **Mitigation:** Keep non-highlighted context visible but visually de-emphasized, and ensure the headline matches what the data actually supports.

## Common failure modes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Highlighting many unrelated elements in the same “accent” color. **Why it fails:** It removes the attentional cue that makes the intended subset stand out.
- **Mistake:** Writing a headline that does not correspond to the highlighted marks. **Why it fails:** Viewers may remember the text but not encode the correct supporting pattern, harming message alignment.
- **Mistake:** Assuming viewers will converge on the same takeaway without focus cues. **Why it fails:** Viewers generated a wide variety of different conclusions when focus was not provided.

## Quick tests <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Different viewers summarize the same chart with different “main points,” or they miss the intended takeaway after a short delay. **Quick Check:** Ask a colleague to look for 10 seconds and then tell you “the one thing this chart says”; if it is not your headline, the focus is weak. **Stronger Test:** Run a brief recall test (redraw gist or write the conclusion) and code whether the response includes the intended pattern.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Add a single-sentence takeaway statement that describes the intended pattern in plain language.
- Use one unique highlight color on only the marks that instantiate the takeaway, keeping the rest in neutral tones.
- Place brief text near the highlighted marks so the connection between claim and evidence is visually explicit.
- If multiple messages matter, split into multiple charts or multiple focused views rather than highlighting everything at once.
