## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->
Use a distinct color to make your most important value stand out, and use shades of one color (e.g., grey) for the rest of the slices. Avoid rainbow color palettes.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->
Rainbow colors distract readers from the data. Using a single highlight color directs the eye immediately to the key insight, while monochromatic shades for the remaining slices keep the context without competing for attention [@muth_pie_charts_2018].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->
*   **User Goal:** Storytelling or emphasizing a specific data point.
*   **Data Type:** Categorical data where one category is the "hero."
*   **Audience:** Readers who need to understand the main takeaway quickly.

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->
*   **Scenario:** Every slice is equally important (no specific story to tell).
*   **Reason:** Highlighting one slice might imply a bias or importance that doesn't exist.

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->
*   **The Sacrifice:** You lose the ability to color-code categories by brand identity or other categorical variables.
*   **The Risk:** The "other" slices blend together visually.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->
*   **The Wrong Fix:** Assigning a random bright color to every slice.
*   **Why it fails:** It creates a "fruit salad" effect that is hard to read.

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->
*   **Visual Sign:** Is the chart multicolored like a rainbow?
*   **The Test:** Close your eyes and open them. Where does your eye go first? If it's not the main data point, the coloring is wrong.

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->
*   **Quick Fix:** Change the most interesting slice to red/blue and the rest to greyscale.
*   **Best Fix:** Define the story you are telling, then use color to support that specific story [@muth_pie_charts_2018].
