## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

Design and test patient education materials under the assumption that a large share of your audience has inadequate health literacy and will also struggle with numeracy and graph interpretation.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

People who score lower on health literacy also tend to have lower objective numeracy and lower graph literacy, meaning that simply “adding a chart” or “adding numbers” can compound comprehension problems rather than solve them.

- **The Principle:** Coupled literacy demands (text + numbers + graphs)
- **The Evidence:** In veterans, health literacy correlated strongly with objective numeracy and graph literacy, and veterans with inadequate health literacy had substantially lower numeracy and graph literacy scores [@rodriguezHealthLiteracyNumeracy2013].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understanding risks/benefits or instructions well enough to decide or act
- **Data Type:** Quantitative health information (probabilities, counts, lab values, medication schedules) presented in text, tables, or charts
- **Audience:** Veterans in primary care, especially populations with high rates of inadequate health literacy [@rodriguezHealthLiteracyNumeracy2013]

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

- **Scenario:** Materials intended only for clinicians or trained staff with verified competency
- **Reason:** The audience is not the patient population studied, and the literacy/numeracy constraints may not apply [@rodriguezHealthLiteracyNumeracy2013].

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

- **The Sacrifice:** More design time for simplification, testing, and revision
- **The Risk:** Oversimplifying can omit nuance needed by higher-skill readers [@rodriguezHealthLiteracyNumeracy2013].

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **The Wrong Fix:** Replacing prose with dense numeric summaries or complex charts “to be precise”
- **Why it fails:** Lower health literacy often co-occurs with lower numeracy and graph literacy, so this increases cognitive burden rather than improving understanding [@rodriguezHealthLiteracyNumeracy2013].

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

- **Visual Sign:** The design requires interpreting a nutrition-label-like panel, doing multi-step calculations, or inferring trends from a graph without guidance.
- **The Test:** Ask a small sample of intended users to explain the message and what action they should take; if many cannot, the design is not robust to low literacy/numeracy [@rodriguezHealthLiteracyNumeracy2013].

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

- **Quick Fix:** Reduce the number of required calculations/inferences; add plain-language explanations adjacent to numbers/graphs.
- **Best Fix:** Redesign the communication so the key takeaway can be understood without advanced numeracy or graph-reading skills, then re-test with the target population [@rodriguezHealthLiteracyNumeracy2013].
