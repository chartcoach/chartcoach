## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

Use a line graph when your main communication goal is the x–y interaction (how the y trend over x differs across the legend variable).

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

Line graphs visually connect points into continuous traces, which makes the x–y relationship and its moderation by the legend variable highly salient, shaping what viewers describe as “the main point.”

- **The Principle:** Good continuity drives visual chunking toward lines (interaction-focused interpretation).
- **The Evidence:** Viewers produced more x–y interaction descriptions for line graphs than bar graphs in open-ended summaries of three-variable data [@shahBarLineGraph2011].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand whether trends over x differ by group (moderation/interaction).
- **Data Type:** Three-variable (two IVs × one DV) data with multiple levels per IV (medium complexity).
- **Audience:** Mixed/unknown audiences when you want the interaction to be the default takeaway.

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

- **Scenario:** You need viewers to report main effects (overall differences) rather than interactions.
- **Reason:** The line format can pull attention toward the interaction and away from main-effect summaries [@shahBarLineGraph2011].

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

- **The Sacrifice:** Viewers may underreport main effects even when they exist.
- **The Risk:** The interaction becomes the “story,” potentially crowding out other important relationships [@shahBarLineGraph2011].

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **The Wrong Fix:** Using a line graph expecting viewers to naturally summarize overall (main) effects.
- **Why it fails:** Open-ended interpretations tend to follow the most salient visual chunks—lines—leading to interaction-heavy descriptions [@shahBarLineGraph2011].

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

- **Visual Sign:** People’s summaries focus on “as x increases…” separately for each line, with little mention of overall differences.
- **The Test:** Ask 2–3 users to write 2–4 “main points.” If most report only trend-by-group statements, you have foregrounded the x–y interaction [@shahBarLineGraph2011].

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

- **Quick Fix:** If main effects matter, add explicit prompts/instructions that ask for overall differences across x or across groups.
- **Best Fix:** Switch to a bar graph when the intended takeaway is main effects or flexible comparisons (see companion guideline) [@shahBarLineGraph2011].
