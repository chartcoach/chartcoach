## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

Present risk information for diagnostic or screening tests as natural frequencies (e.g., "10 out of 100") rather than percentages (e.g., "10%") or probabilities (e.g., "0.1").

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

People generally reason better with absolute numbers than with abstract probabilities. In the context of diagnostic and screening tests, natural frequencies are more intuitive than percentages.
*   **The Principle:** Natural Frequency Effect
*   **The Evidence:** A systematic review found that participants understood natural frequencies significantly better than percentages (Standardized Mean Difference 0.69, moderate effect size) [@akl_using_2011].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

This advice is designed for specific moments.
*   **User Goal:** Understanding the likelihood of a disease given a test result or prevalence.
*   **Data Type:** Diagnostic accuracy statistics (sensitivity, specificity) or screening prevalence.
*   **Audience:** Both health professionals and consumers (patients/general public).

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

List the specific scenarios where you should ignore this rule.
*   **Scenario:** Comparing groups with vastly different denominators.
*   **Reason:** While frequencies aid understanding of a single risk, comparing "3 in 200" to "8 in 500" requires normalization, where percentages might facilitate easier direct comparison. (Note: The review specifically highlights the benefit for diagnostic/screening contexts).

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

Be honest about the downsides.
*   **The Sacrifice:** Brevity. "10 out of 100 people" takes up more space than "10%".
*   **The Risk:** Inaccurate denominators. If you normalize frequencies (e.g., converting "1 in 20" to "50 in 1000"), you must ensure the conversion is mathematically precise.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

Describe the common anti-patterns or "lazy fixes" people use that don't actually solve the problem.
*   **The Wrong Fix:** Using probabilities (e.g., 0.05).
*   **Why it fails:** Decimals are often the hardest format for lay audiences to grasp intuitively compared to frequencies or percentages.

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

*   **Visual Sign:** Look for the "%" symbol or decimal points (0.x) in your risk labels.
*   **The Test:** Read the label aloud. If it sounds like a math problem ("12 percent of the population"), try converting it to a story ("12 people out of every 100").

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

*   **Quick Fix:** Rewrite the label text (e.g., change "15% Risk" to "15 in 100").
*   **Best Fix:** Use a pictorial unit chart (isotype) representing the frequency visually (e.g., 100 icons where 15 are highlighted) to reinforce the text.
