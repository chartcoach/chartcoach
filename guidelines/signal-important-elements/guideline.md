## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->
Explicitly guide the user's attention using cues such as arrows, color coding, headings, or vocal stress on key words.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->
When a presentation is complex or contains incidental details that cannot be removed, users may struggle to select the relevant information.
*   **The Mechanism:** Signaling acts as a cognitive guide, reducing the load of "selecting" and "organizing." It tells the user exactly where to look and what is important, reducing incidental processing of non-essential areas [@mayer_nine_2003].
*   **The Evidence:** This is the *Signaling Effect*. Students learn better when cues are provided (Effect Size 0.74) [@mayer_nine_2003].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->
*   **User Goal:** Finding specific information within a dense display.
*   **Data Type:** Complex graphics, maps, or animations where the focal point changes.
*   **Audience:** Novices who do not know where to look.

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->
*   **Scenario:** The visual is extremely simple.
*   **Reason:** Over-signaling simple content can add clutter (visual noise) rather than clarity.

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->
*   **The Sacrifice:** Visual aesthetics can be compromised by large arrows or bold highlights.
*   **The Risk:** Incorrect signaling is worse than no signaling—it misdirects attention.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->
*   **The Wrong Fix:** Making everything bold or colorful.
*   **Why it fails:** If everything is signaled, nothing is signaled. Cues must be selective.

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->
*   **Visual Sign:** A busy graphic where the user has to hunt for the item being discussed in the narration.
*   **The Test:** Watch the presentation without sound. Can you follow the "story" just by following the visual highlights/arrows?

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->
*   **Quick Fix:** Add red arrows pointing to the active component in an animation.
*   **Best Fix:** Coordinate visual signaling (arrows/color change) with auditory signaling (vocal emphasis) to doubly reinforce the focal point.
