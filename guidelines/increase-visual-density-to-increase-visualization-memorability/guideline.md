## Prefer higher visual density when memorability is the goal <!-- role: advice -->

Increase visual density (more visual elements in the image) when you need the visualization to be remembered as an image rather than to look minimal.

## Why higher density can improve memorability <!-- role: reason -->

Dense images can provide more distinctive visual features that help recognition and reduce the chance of confusing one visualization for another.

**Mechanism:** Additional features create more retrieval cues and can make the image more visually unique within a set.

**Evidence:** Visualizations rated as high in visual density achieved higher memorability scores than low-density visualizations in an online repeat-detection experiment [@borkinWhatMakesVisualization2013a].

**Notes:** The density measure in the study reflects overall element density and does not separate “data” marks from “non-data” marks.

## When to apply higher visual density <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Remember the visualization later.
- **Task:** Recognize the visualization among many similar items.
- **Data:** Any; especially when baseline chart forms are repetitive.
- **Chart Setting:** Static images; brief exposures; competitive attention environments.
- **Audience:** Broad audiences; quick scanning behavior.
- **Success Criterion:** Higher recognition with low confusion.

## When not to increase density <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The success criterion is comprehension of values or trends rather than recognition of the image. **Why:** The evidence tests memorability-as-image and does not establish that denser displays improve understanding [@borkinWhatMakesVisualization2013a].

## Tradeoffs of higher visual density <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Visual simplicity.\
**Risk:** Viewers may focus on dense decorative elements rather than the intended quantitative signal.\
**Mitigation:** Ensure added density still supports the intended narrative or message.

## Common mistakes with visual density <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Adding dense elements assuming it will make the data easier to understand. **Why it fails:** The measured improvement is in memorability of the image, not comprehension [@borkinWhatMakesVisualization2013a].

## Quick checks for density choices <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Viewers remember “there was a lot going on” but cannot recall what the visualization was about.\
**Quick Check:** Ask a viewer to describe the main message after a brief delay; if they only describe decoration, density may be dominating memory.\
**Stronger Test:** Test both recall of the image and recall of the intended message separately.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Add a small number of distinctive, information-carrying features rather than uniformly increasing clutter.
- Use color variety (as measured in the study) to improve discriminability without adding many more marks.
- Introduce a recognizable pictorial cue if appropriate to the setting.
- Switch to a more distinctive visualization type if the base form is highly confusable.
