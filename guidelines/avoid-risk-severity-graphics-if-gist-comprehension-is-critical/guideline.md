## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

If your primary goal is to maximize gist understanding of comparative probabilities, do not add a separate risk-severity graphic layer.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

Adding a severity graphic can introduce competing visual/emotional salience that distracts from the core comparative probability task, reducing gist understanding and shifting perceptions of risk/benefit.

- **The Principle:** Avoid additional visual elements that compete with the main comparison task
- **The Evidence:** Participants receiving severity graphics had lower adequate gist understanding, perceived risks as higher and benefits as lower, and were less willing to consent, while verbatim understanding was unchanged [@taitEffectFormatParents2010].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Accurately identify which option has higher/lower risks and benefits (gist)
- **Data Type:** Multi-outcome risk/benefit comparisons already containing probability information
- **Audience:** Parents/guardians in research consent-like decisions

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

- **Scenario:** Your primary objective is to intentionally increase attention to seriousness (severity) even at the cost of gist probability comprehension.
- **Reason:** The paper indicates severity graphics can raise perceived risk and lower perceived benefit; if that is the intended effect, the tradeoff may be acceptable [@taitEffectFormatParents2010].

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

- **The Sacrifice:** Less explicit signaling about which outcomes are mild vs severe
- **The Risk:** Without severity cues, users may overweight frequent mild effects relative to rare severe effects (the paper raises this concern as a motivation, though the tested severity graphic did not help gist) [@taitEffectFormatParents2010].

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **The Wrong Fix:** Adding a bold severity scale to “balance” probability graphics without testing comprehension.
- **Why it fails:** In this study, the added severity graphic reduced adequate gist understanding and shifted perceptions, suggesting interference rather than clarification [@taitEffectFormatParents2010].

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

- **Visual Sign:** Users focus discussion on severity cues while making errors about which drug changes which probability.
- **The Test:** Compare gist-question accuracy with vs without the severity graphic; if gist drops while verbatim stays flat, the added layer is likely distracting (as observed) [@taitEffectFormatParents2010].

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

- **Quick Fix:** Remove the severity graphic and keep severity as brief text descriptors (“mild/moderate/severe”) only.
- **Best Fix:** If severity must be communicated visually, test alternative designs and verify they do not reduce gist accuracy (the paper indicates the tested severity graphic had negative effects on gist) [@taitEffectFormatParents2010].
