## Report absolute risk reduction alongside relative risk reduction <!-- role: advice -->

When you report a treatment effect, include the absolute risk reduction (ARR) together with the relative risk reduction (RRR) rather than reporting RRR alone.

## Absolute risk reduction prevents inflated impressions of benefit <!-- role: reason -->

Reporting only RRR can make an intervention feel more impactful than it is because it omits the baseline event rate and therefore hides how small the absolute change may be in primary prevention settings.

**Mechanism:** ARR anchors interpretation to baseline risk, reducing over-optimistic impressions that can arise when attention is drawn only to proportional change.

**Evidence:** Physicians who received identical trial outcomes framed as ARR rated lipid-lowering drugs as less effective than physicians who received the same outcomes framed as RRR, and they were less inclined to prescribe treatment in a primary-prevention scenario [@bucherInfluenceMethodReporting1994].

**Notes:** This effect was observed for myocardial infarction outcomes but not for all-cause mortality ratings in this study.

## Contexts where clinicians must interpret trial summaries for prescribing decisions <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Judge whether a preventive drug is effective enough to justify prescribing.
- **Task:** Interpret reported trial results and translate them into a treatment decision.
- **Data:** Binary clinical events with low baseline incidence (typical of primary prevention).
- **Chart Setting:** Textual or tabular presentation of trial outcomes in papers, summaries, or promotional materials.
- **Audience:** Clinicians making prescribing decisions.
- **Success Criterion:** Readers form calibrated impressions of benefit and make consistent decisions given the same evidence.

## Exceptions: When not to follow it <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** Space is so constrained that only one measure can be shown. **Why:** The format may not permit multiple effect-size expressions without harming readability.

## Costs: Tradeoffs and risks <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** More room and slightly more cognitive load to read multiple metrics. **Risk:** Readers may be confused if ARR and RRR are presented without clear labeling. **Mitigation:** Keep labels explicit and place ARR and RRR adjacent.

## Mistakes: Common failure modes <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Reporting only RRR as the headline effect size. **Why it fails:** It can lead readers to rate an intervention as more effective and to be more inclined to treat than when they see ARR for the same underlying data [@bucherInfluenceMethodReporting1994].

## Check: Quick tests <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** The result statement contains a percent reduction but no baseline risk or absolute change.\
**Quick Check:** Scan for both “relative” and an absolute expression (e.g., events per N people over time) in the same result block.\
**Stronger Test:** Give two clinicians the summary and ask them to estimate how many events are prevented per 1000 treated; large disagreement suggests missing absolute context.

## Fix: What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Add an ARR statement alongside the RRR statement for the same endpoint and time horizon.
- Include the event rates (treated and control) that ARR is derived from, stated in the same units and timeframe.
- Place ARR and RRR next to each other in the same sentence or table row to prevent selective reading.
- If you must choose one metric, replace RRR-only reporting with an absolute expression tied to a population denominator and timeframe.
