## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

Frame abstract metrics and large-scale data using familiar concepts, personal contexts, or everyday realities.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

Abstract data often fails to resonate because it lacks a tether to the viewer's lived reality. When viewers can relate data to their own lives, engagement and comprehension increase significantly.

*   **The Principle:** Personal Relevance.
*   **The Evidence:** Research shows that lay audiences frequently dismiss detached charts as uninteresting. Visualizations that utilize relatable references or solution framing are viewed as significantly more motivating [@schuster_being_2024]. In the context of crisis mapping, connecting data to personal memories (such as past heatwaves) or local risks drives empathy and a sense of personal responsibility [@koesten_encountering_2025]. Furthermore, references to local events or familiar timeframes have been observed to trigger emotional reactions and prompt viewers to search for further information [@koesten_what_2023].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

*   **User Goal:** When the objective is to motivate behavior change, evoke empathy, or explain complex phenomena to non-experts.
*   **Data Type:** Large-scale abstractions (e.g., climate change gigatons, national budget billions) or crisis data.
*   **Audience:** The general public or lay audiences who lack the domain expertise to intuitively grasp technical units.

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

*   **Scenario:** Technical analysis for domain experts.
*   **Reason:** Scientists or financial analysts often require raw, precise units for calculation and comparison. Forcing a "relatable" metaphor can introduce ambiguity or be perceived as condescending.

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

*   **The Sacrifice:** You may lose strict scientific neutrality. Framing data through personal experience creates an emotional angle that can be seen as subjective.
*   **The Risk:** If the "everyday experience" chosen is not actually familiar to the specific audience (e.g., a sports metaphor for a non-sporting audience), the visualization may alienate them further.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

*   **The Wrong Fix:** Using clichéd or irrelevant unit conversions (e.g., "The height of 500 Giraffes").
*   **Why it fails:** While "tangible," these comparisons are rarely meaningful to the user's actual life experience or emotional context.
*   **The Wrong Fix:** Oversimplifying complex systems into anecdotal evidence.
*   **Why it fails:** This can lead to the "ecological fallacy," where users assume a specific personal truth applies to the whole dataset.

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

*   **Visual Sign:** The chart relies entirely on technical units (e.g., "Metric Tons," "P-values") with no accompanying explanatory text.
*   **The Test:** The "So What?" Test. Ask a user, "What does this number mean for your daily life?" If they cannot answer, the data is not connected to their experience.

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

*   **Quick Fix:** Add an annotation that translates a technical number into a human-scale equivalent (e.g., "This amount of carbon equals driving a car for one year").
*   **Best Fix:** Reframe the visualization to highlight local or personal dimensions first—such as showing data for the user's specific neighborhood or timeframe—before zooming out to the aggregate view.
