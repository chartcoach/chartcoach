## Teach and assess both reading and construction of visualizations, not just interpretation <!-- role: advice -->

Pair visualization interpretation exercises with construction exercises where learners must choose a reference system and map data variables to graphic symbols and graphic variables.

## Why construction strengthens literacy beyond recognition <!-- role: reason -->

Interpretation alone can leave learners reliant on surface cues and familiar chart patterns, while construction forces explicit decisions about data scales, analyses, encodings, and workflow steps. This supports deeper procedural knowledge and exposes misconceptions that interpretation questions may not reveal.

**Mechanism:** Constructing a visualization requires linking theoretical categories (such as scales, encodings, and visualization types) to procedural steps (acquire, analyze, visualize, deploy, interpret), which strengthens transferable understanding.

**Evidence:** A key gap is that many approaches do not cover assessing construction, and the framework explicitly promotes both reading and construction; it also notes evidence that construction can lead to better understanding and interpretation than deconstructing a finished visualization [@bornerDataVisualizationLiteracy2019].

**Notes:** Construction can be scaffolded from simple mapping tasks to full client-style workflows.

## When to include construction in literacy work <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Build durable data visualization literacy rather than short-term test performance.
- **Task:** Learn to select analyses and create visual encodings that satisfy insight needs.
- **Data:** Small-to-moderate datasets suitable for hands-on mapping and iteration.
- **Chart Setting:** Courses, workshops, museums, or workplace training.
- **Audience:** Novices and mixed-skill groups.
- **Success Criterion:** Learners can create a correct visualization from a table and explain the mapping from data to encodings.

## When construction tasks may be out of scope <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The setting only permits rapid, low-stakes exposure (for example, a short briefing with no tools available). **Why:** Construction requires time and an environment that supports making and revising artifacts.

## Tradeoffs of adding construction assessment <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** More time, tooling, and grading effort than multiple-choice interpretation tests. **Risk:** Learners may be judged on tool proficiency rather than literacy. **Mitigation:** Use rubrics focused on mapping correctness and insight support rather than aesthetic polish.

## Common ways construction assessment goes wrong <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Scoring visualizations only on appearance without checking whether the mappings support the stated insight needs. **Why it fails:** Visual polish can hide incorrect encodings or irrelevant analyses.

## Quick checks for balanced literacy coverage <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Learners can answer questions about a provided chart but cannot build a working chart from the same data. **Quick Check:** Give a tiny dataset and ask for a visualization plus a one-sentence description of what each encoding represents. **Stronger Test:** Use pre/post tasks that measure both interpretation accuracy and construction correctness across multiple visualization types.

## What to do when you can only add a little construction <!-- role: fix -->

- Use a mapping exercise where learners reconstruct a data table from a visualization’s encodings.
- Ask learners to create a visualization from a provided dataset with the chart type fixed, focusing only on symbol/variable mapping.
- Provide a rubric that checks insight need fit, data-scale validity, and encoding correctness.
- Incorporate peer review structured around the same rubric to practice evaluation as well as creation.
