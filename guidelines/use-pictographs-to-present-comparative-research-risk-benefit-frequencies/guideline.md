## Use pictographs for comparative risk/benefit frequencies <!-- role: advice -->

Use pictographs (icon arrays of 100 units) instead of plain numerical text or tables when presenting comparative frequencies for research risks and benefits. Keep the reference population constant (e.g., out of 100) across all outcomes.

## Why pictographs improve risk/benefit comprehension <!-- role: reason -->

Pictographs make proportions visually salient while preserving the numeric meaning, which supports both extracting the overall direction of differences (gist) and recalling exact frequencies (verbatim) when people compare options.

**Mechanism:** Mapping frequencies onto a fixed set of units reduces mental arithmetic and supports parallel visual-and-verbal processing of the same quantitative information.

**Evidence:** Parents shown pictographs were more likely to achieve adequate gist understanding and adequate verbatim knowledge than those shown text or tables in a randomized Internet survey about pediatric research risks/benefits [@taitEffectFormatParents2010]. This advantage held across both lower- and higher-numeracy participants [@taitEffectFormatParents2010].

**Notes:** In this study, text and tables produced similar levels of understanding, while pictographs performed better on both gist and verbatim measures.

## When comparative pictographs apply <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand and compare research risks and benefits across study arms to support an informed participation decision.
- **Task:** Identify which option has more/less of an outcome and report approximate frequencies.
- **Data:** Multiple outcomes with probabilities expressed as frequencies (e.g., X out of 100) for two alternatives.
- **Chart Setting:** Consent materials, decision aids, or survey/online consent flows where users may review the information while answering questions.
- **Audience:** Parents or other lay decision-makers with mixed numeracy.
- **Success Criterion:** Higher rates of adequate gist and verbatim understanding of the communicated statistics.

## When not to follow pictographs-first <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The presentation cannot reasonably use a fixed, countable reference set (e.g., “out of 100”) for the outcomes being compared. **Why:** The tested benefit depends on a consistent frequency frame that can be mapped to discrete units.

## Tradeoffs of pictographs <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Pictographs take more space than a compact table or sentence. **Risk:** The format can shift perceptions (e.g., lower perceived risk and higher perceived benefit) relative to text/tables even when numbers are identical. **Mitigation:** Monitor perception changes as an outcome when choosing formats for consent materials.

## Common pictograph failure modes <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Assuming tables will substantially improve understanding over text for comparative risk/benefit frequencies. **Why it fails:** In this study, tables and text yielded similar gist and verbatim understanding, while pictographs performed better [@taitEffectFormatParents2010].

## Quick checks for pictograph effectiveness <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Users cannot reliably state which option has more/less of an outcome or cannot report approximate frequencies despite being able to reread the information. **Quick Check:** Ask a small set of gist (direction) and verbatim (frequency) questions while allowing users to reference the display. **Stronger Test:** Track the proportion meeting an “adequate understanding” threshold for gist and verbatim knowledge in a pilot.

## What to do instead if pictographs are not feasible <!-- role: fix -->

- Use a single, consistent frequency frame in text (e.g., “out of 100”) for every outcome and option.
- Provide a compact table only if you can validate that users can answer both gist and verbatim questions from it in a pilot.
- Reduce the number of outcomes shown at once by grouping or paging so users can attend to one outcome comparison at a time.
- Add a summary screen that lets users review all risk/benefit numbers while answering comprehension questions.
