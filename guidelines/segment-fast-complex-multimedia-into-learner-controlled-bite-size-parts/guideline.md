## Segment complex lessons into learner-advanced chunks <!-- role: advice -->

Present complex narrated animations in bite-size segments and let the learner advance to the next segment when ready.

## Why segmenting enables deeper processing <!-- role: reason -->

When content is rich and rapid, learners can spend capacity selecting words and images but run out of time to organize them into coherent verbal and pictorial models and to integrate them. Segmenting adds temporal space between chunks so essential organizing and integrating processes can complete before new material arrives.

**Mechanism:** Pauses reduce concurrent essential-processing demands, allowing working memory to move from selection to organization and integration.

**Evidence:** Learner-controlled segmented presentations produced higher transfer than continuous presentations with the same material, consistent with a segmentation effect [@mayerNineWaysReduce2003].

**Notes:** This method targets overload where both channels are burdened by essential processing due to complexity and speed.

## When complexity and pace prevent integration <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Build a coherent explanation of a causal system.
- **Task:** Organize and integrate steps across narration and animation.
- **Data:** Many interacting components and steps presented quickly.
- **Chart Setting:** Continuous narrated animation without natural pauses.
- **Audience:** Learners without strong prior schemas for the topic.
- **Success Criterion:** Improved transfer (applying the explanation to novel problems).

## When to avoid learner-controlled segmentation <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The learning environment cannot support added time or interaction (e.g., fixed-time broadcast delivery). **Why:** The core benefit depends on giving learners time between chunks to complete deeper processing.

## Tradeoffs of segmentation <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Longer total lesson time and added interaction design. **Risk:** Poorly chosen segment boundaries can interrupt coherence. **Mitigation:** Choose boundaries that align with meaningful steps in the causal process.

## Common segmentation failures <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Keeping the lesson continuous but adding a single pause only at the end. **Why it fails:** It does not create processing time at the moments when working memory is being overwhelmed.

## How to tell whether you need segments <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Learners report that the lesson “moves too fast” and cannot explain relationships between steps. **Quick Check:** If multiple new elements appear before earlier ones can be mentally related, the pace likely exceeds capacity. **Stronger Test:** A/B test continuous versus segmented delivery using the same transfer test.

## Other ways to reduce essential-load overload <!-- role: fix -->

- Pretrain learners on component names and characteristics before showing the full causal explanation.
- Reduce conceptual density per unit time by shortening each segment’s content.
- Remove extraneous additions that compete for processing resources.
- Use simultaneous narration and animation within each segment to reduce holding demands.
