## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

When you want viewers to infer and report main effects in three-variable data, use a bar graph rather than a line graph—especially when the topic is familiar to them.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

Main effects require mentally aggregating across the third variable. In the study’s open-ended “main point” task, bar graphs supported those inferences more than line graphs, but primarily when viewers had familiar content (and therefore expectations about likely effects).

- **The Principle:** Format can reduce cognitive load for aggregation and steer what users treat as “important.”
- **The Evidence:** Main-effect inference rates were higher with bar graphs than line graphs for familiar data; format × familiarity effects appeared for main-effect descriptions [@shahBarLineGraph2011].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Extract “overall” differences (main effects) rather than conditional patterns.
- **Data Type:** Three-variable graphs where users must simplify/aggregate to summarize.
- **Audience:** Viewers likely to have expectations about the domain (familiar variable names/content).

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

- **Scenario:** The key message is an interaction and you want that to dominate the narrative.
- **Reason:** Line graphs strongly pull descriptions toward x–y interaction framing [@shahBarLineGraph2011].

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

- **The Sacrifice:** Interactions may be less likely to be the primary takeaway than in line graphs.
- **The Risk:** Users may focus on category-level comparisons and miss nuanced interaction patterns unless prompted [@shahBarLineGraph2011].

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **The Wrong Fix:** Using a line graph and assuming viewers will still compute and state main effects.
- **Why it fails:** In open-ended summaries, the salient line-based interaction can “use up” the limited reporting space/attention, reducing main-effect reporting [@shahBarLineGraph2011].

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

- **Visual Sign:** Summaries rarely contain statements like “overall, y decreases as x increases” or “overall, group A is higher than group B.”
- **The Test:** Run a brief write-a-summary test; if main effects are absent, your format may be discouraging them [@shahBarLineGraph2011].

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

- **Quick Fix:** Add explicit prompts that ask viewers to report overall differences across x or across groups.
- **Best Fix:** Re-encode as a bar graph when main effects are a central intended inference [@shahBarLineGraph2011].
