## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->
Audit your visual metaphors and aesthetic choices (colors, shapes, fonts) to ensure they do not inadvertently trigger cultural associations ("connotations") that contradict the data's meaning.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->
Visualizations operate through both denotation (literal meaning) and connotation (implied meaning). A design choice, such as a "jagged" line graph styled to look like "dancing," triggers cultural viewing codes. If the topic is serious (e.g., political accuracy) but the visual metaphor implies frivolity (e.g., "pole dancing"), the user's interpretation of the data's value will be skewed by these extra-representational associations [@hullman_visualization_2011].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->
*   **User Goal:** Communicating a narrative tone (e.g., serious, playful, urgent).
*   **Data Type:** Thematic data (politics, health, entertainment) where cultural context is heavy.
*   **Audience:** General public with shared cultural references and viewing codes.

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->
*   **Scenario:** Satire or ironic visualization.
*   **Reason:** If the explicit goal is to use irony (a "linguistic-based rhetoric" technique) to mock the data or the subject, conflicting metaphors are appropriate.

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->
*   **The Sacrifice:** Creativity or visual novelty.
*   **The Risk:** "Safe" standard charts (bar/line) minimize risk of misinterpretation but may lower engagement compared to novel metaphors.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->
*   **The Wrong Fix:** Using "visual noise" or "decoration" just to make a chart look interesting.
*   **Why it fails:** It may introduce "gratuitous" dimensions or associations that obscure the data or imply false causality [@hullman_visualization_2011].

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->
*   **Visual Sign:** Ask, "What does this shape remind me of outside of this chart?"
*   **The Test:** Show the graphic to a user without the title/labels. Ask them what the "vibe" or "feeling" of the image is (e.g., chaotic, serious, fun).

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->
*   **Quick Fix:** Remove decorative elements or non-standard shapes; revert to standard geometric encodings.
*   **Best Fix:** Choose a visual metaphor that reinforces the data narrative (e.g., using red/blue for political parties, but avoiding "playful" shapes for serious statistics).
