## Use a point → emphasis → explanation workflow for every chart <!-- role: advice -->

Create charts by first deciding the single point you want readers to remember, then adjusting the visual design to support that point, and finally adding the text elements that precisely explain what the chart shows.

## Why a point-first workflow improves chart clarity <!-- role: reason -->

A chart becomes easier to understand when readers are given an explicit claim to look for, then visual cues that help them find supporting evidence, and finally definitions that remove ambiguity about measures, time frames, and series meaning.

**Mechanism:** Stating the point reduces reader search, emphasis directs attention to the most relevant evidence, and precise framing text (description/labels/source) prevents misinterpretation and missing context.

**Evidence:** A practical workflow that starts with a specific point, uses comparison/color/annotation to emphasize it, and ends with precise explanatory text leads to charts that better communicate what matters and what exactly is being shown [@muth_better_charts_2017].

**Notes:** The steps are interdependent: changing the point typically changes the comparison data, the emphasis techniques, and the required explanations.

## When to use the point → emphasis → explanation workflow <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand the main takeaway of a chart quickly and remember it after reading.
- **Task:** Interpret a chart as evidence for a specific statement within an article, report, or presentation.
- **Data:** Any dataset where multiple valid views or chart forms are possible, especially when comparisons are needed to support a claim.
- **Chart Setting:** Static or lightly interactive charts embedded in editorial or explanatory contexts.
- **Audience:** Readers seeing the chart for the first time who did not participate in the underlying analysis.
- **Success Criterion:** The chart’s takeaway is unambiguous, and the reader can accurately describe what the data represents.

## When not to follow it <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** You are doing early exploratory analysis and do not yet have a defensible point to communicate. **Why:** Committing to a point too soon can constrain discovery and lead to premature framing.

## Tradeoffs and risks <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Time spent iterating between steps and revising the chart as the point evolves. **Risk:** If the point is oversimplified, the emphasis choices can hide relevant nuance. **Mitigation:** Keep the point specific but check that the chart still allows readers to notice additional relevant patterns.

## Common ways this fails in practice <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Starting by polishing a chart before deciding what it should say. **Why it fails:** The design can end up emphasizing the wrong feature or no feature at all.
- **Mistake:** Stopping after visual emphasis and skipping the explanatory text elements. **Why it fails:** Readers may not know what units, timeframe, categories, or series definitions the marks represent.

## Quick tests <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** A reader asks “So what?” or “What am I looking at?” after viewing the chart. **Quick Check:** Hide the body text and ask someone to summarize the chart’s point in one sentence; if they can’t, step 1 is missing or unclear. **Stronger Test:** Ask a reader to restate what the measures and timeframe are; if they guess incorrectly, step 3 is insufficient.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Write a one-sentence takeaway and use it as the draft headline before choosing comparison data or styling.
- Add comparison data that directly supports the intended claim rather than adding series opportunistically.
- Use color and annotations to spotlight only the elements that support the takeaway, and de-emphasize the rest.
- Add a precise description, clear labeling/keys, and a source so readers can interpret the chart without prior context.
