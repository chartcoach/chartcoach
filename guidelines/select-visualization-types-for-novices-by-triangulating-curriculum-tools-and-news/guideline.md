## Select visualization types for novices by triangulating curriculum, tools, and news exposure <!-- role: advice -->

When targeting non-expert adults, select visualization types that appear in at least two of these sources: K–12 curricula, common visualization authoring tools, and news media.

## Why exposure-based selection improves representativeness <!-- role: reason -->

For non-expert populations, representativeness is tied to what people are likely to have learned formally and encountered informally. Triangulating sources reduces the chance that the instrument over-focuses on either school-taught charts or niche professional charts, improving relevance to everyday visualization consumption.

**Mechanism:** Selecting visualization types based on multiple exposure channels increases the likelihood that items measure literacy rather than unfamiliarity with rare chart forms.

**Evidence:** Visualization types for a literacy assessment were selected by surveying K–12 standards, visualization authoring tools, and news outlets, then choosing types present in at least two sources, yielding a set of 12 visualization types [@leeVLATDevelopmentVisualization2017].

**Notes:** This is a selection heuristic for non-experts; expert-focused instruments may need different sources.

## When you are choosing chart types for a general-population assessment <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Build a test or curriculum aligned with what non-experts commonly see and have learned.
- **Task:** Decide which visualization types to include as primary content.
- **Data:** Common datasets used in public communication (temporal, categorical, multivariate, geographic, hierarchical).
- **Chart Setting:** General literacy assessment; public-facing training.
- **Audience:** Adults without specialized visualization expertise.
- **Success Criterion:** Included chart types are defensible as representative of typical exposure.

## When not to use exposure as the primary criterion <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The goal is to assess readiness for specialized analytic work (e.g., domain-specific visual analytics). **Why:** Common exposure under-samples specialized charts that matter for those contexts.

## Tradeoffs of triangulation selection <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Some advanced or emerging visualization types may be excluded. **Risk:** The set may lag behind current practice if sources are outdated. **Mitigation:** Periodically re-run the source surveys and update inclusion thresholds.

## Common selection mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Including a visualization type solely because it is popular in tools. **Why it fails:** Tool availability does not imply general-public exposure or comprehension.

## Quick checks for appropriate selection <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Many test takers fail items because they do not recognize the chart form rather than misreading it. **Quick Check:** For each chart type, verify it is supported by at least two exposure sources relevant to your audience. **Stronger Test:** Run a small pilot to detect chart-type unfamiliarity effects before finalizing the set.

## What to do if key charts are missing or unfamiliarity dominates <!-- role: fix -->

- Add a short pretest familiarity check and remove chart types that are unfamiliar for the target population.
- Swap in more commonly encountered alternatives that support the same target tasks.
- Split the instrument into “core common charts” and “advanced charts” forms.
- Update the source lists (curriculum/tools/media) and re-apply the inclusion rule.
