## Skip severity graphics when prioritizing gist understanding <!-- role: advice -->

Avoid adding a separate risk-severity graphic alongside probability information when your primary goal is improving gist understanding of which option is riskier or more beneficial. If you include severity cues, evaluate whether they distract from the probability comparison task.

## Why severity graphics can hurt gist comprehension <!-- role: reason -->

A second visual encoding for severity can pull attention away from the core probability comparison, increasing cognitive load and changing what information feels most salient when people form an overall impression.

**Mechanism:** Adding an additional salient visual dimension introduces competing signals that can shift attention and alter perceived risk/benefit, even if it does not improve numeric recall.

**Evidence:** In the randomized study, adding risk-severity graphics reduced the proportion of participants with adequate gist understanding compared with presentations without severity graphics [@taitEffectFormatParents2010]. Severity graphics also increased perceived risk, decreased perceived benefit, and reduced stated willingness to enroll compared with no-severity presentations, while not improving verbatim understanding [@taitEffectFormatParents2010].

**Notes:** The severity graphic was presented as a color-and-size scale (mild to severe) with an indicator showing the outcome’s severity category.

## When this warning applies <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Determine which arm has higher/lower risks and higher/lower benefits.
- **Task:** Gist comparison across multiple outcomes (directional judgments).
- **Data:** Probabilities plus qualitative severity categories for outcomes.
- **Chart Setting:** Consent or decision materials where multiple outcomes are shown and users must integrate comparisons.
- **Audience:** Lay decision-makers who may be sensitive to emotionally salient cues.
- **Success Criterion:** Higher adequate gist understanding without unintentionally shifting perceptions of risk/benefit.

## When to include severity graphics anyway <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The decision requires explicitly weighing severity more than probability and you can test that users still correctly understand which arm has higher/lower probabilities. **Why:** The added salience may be appropriate if severity is the dominant decision attribute.

## Tradeoffs of omitting severity cues <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Readers may have less support for distinguishing “common mild” versus “rare severe” outcomes. **Risk:** Probability-focused displays can encourage readers to overweight frequency and underweight severity. **Mitigation:** Track both comprehension and perception outcomes when choosing whether to add severity elements.

## Common severity-graphic mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Adding a severity scale as an extra visual without validating its effect on gist comprehension. **Why it fails:** In the tested context, severity graphics decreased adequate gist understanding and shifted risk/benefit perceptions [@taitEffectFormatParents2010].

## Quick checks for severity-graphic side effects <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Users correctly recall numbers but increasingly choose the wrong arm as “more likely” or “less likely” for outcomes. **Quick Check:** Compare gist-question accuracy with and without the severity graphic in a small pilot. **Stronger Test:** Measure both gist accuracy and perceived risk/benefit ratings to detect comprehension-perception tradeoffs.

## What to do instead of a severity graphic <!-- role: fix -->

- Describe severity with brief standardized words (e.g., mild vs serious) adjacent to the outcome label without a separate visual scale.
- Separate severity information onto a different screen or section so it does not compete with probability comparisons.
- Reduce visual emphasis of severity cues so probability information remains the most salient element in the comparison view.
- Test comprehension with gist questions that specifically check the intended comparative takeaway.
