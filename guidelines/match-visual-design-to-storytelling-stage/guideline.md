## Align the visualization with whether the message is fixed or still evolving <!-- role: advice -->

Design narrative-first when you already know the takeaway, and design data-driven when the takeaway may change as you analyze. Keep the visual emphasis, annotation, and structure consistent with that stage.

## Why narrative-first and exploratory workflows require different design choices <!-- role: reason -->

Narrative-first work optimizes comprehension and persuasion by reinforcing a preselected message, while exploratory work optimizes discovery by keeping encodings and framing flexible enough to accommodate new patterns. Mixing these goals without acknowledging the stage creates a mismatch between what the graphic signals (certainty and conclusion) and what the process requires (revisability and iteration).

**Mechanism:** Viewers infer intent from structure and emphasis; heavy framing and annotation imply a settled conclusion, while lighter framing and flexible views imply ongoing inquiry.

**Evidence:** In newsroom practice, teams commonly follow either a narrative-first workflow where the message determines the visual, or a data-driven workflow where the visual evolves through continued analysis; hybrid approaches are used when balancing hypotheses with exploration [@gregory_data_2024].

**Notes:** Hybrid workflows work best when it is explicit which parts are provisional exploration and which parts are finalized narrative.

## Situations where workflow-stage alignment matters most <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand and remember a specific takeaway, or investigate data to find what matters.
- **Task:** Explain, justify, or persuade versus explore, hypothesize, and refine questions.
- **Data:** Newly collected, uncertain, or rapidly changing data versus stable, well-understood, and vetted data.
- **Chart Setting:** Editorial/story format with limited interaction versus analysis environment with iteration, filtering, and multiple views.
- **Audience:** Broad public needing clear conclusions versus analysts/domain experts comfortable with ambiguity and iteration.
- **Success Criterion:** Narrative: consistent takeaway and low misinterpretation; Exploratory: speed of insight generation and ease of revising the message.

## When to intentionally deviate from stage-matched design <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** You must publish before analysis is fully settled but still need to communicate uncertainty. **Why:** A purely narrative-first design can overstate confidence, while a purely exploratory design can under-serve decision-making.

## Tradeoffs of committing to one stage too strongly <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Narrative-first commitment reduces flexibility to incorporate new findings without redesign, while exploratory commitment reduces rhetorical focus and memorability. **Risk:** Readers may infer the wrong level of certainty, either treating a tentative view as a conclusion or dismissing a final message as mere exploration. **Mitigation:** Make the stage explicit through framing choices such as headline language, annotation tone, and inclusion of uncertainty cues.

## Common ways this goes wrong in practice <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Presenting exploratory, provisional results with definitive narrative framing. **Why it fails:** The design implies the conclusion is settled, discouraging scrutiny and masking uncertainty.
- **Mistake:** Using an exploratory multi-view “dashboard-like” layout for a single clear takeaway. **Why it fails:** The main message competes with optional paths, weakening comprehension and recall.
- **Mistake:** Switching between narrative and exploratory cues within the same figure without signaling the shift. **Why it fails:** The reader cannot tell which elements are evidence, hypothesis, or final claim.

## Quick ways to check whether your design matches your stage <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Readers ask “So what is the point?” in a narrative piece, or they treat a tentative pattern as the conclusion in an exploratory piece. **Quick Check:** Remove the headline and annotations—if the chart still strongly asserts one takeaway, it is narrative-first; if it mostly supports comparison and questioning, it is exploratory. **Stronger Test:** Ask a test reader what level of certainty the graphic implies and compare it to your actual analysis status.

## Practical adjustments to restore stage alignment <!-- role: fix -->

- Add or remove framing so the implied certainty matches reality: strengthen the single takeaway for narrative-first work or soften conclusions for exploratory work.
- Separate exploration from explanation by splitting into two deliverables: an exploratory view for analysis and a distilled narrative view for communication.
- Reconfigure the layout to match intent: single focal chart with supportive annotations for narrative-first, or multiple coordinated views/filters for exploration.
- If you must hybridize, label what is confirmed versus provisional and visually de-emphasize tentative elements so they read as exploratory rather than final.
