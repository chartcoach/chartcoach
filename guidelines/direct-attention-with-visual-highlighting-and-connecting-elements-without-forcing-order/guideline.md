## Use highlighting and connectors to suggest the reading path <!-- role: advice -->

Use visual highlighting and connecting elements such as color emphasis, size, bold text, arrows, and shaded trails to suggest where to look next. Let these cues guide exploration while still allowing readers to choose their own order when the format is not strictly linear.

## Salience cues create narrative flow in space <!-- role: reason -->

Partitioned layouts and posters do not inherently impose a reading order. Attention-guidance devices create an implied path by making some elements visually dominant and by linking related components across the display.

**Mechanism:** Differences in salience and explicit connectors reduce ambiguity about focal points and relationships, producing a perceivable flow even without strict sequencing.

**Evidence:** Case analysis describes narrative paths formed through visual highlighting and connecting elements (arrows, shaded trails) that guide attention across sections without dictating a fixed exploration order [@segelNarrativeVisualizationTelling2010].

**Notes:** Highlighting works best when reinforced by consistent semantics so the viewer can understand what the highlighted elements represent.

## Where implied paths are most useful <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand a multi-part story presented in one screen or poster-like layout.
- **Task:** Follow relationships between sections (overview to inset, comparison to detail).
- **Data:** Dense graphics where many elements compete for attention.
- **Chart Setting:** Partitioned posters, multi-panel graphics, and flow-chart-like narratives.
- **Audience:** Readers scanning quickly who need cues on what matters.
- **Success Criterion:** Viewers reliably start at the intended entry point and notice the key comparisons.

## When not to rely on implied paths <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The narrative requires a strict sequence where later frames depend on earlier explanations. **Why:** Suggested flow can be ignored, leading to misunderstanding.

## Tradeoffs of attention guidance <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Some visual neutrality; emphasis can make parts feel less important even if they are necessary context. **Risk:** Over-highlighting can become visual noise and reduce the effectiveness of salience. **Mitigation:** Reserve the strongest emphasis for the key narrative beats.

## Common failures of attention cues <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Highlighting too many elements at once. **Why it fails:** Nothing stands out, and the viewer cannot infer priority.
- **Mistake:** Using connectors (like arrows) that do not correspond to a meaningful relationship. **Why it fails:** Viewers follow the cue but arrive at an incoherent transition.

## Checks for narrative flow in a spatial layout <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Readers scan randomly and miss the intended key points. **Quick Check:** Blur-squint test: the intended entry point and primary path should remain visually dominant. **Stronger Test:** Ask viewers to describe the story in order after a short viewing; compare their path to the intended one.

## Fixes when viewers don’t follow the intended flow <!-- role: fix -->

- Increase contrast of the primary entry point using size, color, or bold titling.
- Add explicit connectors (arrows, trails, callouts) between the overview and the referenced detail.
- Reduce competing emphasis by de-saturating or simplifying secondary sections.
- Switch to a slideshow format when a strict sequence is required.
