## Use meaning-bearing icons and styling, not decoration <!-- role: advice -->

Include icons, illustrations, textures, and extra color only when they communicate data, categories, direction, or essential context. If an element does not change what a reader can correctly infer, remove it.

## Decorative elements add noise and create uneven interpretation <!-- role: reason -->

Extra visual elements compete with the data for attention, increasing cognitive load and making the intended reading less consistent across viewers. When visuals are tightly coupled to meaning, they can support recognition and engagement; when they are merely ornamental, they create ambiguity and irritation that undermines comprehension.

**Mechanism:** Meaningful visual cues can act as compact labels or grouping signals, but decorative cues create attentional distraction and invite readers to infer patterns that are not in the data.

**Evidence:** Viewers evaluating a bar chart with multiple icons and colors reported mixed reactions—some found the elements helpful, while others found them irritating or “too much,” indicating that ornamental embellishment can increase confusion and variability in interpretation [@saske_multidimensional_2025]. Practitioners report using particular visual styles to either reduce intimidation for less confident readers or, conversely, finding certain styles off-putting, showing that aesthetic choices can shift who feels included and how content is received [@schuster_who_2023]. Purposeful aesthetic craft (form, color, illustrative formats) can increase emotional connection and engagement when it is aligned with the message rather than added as ornament [@gregory_data_2024].

**Notes:** “Relevant” can include context-setting visuals if they directly support the intended takeaway and do not obscure quantitative reading.

## When charts are at risk of feeling “busy” or ambiguous <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand the data quickly and accurately, or compare values/categories without distraction.
- **Task:** Compare, rank, detect change, or extract a takeaway from a static or lightly annotated view.
- **Data:** Quantitative or categorical data where decoding relies on position/length/area and label clarity.
- **Chart Setting:** Reports, dashboards, presentations, or social graphics where space is limited and attention is split.
- **Audience:** Mixed literacy audiences, including viewers who may react differently to stylistic flourishes.
- **Success Criterion:** Consistent interpretation across viewers, minimal confusion, and preserved trust in what is data versus decoration.

## When attention-getting aesthetics are essential to reach the audience <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The primary barrier is that the audience will not engage at all without a strong aesthetic hook, and the added elements are carefully integrated to reinforce the message rather than compete with the data. **Why:** In some communication contexts, deliberate aesthetic treatment can increase approachability and emotional connection, improving the chance the viewer will absorb the intended takeaway.

## What you give up by stripping decoration <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** You may lose some personality, brand expressiveness, or “poster-like” appeal that draws initial attention. **Risk:** Over-simplifying can make the graphic feel sterile, reducing memorability or emotional resonance. **Mitigation:** Preserve engagement with restrained, message-aligned choices (e.g., one consistent motif or a single accent color) that do not create competing encodings.

## How this goes wrong in practice <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Adding icons, flags, textures, and multiple colors as “flair” while the chart already encodes the needed distinctions. **Why it fails:** Readers must parse extra signals that do not change the inference, increasing noise and inconsistent interpretations.

## Quick ways to tell if visuals are decorative <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Removing the icons/ornamentation leaves the data interpretation unchanged, or different viewers disagree on what the visuals mean. **Quick Check:** For each non-data element, state in one phrase what new information it encodes; if you cannot, it is decoration. **Stronger Test:** Run a fast two-version review (with/without the elements) and ask readers to summarize the takeaway; keep only elements that improve accuracy or reduce misreadings.

## Safer alternatives to decoration <!-- role: fix -->

- Replace decorative icons with direct labels, short annotations, or a small legend only where it reduces ambiguity.
- Use one consistent visual motif only when it encodes a real category or status that is also represented textually.
- Move engagement into a meaningful headline/subtitle that states the point, instead of adding non-informative graphics.
- If you need approachability, use restrained illustration or style in the surrounding layout while keeping the data region visually clean.
