## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

Do not mix numeric risk formats (percentages, odds, and frequencies) within the same communication; pick one and stick to it.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

Switching formats increases cognitive effort and invites conversion errors, undermining accurate comparisons and comprehension.

- **The Principle:** Consistency reduces translation and conversion errors
- **The Evidence:** [@lipkusNumericVerbalVisual2007]

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Compare two risks, evaluate benefit vs harm, or understand baseline vs treated risk
- **Data Type:** Probabilities expressed numerically
- **Audience:** Patients and the general public

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

- **Scenario:** You provide a single “translation” once (e.g., “10% (10 out of 100)”) to teach equivalence.
- **Reason:** A one-time, explicit conversion can increase understanding without forcing computation. [@lipkusNumericVerbalVisual2007]

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

- **The Sacrifice:** You may lose a format preferred by part of your audience.
- **The Risk:** If you choose an uncommon format (e.g., odds), comprehension may drop.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **The Wrong Fix:** Showing baseline as “1 in 1,000” and treatment risk as “0.05%.”
- **Why it fails:** Readers must convert mentally before they can compare. [@lipkusNumericVerbalVisual2007]

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

- **Visual Sign:** The same figure contains both “%” and “1 in X” (or odds) for comparable values.
- **The Test:** Circle every probability—are they all in the same unit?

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

- **Quick Fix:** Convert all probabilities to one format (all % or all “out of N”).
- **Best Fix:** Use one format plus a single parenthetical translation on first mention, then remain consistent. [@lipkusNumericVerbalVisual2007]
