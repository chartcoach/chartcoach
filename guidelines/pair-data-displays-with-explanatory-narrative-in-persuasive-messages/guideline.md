## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

Do not show data alone—wrap each data display in a short context paragraph and an explicit evidence statement that tells readers what relationship the data supports.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

The study’s persuasive messages were designed with three components—(a) context to introduce the evidence, (b) an evidence statement (often describing trends like “X increased while Y decreased”), and (c) the supporting data shown as either charts or tables. The observed persuasion differences (charts vs tables, moderated by initial attitude) were measured using this message structure.

- **The Principle:** Persuasion in data-driven messages relies on interpreted evidence, not raw data alone.
- **The Evidence:** [@pandeyPersuasivePowerData2014]

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand what the evidence is supposed to imply and update attitude accordingly.
- **Data Type:** Statistical evidence used to support cause-and-effect style claims (as in the paper’s topics).
- **Audience:** General readers who may not infer the intended claim from the display without guidance. [@pandeyPersuasivePowerData2014]

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

- **Scenario:** Your goal is open-ended exploration rather than persuasion toward a specific claim.
- **Reason:** The rule is derived from “explicit intent to persuade through a carefully crafted message”; in exploratory settings, prescribing an evidence statement may be inappropriate. [@pandeyPersuasivePowerData2014]

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

- **The Sacrifice:** More space and more reading time per figure/table.
- **The Risk:** Overly strong wording can trigger skepticism about “cherry-picked” data (a common reason participants gave for not changing their opinion). [@pandeyPersuasivePowerData2014]

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **The Wrong Fix:** Adding a chart/table without stating the specific takeaway it supports.
- **Why it fails:** Readers may question the logic (“correlation does not imply causation”) or miss the intended inference, reducing attitude change. [@pandeyPersuasivePowerData2014]

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

- **Visual Sign:** Readers can repeat numbers but cannot articulate what the evidence is meant to show.
- **The Test:** Ask an attention/comprehension question per evidence block (as the study did) to see whether readers can correctly answer what the evidence implies. [@pandeyPersuasivePowerData2014]

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

- **Quick Fix:** Add one sentence before each display: “This evidence suggests …” followed by the intended inference.
- **Best Fix:** Rewrite each evidence block into the three-part structure used in the study (context → evidence statement → displayed data) and test pre/post attitude change. [@pandeyPersuasivePowerData2014]
