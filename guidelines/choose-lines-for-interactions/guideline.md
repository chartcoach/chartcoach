## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->
Use line graphs when you want the viewer to focus specifically on the interaction between the x-axis variable and the y-axis variable (the "x–y interaction").

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->
Line graphs utilize the Gestalt principle of **good continuity** to create visual chunks formed by the lines connecting data points. This makes the slope of the line—and differences in slope between lines—highly salient. According to [@shah_bar_2011], viewers are significantly more likely to describe interactions (how the relationship changes) when viewing line graphs because the format visually highlights these trends and ignores the categorical groupings found in bar charts.

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->
*   **User Goal:** The viewer needs to see how a trend accelerates, decelerates, or reverses compared to another group (e.g., "Reading time decreases faster for low-skill readers than high-skill readers").
*   **Data Type:** Multivariate data (three variables) where the x-axis represents an ordinal or continuous variable (e.g., time, intensity, frequency).
*   **Audience:** Users who need to identify complex relationships rather than retrieve specific values.

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->
*   **Scenario:** When the viewer needs to estimate "main effects" (averages across conditions) or compare discrete values.
*   **Reason:** The interaction in a line graph is so visually salient that it interferes with the ability to mentally average data points or process the variables independently [@shah_bar_2011].

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->
*   **The Sacrifice:** You lose the ability to easily see the "z–y interaction" (the effect of the legend variable within a specific x-axis category) because the data points are not grouped by proximity as they are in bar charts.
*   **The Risk:** Novice viewers may describe the data purely as continuous trends even if the x-axis is categorical, potentially misunderstanding the discrete nature of the data.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->
*   **The Wrong Fix:** Using a line graph for nominal/categorical data just to save space.
*   **Why it fails:** Viewers will interpret the connecting lines as a trend or relationship that doesn't exist (e.g., "The more 'Male' a person is, the taller they are") [@shah_bar_2011].

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->
*   **Visual Sign:** Look at the graph. Are the crossing lines or differing slopes the very first thing you notice?
*   **The Test:** Ask a viewer to describe the "main point" of the graph. If they describe a trend ("As X increases, Y increases..."), the line graph is working.

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->
*   **Quick Fix:** If the interaction is not the point, switch to a bar chart.
*   **Best Fix:** Use a line graph only when the interaction (the difference in slopes) is the central story you want to tell.
