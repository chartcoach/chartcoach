## Use the headline to state the chart’s point as a takeaway sentence <!-- role: advice -->

Write a headline that explicitly states the main point the chart should prove, phrased as the one thing readers should remember after viewing it.

## Why takeaway headlines help readers read charts faster <!-- role: reason -->

A takeaway headline reduces the cognitive work of figuring out what matters by telling readers where to focus, and it improves recall by putting the central statement into words.

**Mechanism:** A specific headline acts as a viewing instruction and memory cue, guiding attention toward the evidence in the chart instead of forcing open-ended scanning.

**Evidence:** Using the headline as a clear hypothesis of what the chart shows helps readers know what to pay attention to and remember the central statement better [@muth_better_charts_2017].

**Notes:** The headline should align with what the chart can actually demonstrate; if it doesn’t, revise the point or the chart.

## When to use takeaway headlines <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Quickly grasp what’s important in the chart without reading surrounding text.
- **Task:** Identify the intended interpretation (the claim) and then verify it against the plotted evidence.
- **Data:** Any chart with multiple potential insights, especially time series or multi-series comparisons.
- **Chart Setting:** Editorial charts, reports, dashboards used for communication (not just exploration).
- **Audience:** Mixed literacy audiences who benefit from explicit framing.
- **Success Criterion:** Readers can correctly articulate the intended point after reading only the headline and viewing the chart.

## When not to follow it <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The communication goal is purely descriptive cataloging (e.g., a neutral index of values without a claim). **Why:** A forced “point” headline can mislead by implying a conclusion that isn’t intended.

## Tradeoffs and risks <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Some nuance and completeness in the headline. **Risk:** A headline can become opinionated or overstated relative to the evidence shown. **Mitigation:** Treat the headline as a testable statement and ensure the chart provides visible support for it.

## Common ways this fails in practice <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Using a generic title that only names the metric (e.g., “iPhone sales”). **Why it fails:** Readers must infer the point themselves and may focus on the wrong feature.
- **Mistake:** Writing a headline that promises an insight the chart doesn’t actually show. **Why it fails:** Readers can’t verify the claim from the visual evidence and lose trust.

## Quick tests <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Different readers extract different “main points” from the same chart. **Quick Check:** Ask a colleague to read only the headline and say what they expect to see; if their expectation is vague, the headline is too vague. **Stronger Test:** Ask whether the chart provides visible evidence for the headline without additional narration; if not, the headline-chart alignment is weak.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Rewrite the headline as a single sentence that includes a comparison or direction (what changed, who differs, what is largest).
- If you can’t write a specific headline, revisit the purpose of the chart and decide what it is meant to prove.
- Adjust the data shown (especially comparisons) so the chart can actually support the headline statement.
- Move secondary insights into annotations instead of cramming them into the headline.
