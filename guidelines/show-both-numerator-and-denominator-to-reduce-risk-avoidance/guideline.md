## Include the full at-risk population, not only the affected cases <!-- role: advice -->

When graphing risks as counts or frequencies, display both the number affected and the number not affected so the viewer can see the risk in the context of the whole population at risk.

## Why numerator-only displays inflate perceived risk and avoidance <!-- role: reason -->

Risk graphics can shift attention toward the foreground (affected cases) or include the background (everyone at risk). Foreground-only views make the adverse outcome more salient and can increase avoidance behaviors even when the numeric risk is unchanged.

**Mechanism:** Foreground salience increases perceived magnitude and drives more risk-avoidant choices; adding the background reinstates the reference class and can attenuate that effect.

**Evidence:** Displays that emphasize only the numerator (foreground) increase risk-avoidant behaviors such as willingness to pay, while displays that show both numerator and denominator reduce risk-avoidant behavior. [@lipkusNumericVerbalVisual2007]

**Notes:** This guideline addresses behavioral bias and salience; it does not guarantee more precise numeric estimates than numeric-only formats.

## When this is most applicable <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Decide between options (treatments, products, preventive actions) based on risk.
- **Task:** Compare frequencies across options; assess how big the risk “really is.”
- **Data:** Frequencies such as “X out of N” or similar incidence summaries.
- **Chart Setting:** Patient decision aids, public health risk comparisons, side-effect communication.
- **Audience:** General public; especially those prone to denominator neglect.
- **Success Criterion:** Choices are less driven by salience of affected cases and more by the intended magnitude.

## When not to follow it <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The only ethical and communicative goal is to maximize vigilance for a rare but critical hazard. **Why:** Foreground-only emphasis can be intentionally persuasive.

## Tradeoffs and risks <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Showing the full denominator can take more space and may feel less emotionally compelling. **Risk:** Viewers may downplay a serious risk if the background dominates attention. **Mitigation:** Add a clear verbal statement that reinforces the intended level of concern.

## Common failure modes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Showing only affected icons or only a “benefit” bar without the remainder. **Why it fails:** It strips away the reference class and amplifies perceived risk.

## Quick tests <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Viewers describe the outcome as common even when the denominator is large. **Quick Check:** Confirm the graphic makes the denominator visually present and legible. **Stronger Test:** Compare willingness-to-pay or preference patterns between numerator-only and full-population versions in a small A/B test.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Use stacked bars that include both affected and unaffected portions.
- Use icon arrays that show the whole matrix with affected icons highlighted.
- Add a label stating the denominator explicitly (e.g., “out of 5,000 people”).
- Pair the display with a short explanatory sentence clarifying what the unaffected portion represents.
