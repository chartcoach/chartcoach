## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

Include a diverse group of viewers in your design and testing process to capture different perspectives. Do not rely solely on homogeneous groups or convenience samples.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

Homogeneous groups often share the same background knowledge and blind spots, leading to "echo chamber" validation where critical usability issues are missed. By expanding the participant pool, you surface insights that are invisible to experts or specific demographic cohorts.

*   **The Principle:** **Demographic Gap Analysis.** diverse audiences (varying in age, education, and visual literacy) interact with information differently.
*   **The Evidence:** Workshops and interviews including people aged 75+, students, and those with non-academic degrees surfaced insights that would have been missed in a homogeneous group [@knoll_gulf_2025]. Furthermore, a representative survey of ages 18–74 successfully gathered hundreds of constructive critiques on climate visualizations, proving that broad audiences can provide actionable feedback [@saske_multidimensional_2025].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

Apply this whenever the visualization is intended for public consumption or a mixed-expertise audience.

*   **User Goal:** ensuring the visualization is interpreted correctly by the widest possible audience.
*   **Audience:** The general public, encompassing wide age ranges (e.g., 18–74+) and varying educational backgrounds.
*   **Process Stage:** During the co-design phase and final usability testing.

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

*   **Scenario:** Designing specialized dashboards for a highly specific, expert user base (e.g., a monitoring tool for air traffic controllers or neurosurgeons).
*   **Reason:** Feedback from laypeople may be irrelevant or distracting because they lack the domain knowledge required to use the tool in a real-world context.

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

*   **The Sacrifice:** Recruitment time and complexity. It is significantly harder to find a representative sample than it is to ask colleagues for feedback.
*   **The Risk:** You may receive conflicting feedback (e.g., novices requesting over-simplification vs. experts requesting more density), requiring careful synthesis and prioritization.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

*   **The Wrong Fix:** "Hallway Testing" (asking only immediate colleagues or designers).
*   **Why it fails:** Colleagues usually possess high visual literacy and domain context, masking the struggles a true layperson would face.
*   **The Wrong Fix:** Relying exclusively on university students as test subjects.
*   **Why it fails:** While convenient, students represent a narrow slice of age and education demographics and may not reflect the visual literacy of older adults or non-academic populations.

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

*   **Visual Sign:** Review your participant metadata table.
*   **The Test:** Look at the demographics of your testers. If 90% represent a single age bracket, education level, or job title, you have failed this rule.

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

*   **Quick Fix:** Step outside your department. Ask administrative staff, family members outside your field, or people in a different age bracket to explain the chart to you.
*   **Best Fix:** Recruit a representative sample that mirrors your target population's age and gender split (e.g., a stratified sample of n=400+) to gather robust qualitative and quantitative critique.
