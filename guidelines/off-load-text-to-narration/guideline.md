## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->
When presenting a complex animation or dynamic visualization, deliver the explanatory words as spoken narration rather than on-screen text.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->
Humans possess separate channels for processing visual and verbal material (Dual Channel assumption), but the visual channel has limited capacity. When a user must watch an animation and read on-screen text simultaneously, the visual channel becomes overloaded (Type 1 Overload).
*   **The Mechanism:** By moving words to the auditory channel (narration), you "off-load" the visual channel. The user processes images with their eyes and words with their ears, allowing them to select and organize both streams effectively without split attention [@mayer_nine_2003].
*   **The Evidence:** This is known as the *Modality Effect*. Research shows a median effect size of 1.17 in favor of narration over on-screen text for multimedia explanations [@mayer_nine_2003].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->
*   **User Goal:** Understanding a causal system or process (e.g., "how lightning works" or "how a pump functions").
*   **Data Type:** Fast-paced animations, video, or complex interactive visualizations that require constant visual attention.
*   **Audience:** Learners or users who are unfamiliar with the content.

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->
*   **Scenario:** Technical or reference material requiring precision.
*   **Reason:** Narration is transient; if the user needs to reference specific values or formulas repeatedly, text is superior for "representational holding."
*   **Scenario:** Accessibility constraints.
*   **Reason:** Users with hearing impairments require captions (though captions should be designed to minimize visual interference).

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->
*   **The Sacrifice:** Users cannot control the reading pace as easily as they can with text (unless the narration is segmentable).
*   **The Risk:** If the narration is too fast, the auditory channel may become overloaded, though this is generally less risky than visual split-attention.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->
*   **The Wrong Fix:** Presenting both narration and identical on-screen text simultaneously.
*   **Why it fails:** This creates redundancy (Type 4 Overload), forcing the user to reconcile the two streams rather than focusing on the visualization.

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->
*   **Visual Sign:** Are there paragraphs of text appearing at the bottom of the screen while an animation plays in the center?
*   **The Test:** Mute the audio. If the visualization is unintelligible without the text, but impossible to watch *while* reading the text, the rule is broken.

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->
*   **Quick Fix:** Remove the on-screen text and record a voice-over reading it.
*   **Best Fix:** Design a script specifically for the ear that guides the eye, ensuring the narration describes the visual events exactly as they happen.
