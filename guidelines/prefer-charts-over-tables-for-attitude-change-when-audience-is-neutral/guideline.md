## Use charts (not tables) for neutral/weakly polarized audiences <!-- role: advice -->

Use charts rather than tables when your goal is to shift attitudes among people who do not already hold strong opinions on the topic. Keep the message structure the same and change only the data presentation format.

## Why charts persuade neutral audiences more than tables <!-- role: reason -->

Charts enable faster extraction of trends and comparisons than reading values sequentially in a table, which can make the evidence feel more compelling when viewers are open to updating their attitude.

**Mechanism:** Visual pattern detection supports rapid recognition of relationships in the data, which can increase the likelihood that evidence is noticed and incorporated into attitude judgments.

**Evidence:** In randomized experiments across three topics, participants with neutral/weak initial attitudes showed higher persuasion likelihood with charts than with tables (topic-level Fisher tests p < 0.05; aggregated p ≈ 0.000031) [@pandeyPersuasivePowerData2014]. Aggregated attitude change was also higher for charts than tables (mean difference ≈ 0.381 on a 7-point Likert attitude scale; p ≈ 0.0008) [@pandeyPersuasivePowerData2014].

**Notes:** Effects were observed for charted statistical evidence using bar charts and line charts compared against the same data in tabular form.

## When this applies: attitude-change messages with statistical evidence <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Decide what to believe about a debated claim after seeing evidence.
- **Task:** Update an attitude rating (pre/post exposure) based on presented evidence.
- **Data:** Small-to-moderate datasets used as evidence, typically trends and cross-group comparisons.
- **Chart Setting:** Explanatory narrative with multiple evidence blocks; same text, different data presentation (charts vs tables).
- **Audience:** People without strong prior positions (neutral or weakly polarized).
- **Success Criterion:** Higher fraction of positive attitude change and/or larger mean attitude change.

## When not to follow it: strongly negative initial attitudes <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** Your audience starts strongly opposed to the advocated position. **Why:** In the experiments, tables sometimes outperformed charts for negatively polarized participants, reversing the direction of the effect [@pandeyPersuasivePowerData2014].

## Tradeoffs of using charts for persuasion <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** More design effort and space than a compact table. **Risk:** Viewers may treat charts as rhetorical devices and respond with skepticism about manipulation. **Mitigation:** Monitor trust-related feedback and consider alternative presentation formats if skepticism is high.

## Common failure modes when using charts to persuade <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Assuming charts will increase persuasion regardless of prior attitudes. **Why it fails:** Initial attitude strongly modulated persuasion outcomes and can reverse the effect [@pandeyPersuasivePowerData2014].
- **Mistake:** Changing the narrative text along with the format switch. **Why it fails:** You lose isolation of the presentation-type effect and can’t attribute changes to charts vs tables [@pandeyPersuasivePowerData2014].

## Quick checks for this guideline <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Charts do not increase the share of positive attitude change compared with tables for the same content. **Quick Check:** Segment your audience by initial attitude (e.g., negative/neutral/positive) and compare (+) change vs no change. **Stronger Test:** Run an A/B test with identical text and data, varying only charts vs tables, and measure pre/post attitude change.

## What to do instead if charts don’t help <!-- role: fix -->

- Use tables for audiences that begin strongly opposed and compare outcomes empirically against charts.
- Segment results by initial attitude and target chart-based persuasion primarily to neutral/weakly polarized groups.
- Add a control condition with no data display to estimate whether the effect is driven by any evidence versus the visual form.
- Collect open-ended feedback to detect skepticism about data or logic and revise the evidence presentation accordingly.
