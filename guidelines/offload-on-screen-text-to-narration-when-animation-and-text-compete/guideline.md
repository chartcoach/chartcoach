## Off-load verbal information to audio during animation viewing <!-- role: advice -->

Present the verbal explanation as spoken narration rather than as on-screen text when learners must simultaneously inspect an animation.

## Why narration reduces visual-channel overload <!-- role: reason -->

When words and pictures both arrive through vision, they compete for limited visual working memory resources, leaving less capacity to select and organize the animation. Moving words to audio redistributes essential processing across separate verbal and visual channels, making it more likely that learners can build and integrate verbal and pictorial mental models.

**Mechanism:** Shifting words from the visual channel to the auditory/verbal channel reduces competition in visual working memory, freeing resources for selecting and organizing the animation.

**Evidence:** Transfer performance was higher for animation-plus-narration than for animation-plus-on-screen-text across multiple experiments, consistent with a modality effect in multimedia explanations [@mayerNineWaysReduce2003].

**Notes:** This recommendation targets overload created by essential processing demands concentrated in the visual channel.

## When animation and text compete for visual attention <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand how a system works well enough to apply it to new problems.
- **Task:** Build a causal mental model from words and dynamic visuals.
- **Data:** Procedural/causal steps with multiple elements.
- **Chart Setting:** Narrated animation or video with explanatory verbal content.
- **Audience:** Learners with limited prior knowledge of the system.
- **Success Criterion:** Higher problem-solving transfer after the lesson.

## When not to use narration-only words <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** There is no competing visual material (no animation/graphics that must be inspected while reading). **Why:** Adding on-screen text does not create the same visual competition when it is not contending with an animation for visual processing capacity.

## Tradeoffs of narration over on-screen text <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Reduced skimmability and harder quoting/searching compared with visible text. **Risk:** Learners in sound-off environments or with hearing impairments may be disadvantaged. **Mitigation:** Ensure accessibility support is available through a separate modality that does not reintroduce simultaneous visual competition.

## Common misapplications of the modality approach <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Presenting animation, narration, and identical on-screen text simultaneously. **Why it fails:** The duplicated stream can prompt extra processing to reconcile formats, consuming capacity needed for making sense of the explanation.

## Quick ways to detect visual-channel overload <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Learners must look back and forth between reading and watching, missing steps in the animation. **Quick Check:** If the learner cannot read the text without taking eyes off the relevant animation content, the design is likely visually overloaded. **Stronger Test:** Compare transfer scores for a narration version versus an on-screen-text version while holding content constant.

## Alternatives when narration is not feasible <!-- role: fix -->

- Segment the lesson so learners can alternate between reading and viewing without time pressure.
- Align printed words directly next to the part of the graphic they describe to reduce scanning.
- Remove nonessential visual elements so remaining on-screen text competes with fewer visual demands.
- Redesign the sequence so small chunks of text are shown only when the corresponding animation is not requiring attention.
