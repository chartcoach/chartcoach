## Use absolute risk to describe benefits and harms <!-- role: advice -->

When describing how an intervention changes outcomes, present the change using absolute risks (for example, “from 4% to 2%”) rather than relative risk (for example, “50% reduction”) or Number Needed to Treat (NNT) as the only format.

## Absolute risk reduces framing-driven overestimation <!-- role: reason -->

Relative risk tends to make changes look larger than they are in real terms, which can inflate perceived effectiveness and bias choices; NNT is often hard for patients to understand.

**Mechanism:** Absolute risk keeps the baseline and the outcome level visible, preventing magnitude inflation that occurs when only proportional change is shown.

**Evidence:** People perceive risk reductions as larger and view treatments more favorably when benefits are presented as relative risk rather than absolute risk, and NNT is harder for patients to understand and should not be the sole presentation format [@fagerlinHelpingPatientsDecide2011].

**Notes:** Absolute risk is especially important for preference-sensitive decisions that require trading off benefits against harms.

## When absolute-risk presentation applies <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Decide whether the benefits of an option outweigh its harms.
- **Task:** Compare options where effects are expressed as changes in probability.
- **Data:** Baseline risk and post-intervention risk for outcomes and side effects.
- **Chart Setting:** Any medium; often used in patient education and decision aids.
- **Audience:** Patients with limited numeracy; mixed familiarity with statistics.
- **Success Criterion:** Users can correctly state both baseline and resulting risks and compare options without exaggerating effect size.

## When not to follow it <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** You must communicate a proportional effect for a technical audience and can assume high statistical literacy. **Why:** Some expert settings require proportional effect measures for comparability across studies.

## Tradeoffs and risks <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Absolute risks can feel less dramatic and may require more context (baseline plus post-treatment). **Risk:** If baseline risk is omitted, even an absolute risk can be misread. **Mitigation:** Always pair the absolute change with the baseline level it comes from.

## Common failure modes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Reporting only a relative risk reduction. **Why it fails:** It can make modest absolute changes appear large and can bias decisions toward treatment.
- **Mistake:** Using NNT as the only summary statistic. **Why it fails:** Many patients do not understand NNT well enough to apply it to decisions.

## Quick tests <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Users describe benefits as “huge” when the underlying absolute change is small. **Quick Check:** Verify that every benefit statement includes both baseline risk and post-treatment risk. **Stronger Test:** Ask users to estimate how many out of 100 benefit; compare their answer to the absolute-risk statement.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- State the baseline risk and the risk with treatment as two absolute numbers in the same time window.
- If relative risk is required, include it only as a secondary detail after the absolute risks.
- Avoid NNT-only summaries; if used, pair NNT with the absolute risk change.
- Use a consistent denominator (such as “out of 100”) across options to support comparison.
