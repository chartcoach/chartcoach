## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->
Use statistical charts (such as bar charts or line charts) rather than tables when your goal is to change the attitude of an audience that does not hold strong pre-existing opinions on the topic.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->
Graphical depictions of data are more effective at influencing the attitudes of people who are "neutral" or "weakly polarized" regarding a topic.
*   **The Principle:** **Persuasion Likelihood.** According to experimental evidence, visual treatments result in a higher percentage of participants changing their attitude in the advocated direction compared to tabular treatments for this specific demographic.
*   **The Evidence:** In three separate studies covering different topics, [@pandey_persuasive_2014] found that 67.29% of neutral/weakly polarized participants were persuaded by charts, compared to only 42.68% by tables. The visual format can lead to a moderate but consistent increase in persuasion (approximately 8% improvement in attitude score).

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->
*   **User Goal:** To persuade or advocate for a specific viewpoint (e.g., "Video games do not cause violence").
*   **Audience:** Individuals who do not have strong core beliefs or prior knowledge contradicting the message (the "Neutral/Weakly Polarized" category).
*   **Data Type:** Statistical evidence supporting a cause-and-effect argument.

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->
*   **Scenario:** The audience holds strong negative views against your message.
*   **Reason:** [@pandey_persuasive_2014] found that for "Negatively Polarized" participants (those who strongly disagree initially), charts are *less* effective than tables. Charts may trigger skepticism or be viewed as "manipulated" by those who already disagree.

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->
*   **The Risk:** If you misjudge the audience and they are actually strongly opposed to the message, the chart may trigger a "backfire effect" where they scrutinize the visualization for manipulation rather than accepting the data.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->
*   **The Wrong Fix:** Providing raw data tables to a general, neutral audience to appear "scientific."
*   **Why it fails:** Tables showed significantly lower persuasion rates for neutral audiences, likely because they are less vivid or harder to process quickly for patterns than charts [@pandey_persuasive_2014].

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->
*   **Visual Sign:** Are you using a table to convince a general audience of a trend?
*   **The Test:** Survey a sample of the audience for their initial attitude. If the majority score is between -1 and +1 (on a -3 to +3 scale), ensure you are using a chart, not a table.

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->
*   **Quick Fix:** Convert the summary data from the table into a standard bar chart or line chart.
*   **Best Fix:** Create a clear visualization that explicitly highlights the trend or comparison that supports the persuasive message, ensuring the title reinforces the conclusion.
