## Treat network visualizations as high-difficulty and allocate extra literacy support <!-- role: advice -->

When presenting network visualizations to general audiences, assume they will be harder to interpret than many other visualization types and plan additional scaffolding, practice, or alternative views.

## Why networks often produce interpretation failures <!-- role: reason -->

Network layouts can be unfamiliar and require readers to infer meaning from spatial arrangements that may be algorithmic rather than directly measured. This increases the likelihood of misnaming, misreading, and misunderstanding compared with more familiar charts and maps.

**Mechanism:** Interpreting networks often requires readers to build a mental model of nodes, edges, and layout semantics, which increases cognitive effort and error relative to reference systems with clearer spatial meaning.

**Evidence:** Assessments of museum visitors show significant limitations in naming and interpreting visualizations, with particular difficulty when reading network layouts, and related work finds maps can be easier to read and more memorable than networks for relational data recall [@bornerDataVisualizationLiteracy2019].

**Notes:** Difficulty is not inevitability; it signals the need for support and careful evaluation.

## When to treat networks as high-risk for misinterpretation <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand relationships, communities, or connectivity patterns.
- **Task:** Identify clusters, key nodes, or relational structure.
- **Data:** Relational or network data.
- **Chart Setting:** Public-facing communication, education, or mixed-literacy audiences.
- **Audience:** Novices or readers unfamiliar with network conventions.
- **Success Criterion:** Readers can correctly answer basic relationship questions using the network view.

## When a network view may be acceptable without extra scaffolding <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The audience is trained in network analysis and the layout semantics are standard in that community. **Why:** Domain familiarity can reduce the interpretive burden.

## Tradeoffs of adding network scaffolding <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Additional time and space for explanation, legends, or companion views. **Risk:** Overexplaining can clutter the display and distract from the main message. **Mitigation:** Focus support on the specific tasks readers must perform.

## Common pitfalls with network communication <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Showing a dense network without explaining what node position means in the chosen layout. **Why it fails:** Readers may infer false spatial meaning from an algorithmic layout.

## Quick checks for network comprehensibility <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Readers describe the network in vague terms or misinterpret distance and grouping. **Quick Check:** Ask readers to identify a relationship (edge), a key node, and a cluster, and note confusion points. **Stronger Test:** Compare task performance on a network view versus a map-based or tabular alternative for the same relational questions.

## What to do if the network is not being understood <!-- role: fix -->

- Provide a concise explanation of what nodes and edges represent and what the layout implies.
- Reduce density by filtering, clustering, or extracting a subnetwork aligned to the insight need.
- Pair the network with a complementary representation (such as a table or map-like view) to support basic lookup and comparison.
- Use interaction (for example, search, filter, details on demand, or link-and-brush) to help readers navigate and validate interpretations.
