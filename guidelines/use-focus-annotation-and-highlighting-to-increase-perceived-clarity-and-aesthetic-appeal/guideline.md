## Add concise annotation and restrained highlighting to improve perceived clarity and aesthetics <!-- role: advice -->

Add a concise explanatory annotation and a restrained highlight color that visually links the annotation to the relevant marks to make the chart feel clearer and more visually appealing.

## Why focused designs feel clearer and more appealing <!-- role: reason -->

When viewers are told what pattern matters and can immediately see which marks support it, they experience less ambiguity about “what this is for,” which increases perceived clarity; the restrained use of a single accent color can also improve the overall look relative to heavily formatted defaults.

**Mechanism:** Annotation reduces interpretive uncertainty, and selective color creates a clear visual hierarchy that makes the chart easier to parse and more pleasant to look at.

**Evidence:** Focused designs were rated significantly higher than cluttered designs on aesthetics and professionalism and showed higher clarity than cluttered designs (trend), and focused designs were rated higher than decluttered designs on aesthetics and clarity (both trending) in simultaneous side-by-side comparisons [@ajaniDeclutterFocusEmpirically2022a].

**Notes:** Trustworthiness did not show strong evidence of improvement from focusing, and some viewers reported suspicion about “agenda” in focused charts.

## When focusing for clarity and aesthetics applies <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Make the chart’s purpose and takeaway easy to understand at a glance.
- **Task:** Rapid comprehension of “what information is being presented and why.”
- **Data:** Common presentation charts (bars, lines, stacked bars) with multiple categories/series.
- **Chart Setting:** Slides or documents where multiple designs could be compared or scanned quickly.
- **Audience:** Mixed literacy audiences who value quick interpretation.
- **Success Criterion:** Higher ratings of clarity and visual appeal for the same underlying data.

## When not to follow it <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The communication context requires strict neutrality (e.g., reporting without interpretation). **Why:** Added emphasis and annotation can be interpreted as steering the viewer toward a preferred narrative.

## Tradeoffs and risks <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Additional space for text and design time to craft annotation. **Risk:** The chart may be seen as persuasive rather than informative, potentially affecting perceived trust for some viewers. **Mitigation:** Keep the annotation factual and directly supported by the highlighted evidence.

## Common failure modes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Adding annotation that is verbose or competes with the chart for attention. **Why it fails:** It can reintroduce clutter and reduce perceived clarity.
- **Mistake:** Using highlight color without explaining what is being highlighted. **Why it fails:** Viewers may notice the emphasis but not understand the intended takeaway.

## Quick tests <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Viewers say the chart is confusing or cannot explain why certain elements are emphasized. **Quick Check:** Hide the annotation and ask whether the highlighted subset still has an obvious intended meaning; then show the annotation and confirm the meaning becomes unambiguous. **Stronger Test:** Collect 1–5 clarity and aesthetics ratings on multiple versions with the same data.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Write a short takeaway sentence that answers “what’s the point of this chart?” in plain language.
- Apply a single accent color to only the evidence marks that support the takeaway and keep other marks neutral.
- Visually connect the takeaway text to the highlighted marks through proximity or matching color.
- If the chart must remain neutral, remove the highlight and keep only minimal contextual labels that help interpretation.
