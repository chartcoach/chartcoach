## Design graphs to be interpretable for younger African American veterans <!-- role: advice -->

When your patient audience includes African American veterans under age 65, assume higher risk of difficulty with numbers and graphs and choose graph formats and explanations that minimize interpretation burden.

## Why this improves comprehension equity <!-- role: reason -->

If a subgroup has systematically lower ability to read health text, manipulate numbers, and interpret graphs, then designs that require these skills will disproportionately reduce comprehension for that subgroup and can widen communication gaps.

**Mechanism:** Lower-demand graph choices reduce reliance on inference and multi-step decoding, which is more likely to fail when graph literacy and numeracy are lower.

**Evidence:** After adjustment for multiple covariates, African American veterans under 65 scored lower than White veterans under 65 on health literacy, subjective numeracy, objective numeracy, and graph literacy [@rodriguezHealthLiteracyNumeracy2013]. The race effect was qualified by an age-by-race interaction indicating differences were most evident in those younger than 65 [@rodriguezHealthLiteracyNumeracy2013].

**Notes:** The guideline targets subgroup robustness: designs should work for the subgroup with lower measured skills, not only the average user.

## When this applies in veteran-facing materials <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand benefits/risks or other quantitative tradeoffs to participate in care decisions.
- **Task:** Read a chart, compare quantities, interpret probability or frequency information.
- **Data:** Quantitative outcomes, risks, rates, lab values, medication-related quantities.
- **Chart Setting:** Shared decision-making materials, consent discussions, clinic education materials that include charts.
- **Audience:** Mixed-race veteran populations, especially settings with many African American veterans under 65.
- **Success Criterion:** Comprehension accuracy does not drop sharply for African American veterans under 65 relative to others.

## When not to follow it <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The material is tailored to an individual patient and you have confirmed comprehension in real time. **Why:** The design can rely on interactive clarification rather than subgroup-robust standalone interpretability.

## Tradeoffs and risks <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Some nuanced or information-dense graph designs may be avoided. **Risk:** Over-simplification can omit important qualifiers or uncertainty. **Mitigation:** Keep the full detail available as optional supporting content while ensuring the main message remains interpretable.

## Common failure modes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Use the same graph-heavy materials for all veterans without checking subgroup comprehension. **Why it fails:** Younger African American veterans showed lower adjusted numeracy and graph literacy, so uniform graph demands can create unequal comprehension [@rodriguezHealthLiteracyNumeracy2013].
- **Mistake:** Assume age alone predicts lower skill and focus only on older users. **Why it fails:** After adjustment, age differences were not significant across literacy variables, while race differences (especially in younger participants) were [@rodriguezHealthLiteracyNumeracy2013].

## Quick tests <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Younger African American users ask for repeated explanation of what the chart “means” even when labels are present. **Quick Check:** In a small pilot, compare comprehension for at least one key takeaway between younger African American and younger White users. **Stronger Test:** Use the same comprehension questions across race-by-age subgroups and look for a gap in correct interpretation.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Reduce multi-step inference demands by limiting the number of encoded variables per chart in patient-facing materials.
- Add direct verbal/printed statements of the key takeaway so the decision does not depend on graph inference.
- Provide a non-graph alternative for the same quantitative message when feasible.
- Pilot test comprehension with younger African American veterans before scaling a graph-based education artifact.
