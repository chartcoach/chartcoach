## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

When your audience includes low-numeracy users, present risk/benefit probabilities with pictographs so understanding does not depend on numeracy.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

Pictographs improved adequate gist and verbatim understanding for both low- and high-numeracy parents, reducing numeracy as a barrier to using quantitative risk information.

- **The Principle:** Reduce computation/translation demands by making frequencies perceptually countable
- **The Evidence:** Across numeracy strata, pictographs produced higher adequate gist and verbatim knowledge than text/tables; low numeracy was associated with worse understanding overall, but pictographs mitigated this gap [@taitEffectFormatParents2010].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Correctly interpret probability differences without doing math
- **Data Type:** Multiple outcome probabilities (benefits and side effects), expressed as frequencies
- **Audience:** Mixed numeracy populations, especially parents/guardians in consent contexts

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

- **Scenario:** You are only allowed to provide a short, text-only disclosure.
- **Reason:** The rule requires a visual channel; the study’s benefit depended on the pictograph format being shown [@taitEffectFormatParents2010].

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

- **The Sacrifice:** Additional design effort to build consistent pictographs across outcomes
- **The Risk:** If pictographs are inconsistently encoded across outcomes (e.g., changing colors/denominators), they may create confusion; the study used consistent “out of 100” framing and consistent color meaning [@taitEffectFormatParents2010].

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **The Wrong Fix:** Keeping text/tables but adding more numeric detail (more percentages, more rows).
- **Why it fails:** Low numeracy users still underperform, and text/tables did not match pictographs on understanding in this experiment [@taitEffectFormatParents2010].

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

- **Visual Sign:** Low-numeracy users disproportionately miss verbatim questions (e.g., “how many out of 100?”).
- **The Test:** Compare correct-response rates across numeracy groups; if low numeracy users lag heavily, the display is not numeracy-robust (pictographs reduced this gap in the study) [@taitEffectFormatParents2010].

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

- **Quick Fix:** Convert percentages to “X out of 100” pictographs with colored blocks for affected vs not affected.
- **Best Fix:** Keep the denominator constant at 100 and provide a short training example of how to read the pictograph before presenting study-specific risks/benefits [@taitEffectFormatParents2010].
