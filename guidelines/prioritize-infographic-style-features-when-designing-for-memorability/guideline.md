## Use infographic-style presentation when the goal is memorability <!-- role: advice -->

When memorability is the primary objective for a static visualization, use an infographic-style presentation that increases visual distinctiveness.

## Why infographic-style visuals can be more memorable <!-- role: reason -->

Infographic-style designs tend to incorporate multiple features that increase discriminability in a rapid recognition setting, such as pictorial elements and richer use of color and non-data ink.

**Mechanism:** A more distinctive overall image reduces confusion with other visualizations and provides more cues for recognition.

**Evidence:** Visualizations sourced from infographic venues had higher memorability scores than those from government/world organizations and other sources, and the difference persisted even after removing pictogram-containing visualizations [@borkinWhatMakesVisualization2013a].

**Notes:** Source differences may bundle multiple correlated design attributes rather than isolating a single cause.

## When this applies <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Remember the visualization after exposure.
- **Task:** Recognize the visualization among many competing visuals.
- **Data:** Any.
- **Chart Setting:** Static publication; attention competition (feeds, reports, presentations).
- **Audience:** General audiences.
- **Success Criterion:** Higher memorability score in recognition-style evaluation.

## When not to emulate infographic style <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** You need to attribute any improvement to a single design element. **Why:** The source effect likely reflects multiple correlated features and does not isolate one causal styling choice [@borkinWhatMakesVisualization2013a].

## Tradeoffs and risks <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Editorial uniformity and minimalist aesthetics.\
**Risk:** The visualization becomes memorable for superficial styling rather than the intended message.\
**Mitigation:** Treat memorability testing as separate from comprehension testing.

## Common mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Copying the surface look of infographics without ensuring the visualization remains a single coherent graphic. **Why it fails:** Multiple competing elements can increase confusion rather than distinctiveness in recognition [@borkinWhatMakesVisualization2013a].

## Quick checks <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** The visualization is visually indistinguishable from other charts in the same publishing environment.\
**Quick Check:** Compare thumbnails of several visuals from the same source; if they look templated, memorability may be lower.\
**Stronger Test:** Run a recognition test comparing a baseline version to an infographic-styled version and examine both hits and false alarms.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Add a single, topic-relevant pictorial cue to increase distinctiveness without fully changing style.
- Increase distinct color variety to improve discriminability.
- Choose a more distinctive visualization type rather than relying on styling alone.
- If uniformity is required, accept lower memorability and focus evaluation on comprehension outcomes instead.
