## Avoid trust messaging that is triggered solely by low health literacy <!-- role: advice -->

Do not assume that veterans with lower health literacy trust their physicians less, and do not add trust-repair messaging solely based on health literacy level.

## Why this prevents mis-targeted trust interventions <!-- role: reason -->

If trust does not vary with health literacy, then designing trust interventions around literacy can waste attention and may distract from the main comprehension task.

**Mechanism:** Targeting trust supports to variables that actually relate to trust reduces unnecessary content and keeps materials focused on decision-relevant information.

**Evidence:** Veterans with adequate versus inadequate health literacy trusted their physicians to a similar extent, and African American and White veterans also did not differ in trust [@rodriguezHealthLiteracyNumeracy2013]. Older veterans reported higher trust in physicians than younger veterans [@rodriguezHealthLiteracyNumeracy2013].

**Notes:** This guideline concerns what to predict from literacy level, not whether content should be understandable.

## When this applies in message framing <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Maintain effective patient–physician interaction while communicating information.
- **Task:** Decide whether to add trust-building framing or reassurance in materials.
- **Data:** Any patient education or decision support content.
- **Chart Setting:** Handouts, portal content, decision aids, and other materials that may include trust cues.
- **Audience:** Veterans with varying literacy levels and ages.
- **Success Criterion:** Trust messaging is included only when it addresses an observed need, not a presumed literacy-based deficit.

## When not to follow it <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** You have direct evidence of low trust in a specific clinic or subgroup beyond literacy level. **Why:** The design target is trust itself, not literacy.

## Tradeoffs and risks <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Less ability to use a simple literacy screen as a proxy for multiple relational needs. **Risk:** Teams may omit trust supports even when needed for other reasons. **Mitigation:** Measure trust separately if it is an outcome you care about.

## Common failure modes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Add extensive reassurance or credibility language only to “low literacy” versions. **Why it fails:** Trust did not differ by health literacy group in this sample [@rodriguezHealthLiteracyNumeracy2013].
- **Mistake:** Assume race-based trust deficits and overcorrect content tone for African American veterans. **Why it fails:** Trust did not differ by race, while other differences (literacy skills and decision-role mismatch) did [@rodriguezHealthLiteracyNumeracy2013].

## Quick tests <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Trust-focused content is long but users still misunderstand the quantitative message. **Quick Check:** Ask whether users perceive the material as trustworthy and separately whether they understood it. **Stronger Test:** Track trust scores and comprehension outcomes independently when evaluating revisions.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Measure trust separately from comprehension if you are optimizing for both.
- Keep trust framing brief unless you have evidence it addresses a real barrier.
- Focus literacy adaptations on comprehension (reading, numeracy, graph interpretation) rather than presumed distrust.
- If segmenting anything by demographics, validate that the segment is actually associated with trust in your population.
