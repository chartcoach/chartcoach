## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

Arrange small-multiple panels in a clear, linear reading order (e.g., a single row) and reorder them to match the narrative sequence you want the reader to follow. Add brief text annotations to connect each panel’s meaning to the next. [@mintzer_sequential_storytelling_2024]

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

Sequencing turns “several trends at once” into a guided, step-by-step explanation: the reader’s attention moves panel-to-panel in a controlled path, so relationships between metrics can be introduced one at a time instead of being inferred all at once. Text annotations supply the intended causal or thematic links between trends, making the connection explicit rather than implied. [@mintzer_sequential_storytelling_2024]

- **The Principle:** Sequential storytelling with small multiples
- **The Evidence:** [@mintzer_sequential_storytelling_2024]

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand how multiple indicators relate (e.g., “these trends are connected, and here’s how”) and feel the “wow” of the combined change. [@mintzer_sequential_storytelling_2024]
- **Data Type:** Multiple time series with different units/scales that benefit from separate panels (e.g., population vs fertility vs life expectancy vs infant mortality). [@mintzer_sequential_storytelling_2024]
- **Audience:** Readers who won’t study the chart deeply unless you guide them (e.g., general feature-story audiences). [@mintzer_sequential_storytelling_2024]

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

- **Scenario:** The primary task is precise cross-metric comparison at the same time point (not narrative understanding).
- **Reason:** A forced sequence can slow down scanning and make side-by-side comparison harder than a layout optimized for simultaneous lookup. [@mintzer_sequential_storytelling_2024]

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

- **The Sacrifice:** Space and simplicity—linear strips and annotations can require more horizontal room and more text. [@mintzer_sequential_storytelling_2024]
- **The Risk:** You can imply a single “story” that may feel overly curated if viewers expect a neutral presentation of all series at once. [@mintzer_sequential_storytelling_2024]

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **The Wrong Fix:** Keeping panels in an arbitrary/grid order that doesn’t match the narrative, then hoping the title alone supplies “bonkersness vibes.”
- **Why it fails:** Readers won’t reliably infer the intended pathway or causal chain; the chart still reads as “multiple separate trends” rather than one connected transformation. [@mintzer_sequential_storytelling_2024]

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

- **Visual Sign:** The viewer can’t tell which panel to look at first, or the panels feel like independent facts rather than steps in one argument. [@mintzer_sequential_storytelling_2024]
- **The Test:** Hide the title and ask: “Can someone describe the intended story in the right order just by moving left-to-right?” If not, the sequence/annotations aren’t doing enough. [@mintzer_sequential_storytelling_2024]

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

- **Quick Fix:** Put the panels in a single row and reorder them so the first panel introduces the starting condition and the last panel shows the outcome. [@mintzer_sequential_storytelling_2024]
- **Best Fix:** Build the chart as a “comic strip”: sequence the panels to express your chosen narrative, then add short, panel-specific text annotations that explicitly link the trends from one panel to the next. [@mintzer_sequential_storytelling_2024]
