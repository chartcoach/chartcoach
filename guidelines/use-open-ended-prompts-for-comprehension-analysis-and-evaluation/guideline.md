## Ask viewers to summarize, analyze, and justify in their own words <!-- role: advice -->

For comprehension, analysis, and evaluation, use open-ended prompts that ask viewers to summarize the message, describe a specific trend, and justify a decision or argument using evidence from the data.

## Why open-ended prompts capture design-driven takeaways <!-- role: reason -->

Closed-ended tasks can hide differences in what viewers find salient, especially when multiple reasonable interpretations exist. Open-ended responses expose which features people notice, what story they tell, and what evidence they select when forming an argument.

**Mechanism:** Free-response prompts surface salience and reasoning pathways (what viewers choose to mention and how they frame it), allowing comparison of interpretation distributions between designs.

**Evidence:** In case studies, open-ended comprehension and analysis questions revealed that one redesign increased mentions of county-level detail and improved recognition of a bi-modal pattern, while other redesigns produced similar takeaway distributions despite design changes [@burnsHowEvaluateData2020]. Open-ended evaluation prompts showed that participants could argue for similar policies across chart versions even when the encodings differed, indicating that higher-level conclusions may not track low-level decoding differences [@burnsHowEvaluateData2020].

**Notes:** Responses can be coded by tagging whether each pre-defined conclusion/topic appears.

## When open-ended measurement is most useful <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Learn what messages and conclusions a design affords, not only whether viewers can read values.
- **Task:** Summarize takeaways, describe trends/relationships, justify a policy/decision using data.
- **Data:** Data where multiple summaries are plausible or where misleading structure could shift interpretation.
- **Chart Setting:** Remote or in-lab; works with static graphics using text entry.
- **Audience:** General readers; especially when you cannot assume shared domain framing.
- **Success Criterion:** Detect shifts in salience, interpretation, and evidence use across designs.

## When open-ended prompts are a poor fit <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** You cannot reasonably analyze qualitative data (time, staffing, or expertise constraints). **Why:** The value comes from systematic coding and comparison, and skipping analysis collapses the method into anecdote.

## Tradeoffs and risks of open-ended responses <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Coding time and the need to define categories of conclusions or trends. **Risk:** Participant verbosity varies, and some may omit details they noticed. **Mitigation:** Keep prompts specific (e.g., “describe how X changed over time”) to narrow response scope.

## Common mistakes with open-ended evaluation prompts <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Asking a generic “what do you think?” question. **Why it fails:** It mixes Bloom levels and produces responses that are difficult to compare across participants and designs.
- **Mistake:** Coding responses while knowing which design the participant saw. **Why it fails:** It can bias which themes you notice and how strictly you apply categories.

## Quick checks for open-ended prompt quality <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Responses are mostly vague opinions (“looks bad,” “it’s confusing”) rather than data-grounded descriptions.\
**Quick Check:** Confirm each prompt names a concrete object (message, trend for a named series, or argument with evidence).\
**Stronger Test:** Blind-code a small pilot set and verify you can tag responses into a stable set of themes.

## What to do instead if open-ended prompts underperform <!-- role: fix -->

- Add a constraint to the prompt (e.g., “use one sentence” or “cite one specific comparison”).
- Provide a named focus (a specific county/series/time window) for analysis questions.
- Use a structured coding rubric and tag presence/absence of themes rather than trying to score “quality” holistically.
- Convert the prompt into a checklist of possible takeaways and ask participants to select all that apply.
