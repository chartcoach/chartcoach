## Prefer distinctive visualization types over common charts for memorability <!-- role: advice -->

When memorability is the priority, favor more distinctive visualization types (such as diagrams, grid/matrix displays, and trees/networks) over common charts like bars, lines, and tables.

## Why distinctive types are remembered better <!-- role: reason -->

Distinctive overall structures reduce category-level similarity, decreasing interference from other items and improving recognition while lowering confusion.

**Mechanism:** When many visuals share the same basic form, viewers are more likely to confuse them; unusual forms provide stronger identity cues.

**Evidence:** Diagrams, grid/matrix visualizations, and trees/networks had higher memorability scores than bars, lines, points, and tables, and the pattern remained after removing pictogram-containing visualizations [@borkinWhatMakesVisualization2013a].

**Notes:** The target set reflected distributions of visualization types “in the wild,” so some categories were less frequent than others.

## When to apply distinctive chart types for memorability <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Remember which visualization was seen (recognition over time).
- **Task:** Distinguish one visualization from many in a collection or feed.
- **Data:** Any, but especially where multiple charts show similar structures across topics.
- **Chart Setting:** Static, single-panel; brief exposure.
- **Audience:** General or mixed-expertise viewers.
- **Success Criterion:** Higher memorability with fewer confusions.

## When not to prioritize distinctiveness <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** You must adhere to a fixed house style that requires a specific common chart type for consistency. **Why:** The setting constrains chart-type choice even if memorability would benefit from a more distinctive form [@borkinWhatMakesVisualization2013a].

## Tradeoffs of choosing distinctive types <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Standardization and immediate familiarity with conventional charts.\
**Risk:** A more distinctive form may be remembered as an image without being understood.\
**Mitigation:** Treat memorability as a separate objective from comprehension and validate both if needed.

## Common mistakes with “unique” chart types <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Switching to an unusual chart type and assuming it improves effectiveness overall. **Why it fails:** The evidence establishes differences in memorability, not comprehension or decision quality [@borkinWhatMakesVisualization2013a].

## Quick checks for chart-type memorability <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Multiple charts in a set blur together because they share the same overall silhouette (e.g., many similar bar charts).\
**Quick Check:** Thumbnail the set; if many charts are visually indistinguishable, memorability is likely low.\
**Stronger Test:** Run a brief recognition test by mixing your chart among similar ones and measuring confusions.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Add a distinctive pictorial cue if you cannot change the chart type.
- Increase distinct colors to improve discriminability while keeping the same chart type.
- Introduce additional structural variation within the allowed chart family (e.g., diagrammatic framing) to reduce sameness.
- If neither type nor styling can change, rely on accompanying narrative context and accept lower image memorability.
