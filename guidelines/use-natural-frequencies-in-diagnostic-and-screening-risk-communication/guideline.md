## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

Present diagnostic or screening test risks using natural frequencies (e.g., “10 out of 1000”) rather than percentages (e.g., “1%”).

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

Natural frequencies reduce cognitive load in probabilistic reasoning by grounding quantities in countable cases, improving objective comprehension of risk statements.

- **The Principle:** Frequency-format facilitation of probabilistic reasoning
- **The Evidence:** Participants understood diagnostic/screening risks better with natural frequencies than percentages (SMD 0.69, 95% CI 0.45 to 0.93) in studies summarized by [@aklUsingAlternativeStatistical2011].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Correctly interpret a diagnostic or screening test result (e.g., positive predictive value-type reasoning).
- **Data Type:** Single-risk statements or conditional-risk statements tied to screening/diagnosis scenarios.
- **Audience:** Health consumers and health professionals (effects were similar across groups in [@aklUsingAlternativeStatistical2011]).

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

- **Scenario:** The communication is about treatment benefits/harms rather than diagnostic/screening test risks.
- **Reason:** The review’s evidence for frequency superiority is explicitly in diagnostic/screening contexts; it did not test treatment-effect communication for this comparison [@aklUsingAlternativeStatistical2011].

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

- **The Sacrifice:** More space and words than a single percentage.
- **The Risk:** Poorly chosen denominators (e.g., “1 in 17,543”) can make frequencies harder to scan.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **The Wrong Fix:** Showing only a percentage and calling it “probability.”
- **Why it fails:** The evidence in [@aklUsingAlternativeStatistical2011] compares frequency vs percentage formats; relabeling does not change the numeric format that drives comprehension differences.

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

- **Visual Sign:** Risk information appears only as “X%” (or “0.X”) with no “out of N” framing.
- **The Test:** Ask a reader to restate “how many people out of 1000” are affected; if they struggle, you likely violated the rule.

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

- **Quick Fix:** Add an equivalent frequency next to the percentage (e.g., “1% (10 out of 1000)”).
- **Best Fix:** Rewrite the entire diagnostic/screening scenario using consistent natural-frequency counts throughout (e.g., “out of 10,000…”) as the primary format, as aligned with the intervention format evaluated in [@aklUsingAlternativeStatistical2011].
