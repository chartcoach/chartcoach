## Use icon arrays to show both numerator and denominator for unequal groups <!-- role: advice -->

Add icon arrays alongside numeric risk statements whenever you compare outcomes across groups with different total sizes (different denominators). Show the full group size and mark the affected subset within each group.

## Why icon arrays reduce denominator neglect in unequal-denominator comparisons <!-- role: reason -->

When denominators differ, people often focus on the absolute count of events (numerators) and underweight the total opportunities (denominators), producing biased comparisons. Icon arrays make the “part-to-whole” relationship visually explicit for each group, which helps viewers incorporate the denominator into judgment.

**Mechanism:** The display increases salience of the total group size and supports proportion-based comparison rather than raw-count comparison.

**Evidence:** With numeric-only presentations and unequal denominators, many people over- or underestimate treatment risk reduction in patterns consistent with denominator neglect; adding icon arrays reduces these errors, especially for low-numeracy participants [@garcia-retameroUsingVisualAids2012]. This reduction also appears when information is presented in a nonnative language, where icon arrays improve accuracy relative to numbers alone [@garcia-retameroUsingVisualAids2012].

**Notes:** The benefit is largest when denominators are inconsistent across the compared groups.

## When unequal-denominator risk comparisons are being communicated <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand or compare treatment benefit/harms across options.
- **Task:** Judge relative risk reduction (or similar ratio-based effect) from group outcome data.
- **Data:** Two-group outcomes with different group sizes (e.g., 100 treated vs 800 untreated).
- **Chart Setting:** Patient-facing materials, consent/decision aids, public health messages, or media-style explanations.
- **Audience:** Mixed numeracy and mixed language proficiency.
- **Success Criterion:** More accurate proportional judgments and fewer systematic over/underestimates.

## When not to rely on icon arrays alone <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The audience has very low ability to interpret graphs (low graph literacy) and the icon array is likely to be misunderstood. **Why:** The effectiveness of icon arrays is moderated by graph literacy, so some viewers gain less from them [@garcia-retameroUsingVisualAids2012].

## Tradeoffs of adding icon arrays <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Space and layout simplicity, especially when totals are large. **Risk:** Viewers with low graph literacy may not fully benefit and may still rely on the numbers. **Mitigation:** Plan for supplemental non-graph supports if graph literacy is expected to be low.

## Common ways icon arrays fail to fix denominator neglect <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Showing only the number affected (numerator) with icons but not the total group represented. **Why it fails:** It preserves numerator salience without making the denominator visually available, so denominator neglect can persist.

## Quick checks for denominator-neglect-resistant design <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Viewers could plausibly compare groups using only event counts (e.g., “5 deaths vs 80 deaths”) without noticing different totals. **Quick Check:** For each group, verify the icon array visibly encodes the full group size and the affected subset within that same frame. **Stronger Test:** Ask a small sample of users to explain “out of how many?” for each group without prompting.

## What to do instead when icon arrays are not sufficient <!-- role: fix -->

- Add explicit prompts in text that restate the denominator for each group (e.g., “out of 100” and “out of 800”) adjacent to the visual.
- Reduce the cognitive load by limiting comparisons to the two groups required for the decision.
- Provide additional non-visual support for interpreting the graphic when graph literacy is expected to be low.
- Replace the unequal-denominator comparison with an equivalent presentation that avoids inconsistent denominators when feasible.
