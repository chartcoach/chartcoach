## Use more distinct colors to increase memorability <!-- role: advice -->

Use a palette with seven or more distinct colors when the visualization must be memorable as an image.

## Why more distinct colors increase memorability <!-- role: reason -->

More distinct colors increase visual discriminability, making a visualization less confusable with others and easier to recognize after a delay.

**Mechanism:** Color variety creates additional unique visual signatures that support recognition and reduce interference from similar-looking charts.

**Evidence:** Visualizations with seven or more distinct colors had higher memorability scores than those with fewer colors, and the effect remained statistically significant even after removing visualizations with pictograms [@borkinWhatMakesVisualization2013a].

**Notes:** This finding concerns memorability in a rapid serial visual presentation memory game, not analytic accuracy.

## When to apply high color variety <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Recognize or recall the visualization later.
- **Task:** Distinguish the visualization from many others (low confusability).
- **Data:** Any; especially where many visuals of similar structure exist.
- **Chart Setting:** Static images; attention is brief and competing visuals are many.
- **Audience:** General audiences; mixed visualization expertise.
- **Success Criterion:** Higher memorability score (recognition with fewer false alarms).

## When not to maximize distinct colors <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The chart has only a few meaningful groups and additional colors would not encode additional structure. **Why:** Extra distinct colors may not correspond to meaningful visual information even if they increase image distinctiveness [@borkinWhatMakesVisualization2013a].

## Tradeoffs of using many colors <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Simplicity and ease of maintaining consistent semantics across a report or product.\
**Risk:** Color becomes decorative rather than informative, potentially shifting attention away from the intended message.\
**Mitigation:** Keep color assignments systematic and aligned to categories that matter.

## Common mistakes with color for memorability <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Adding many colors without tying them to distinct groups or elements. **Why it fails:** The visualization may look busy without improving the viewer’s ability to interpret what the colors mean [@borkinWhatMakesVisualization2013a].
- **Mistake:** Treating “more color” as a substitute for distinct structure. **Why it fails:** The study’s measure is recognition-based and does not show improved comprehension [@borkinWhatMakesVisualization2013a].

## Quick checks for color-driven memorability <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** The visualization looks visually similar to others when squinted or viewed as a thumbnail.\
**Quick Check:** Convert the image to grayscale; if it becomes hard to distinguish from similar charts, color may be doing most of the identity work.\
**Stronger Test:** Compare recognition performance (hits and false alarms) between a low-color and high-color variant in a small pilot.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Increase contrast and distinctiveness through overall layout or mark shapes rather than adding more hues.
- Choose a more distinctive visualization type (e.g., diagram-like forms) if color cannot be increased.
- Add a pictorial/recognizable object cue if appropriate to the communication setting.
- If the primary goal is comprehension, validate with task-based measures rather than memorability-only metrics.
