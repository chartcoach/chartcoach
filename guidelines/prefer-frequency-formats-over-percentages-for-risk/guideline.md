## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

When presenting risk statistics, consider using frequencies (e.g., “60 out of 100”) instead of (or alongside) percentages.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

Frequencies can be easier to understand in some studies and can make risks feel less abstract than percentages, affecting perceived risk; patients also prefer receiving frequency formats.

- **The Principle:** Make probabilities concrete and less abstract
- **The Evidence:** Evidence is mixed on comprehension, but frequencies can improve understanding in some studies, change risk perception (especially for lower numeracy groups), and are preferred by people [@fagerlinHelpingPatientsDecide2011].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand the magnitude of side effects or outcomes well enough to compare options.
- **Data Type:** Probabilities such as side-effect rates (e.g., impotence risk after surgery).
- **Audience:** Patients, including those with low numeracy.

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

- **Scenario:** None stated in the paper.
- **Reason:** Not specified.

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

- **The Sacrifice:** Frequency statements can feel more vivid and therefore more worrying.
- **The Risk:** Users may react more strongly to the same numeric value when it is phrased as “X out of 100” [@fagerlinHelpingPatientsDecide2011].

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **The Wrong Fix:** Using percentages only (e.g., “10%”) when the goal is concrete understanding.
- **Why it fails:** Percentages can feel abstract and may change perceived risk, particularly among lower numeracy audiences [@fagerlinHelpingPatientsDecide2011].
- **The Wrong Fix:** Using inconsistent denominators (e.g., “1 in 37” in one place and “5 in 100” elsewhere).
- **Why it fails:** It increases cognitive effort and makes comparisons harder (the paper emphasizes numeracy challenges) [@fagerlinHelpingPatientsDecide2011].

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

- **Visual Sign:** Risk messages rely on “%” without any “out of N” translation.
- **The Test:** Ask whether a reader can restate the risk as “X out of 100 people”; if they can’t, add frequency wording.

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

- **Quick Fix:** Add frequency equivalents next to percentages (e.g., “10% (10 out of 100)”).
- **Best Fix:** Standardize on a single denominator (commonly 100) across all options so comparisons are straightforward [@fagerlinHelpingPatientsDecide2011].
