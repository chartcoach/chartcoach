## Add topic-linked imagery for long-term recall <!-- role: advice -->

Add strong, topic-linked visual imagery to a chart when you need people to remember its topic and key details after days or weeks. Keep the imagery clearly related to the chart’s subject rather than generic decoration.

## Why imagery helps long-term recall without harming comprehension <!-- role: reason -->

Embellished imagery provides additional memory cues that can be encoded alongside the data, improving later retrieval even when the viewer is not trying to memorize the chart. When the imagery is tightly connected to the chart’s subject and structure, it can support recall without reducing accuracy during on-screen interpretation.

**Mechanism:** The chart becomes more distinctive and easier to cue from memory because viewers encode both the data depiction and the associated image.

**Evidence:** Chart descriptions (with the chart visible) were no less accurate for embellished charts than for plain versions, with no significant differences on subject, values/categories, or trend accuracy [@batemanUsefulJunkEffects2010a]. After a 2–3 week delay, recall of chart subject, categories, trend, and value message was significantly better for embellished charts than plain ones [@batemanUsefulJunkEffects2010a].

**Notes:** The same benefit was not observed after a short (five-minute) delay, where recall differences were mostly not significant outside of value message [@batemanUsefulJunkEffects2010a].

## When long-term memorability is the primary objective <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Remember chart topics and key takeaways after the chart is no longer available.
- **Task:** Recall subject, categories/values, trends, and intended message after a substantial delay.
- **Data:** Any chartable dataset where a high-level takeaway and a few key details are important to retain.
- **Chart Setting:** Static or presentation-style charts where viewers can look as long as needed during initial viewing.
- **Audience:** General readers, including busy or lay readers, where recall is valued.
- **Success Criterion:** Higher long-term recall with no loss of immediate comprehension accuracy.

## When not to rely on strong embellishment <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The chart must support careful, bias-minimized interpretation where persuasive framing is unacceptable. **Why:** Strong imagery can increase perceived “value message” and may push interpretation toward a particular framing [@batemanUsefulJunkEffects2010a].

## Tradeoffs of using strong imagery <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** More design effort and visual complexity than a plain chart. **Risk:** Imagery can increase perceived messaging or persuasion beyond the data, which may be undesirable in some contexts. **Mitigation:** Treat imagery as a memory aid only when that goal is explicit and acceptable for the setting.

## Common ways this goes wrong <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Adding imagery that is not clearly connected to the chart’s topic or structure. **Why it fails:** The added content may not contribute useful memory cues and can shift attention away from data without improving recall.

## Quick checks for whether the imagery is helping <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Viewers remember the picture but cannot recall what the chart was about or what changed. **Quick Check:** Ask a few readers to summarize the chart’s subject and trend immediately after viewing; accuracy should not drop versus a plain version. **Stronger Test:** Re-contact readers after 2–3 weeks and compare recall of subject, categories, and trend between embellished and plain versions.

## What to do instead if imagery is risky <!-- role: fix -->

- Use a plain chart when neutrality and minimizing implied messaging is the priority.
- Keep any added visuals minimal and avoid elements that imply an evaluative “value message.”
- Prefer a design that preserves the same data, chart type, and labels while reducing non-data visuals if you cannot validate recall benefits.
- Pilot-test both versions with your audience and select the one that preserves comprehension while meeting your recall goal.
