## Present diagnostic or screening test risks as natural frequencies instead of percentages <!-- role: advice -->

Present diagnostic or screening test risks using natural frequencies (for example, “10 out of 1000”) rather than percentages (for example, “1%”). Keep the denominator explicit so the reader can track the reference class.

## Why natural frequencies improve understanding in diagnostic and screening tasks <!-- role: reason -->

Natural frequencies make the sample size and nested groups concrete, reducing the mental conversion burden when interpreting conditional-risk information (such as what a positive test result means). This supports more accurate computation or estimation of the target probability from the same underlying evidence.

**Mechanism:** Frequencies externalize the denominator and subgroup counts, which helps people keep base rates and test accuracy information aligned when reasoning about test results.

**Evidence:** Across studies using diagnostic or screening scenarios, understanding was higher with natural frequencies than with percentages (standardized mean difference about 0.69 standard deviations) [@aklUsingAlternativeStatistical2011].

**Notes:** This evidence base in the review is specific to interpreting diagnostic or screening test results, not treatment effect comparisons.

## When to use natural frequencies for risks <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Interpret what a test result means (for example, the chance of disease after a positive test).
- **Task:** Estimate or interpret a risk/probability from diagnostic or screening performance information.
- **Data:** Probabilities or rates that can be expressed as counts in a defined sample (for example, per 1000 people).
- **Chart Setting:** Textual explanations, decision aids, consent forms, lab reports, or tables accompanying test results.
- **Audience:** Health consumers or health professionals with mixed numeracy.
- **Success Criterion:** Higher objective accuracy in risk interpretation.

## When not to use natural frequencies as the primary format <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The communication cannot supply a meaningful denominator (for example, the reference class is undefined or unstable across contexts). **Why:** Frequencies depend on a clear reference class; without it, the counts can mislead.

## Tradeoffs of using natural frequencies <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Natural frequencies may take more space and can feel less succinct than a single percentage. **Risk:** Poorly chosen denominators (too small or inconsistent) can make comparisons harder. **Mitigation:** Use a stable, round denominator that matches the scenario and keep it consistent within the same communication.

## Common mistakes when using natural frequencies <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Switching denominators across statements (for example, “out of 100” in one place and “out of 10,000” elsewhere). **Why it fails:** Readers lose the shared reference class and cannot reliably compare or combine the numbers.
- **Mistake:** Omitting the denominator (“10 will have the disease”) without stating “out of how many.” **Why it fails:** The magnitude becomes uninterpretable.

## Quick checks for frequency-based risk communication <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Readers can repeat numbers but cannot answer what the chance of disease is after a positive result. **Quick Check:** Verify every frequency statement includes an explicit “x out of y” reference class. **Stronger Test:** Ask a small sample of intended users to compute or choose the correct post-test risk using the displayed numbers.

## What to do instead if frequencies do not fit <!-- role: fix -->

- Use percentages only alongside an explicit shared denominator statement (for example, “per 1000 people”) to preserve the reference class.
- Provide a simple table that lists the counts in each subgroup defined by disease status and test result, using one consistent denominator.
- Reduce the task to an interpretation question (for example, multiple-choice) that does not require users to reconstruct the denominator.
