## Use human-recognizable pictograms to boost memorability <!-- role: advice -->

Add a human-recognizable pictogram (photo, cartoon, or icon of a real-world object) if you want the visualization to be remembered as an image.

## Why human-recognizable pictograms boost memorability <!-- role: reason -->

Human-recognizable imagery provides a strong, distinct visual cue that can be encoded and later recognized quickly, improving repeat-detection in memory tasks.

**Mechanism:** Recognizable objects act as salient anchors, making the overall image easier to discriminate from others and easier to retrieve later.

**Evidence:** Visualizations containing human-recognizable pictograms had substantially higher memorability scores than those without pictograms in a large-scale online repeat-detection experiment [@borkinWhatMakesVisualization2013a].

**Notes:** This result is about memorability of the visualization as an image, not about understanding the data.

## When to apply pictograms for memorability <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Remember the visualization later (e.g., post-exposure recall).
- **Task:** Visual recognition / repeat detection of the visualization.
- **Data:** Any; especially when the base chart form is visually similar to many others.
- **Chart Setting:** Static, single-panel visualization; brief viewing time.
- **Audience:** Broad audiences with varied visualization literacy.
- **Success Criterion:** Higher recognition after a delay or among many competing visuals.

## When not to rely on pictograms <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** The intended evaluation outcome is comprehension of the underlying data rather than remembering the image. **Why:** The measured effect is memorability-as-image and does not establish improved understanding [@borkinWhatMakesVisualization2013a].

## Tradeoffs of adding pictograms <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Visual simplicity and a higher data-ink ratio.\
**Risk:** Viewers remember the pictorial element more than the data content.\
**Mitigation:** Treat the pictogram as a supporting cue rather than the main carrier of the quantitative message.

## Common mistakes with pictograms <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Assuming higher image memorability implies better data comprehension. **Why it fails:** The evidence isolates memorability of the visualization as an image and does not test comprehension [@borkinWhatMakesVisualization2013a].

## Quick checks for pictogram-driven memorability <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Viewers cannot distinguish your visualization from others of the same chart type after brief exposure.\
**Quick Check:** Ask someone to describe one non-textual element they remember after a short delay; if they report nothing distinctive, the image may be forgettable.\
**Stronger Test:** Run a small repeat-detection pilot with your visualization among similar ones and compare hit/false-alarm rates.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Add a single, clearly recognizable pictorial element that is visually distinct from the surrounding marks.
- Use a visualization type with more distinctive overall form if pictograms are not appropriate.
- Increase discriminability through other visual channels measured in the study, such as using more distinct colors.
- If the goal is comprehension, prioritize clear encodings and validate with task-based user testing rather than memorability testing.
