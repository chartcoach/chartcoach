## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->
If your goal is to persuade or convey a specific "value message" (e.g., that a trend is bad, scary, or good), explicitly use thematic imagery to reinforce that narrative. Do not rely on the data geometry alone to carry the emotional weight.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->
Minimalist charts are often perceived as objective, but they may fail to communicate the author's intended message. In testing, participants identified a "value message" (e.g., "costs are monstrous") significantly more often in embellished charts than in plain charts, even when the underlying data was identical [@bateman_useful_2010].

*   **The Principle:** Rhetorical Imagery.
*   **The Evidence:** Significant difference in "value message" description scores (p=.003) in favor of embellished charts [@bateman_useful_2010].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->
*   **User Goal:** Editorializing, campaigning, or storytelling.
*   **Audience:** Mass media consumers (e.g., readers of *TIME* or *USA Today*).

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->
*   **Scenario:** Scientific or Financial Reporting.
*   **Reason:** When the goal is strict objectivity or neutrality. The paper notes that "images convey messages that may intentionally or unintentionally bias the viewer’s interpretation" [@bateman_useful_2010].

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->
*   **The Sacrifice:** Perceived Objectivity.
*   **The Risk:** The audience may view the chart as biased or manipulative because the "image-as-opinion" is difficult for people to ignore [@bateman_useful_2010].

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->
*   **The Wrong Fix:** Using a plain bar chart and hoping the steep slope conveys "danger."
*   **Why it fails:** Participants often miss the editorial subtext in plain charts compared to those with evocative imagery (like a monster) [@bateman_useful_2010].

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->
*   **Visual Sign:** Does the chart evoke an emotion (fear, happiness, disgust)?
*   **The Test:** Ask a viewer, "Is the author trying to communicate a specific point or just presenting facts?" If they say "just facts," your value message is weak.

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->
*   **Quick Fix:** Add a title that explicitly states the judgment (e.g., "Monstrous Costs") and pair it with a relevant icon.
*   **Best Fix:** Use visual metaphors (e.g., representing a crashing stock market as a falling object) to make the value judgment an inseparable part of the visualization.
