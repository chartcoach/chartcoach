## Retain icon arrays as an additive aid alongside absolute risk reduction <!-- role: advice -->

Keep icon arrays as a visual supplement even when you already present risks using Absolute Risk Reduction (ARR) rather than Relative Risk Reduction (RRR).

## Why icon arrays still help when numbers are already “transparent” <!-- role: reason -->

Even relatively clear numerical framing can leave readers needing to translate and compare quantities; icon arrays supply a parallel representation that supports the same inference through perceptual comparison.

**Mechanism:** ARR reduces framing confusion, while icon arrays reduce translation and comparison burden by making the “before vs. after” difference visually inspectable.

**Evidence:** Icon arrays improved accuracy independently of whether numerical information was given as ARR or RRR, indicating an additional benefit beyond using ARR alone [@galesicUsingIconArrays2009].

**Notes:** The incremental benefit held across both older adults and students.

## When ARR-plus-icon-arrays is the right combination <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Correctly understand treatment benefit magnitude.
- **Task:** Compare baseline risk to treated risk and infer the reduction.
- **Data:** Two absolute risks with the same outcome definition.
- **Chart Setting:** Decision aids, consent materials, or educational modules that already use ARR.
- **Audience:** Broad audiences where numeracy varies.
- **Success Criterion:** Measurable improvement in correct responses over ARR-only materials.

## When not to follow this exactly <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** Adding a visual element would crowd out the numeric values needed for precise reference (e.g., extremely space-constrained formats). **Why:** If the numbers become hard to find or read, the combined format may not support the intended comparison.

## Tradeoffs and risks <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** More layout space and design effort than ARR-only text. **Risk:** The visual format may shift perceived seriousness or helpfulness relative to numbers-only presentation. **Mitigation:** Pretest perception outcomes if the communication goal includes calibrated risk perception.

## Common failure modes <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Presenting ARR in text but drawing icon arrays that do not correspond to the same denominator or exact counts. **Why it fails:** Inconsistent mappings undermine the comparison the viewer must make.

## Quick tests <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** Readers can report the ARR sentence but still miscompute the implied treated and untreated counts out of a fixed N.\
**Quick Check:** Ask readers to answer both “out of N without” and “out of N with” questions after exposure.\
**Stronger Test:** Run a short comprehension test comparing ARR-only versus ARR-plus-icon-arrays with the same content.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Present baseline and treated risks as frequencies with a shared denominator (e.g., “X out of 1,000” and “Y out of 1,000”).
- Pair the ARR statement with explicit counts for both conditions to minimize translation.
- Reduce cognitive load by showing only one outcome and one comparison at a time.
- If visuals must be removed, keep the ARR format and ensure both baseline and treated risks are stated explicitly.
