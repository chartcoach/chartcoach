## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->
Use less saturated, pastel, or "toned down" colors rather than vibrant, pure "crayon" colors when visualizing race.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->
Highly saturated colors carry strong semantic meaning (e.g., Red = danger/important; Green = good/correct; Dark Blue = competent/royal). You generally do not want to attach these adjectives to racial groups. Pale or desaturated colors (e.g., pale red or rose instead of bright red) dampen these emotional associations [@muth_race_ethnicity_colors_2024].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->
*   **User Goal:** Neutral presentation of demographic data.
*   **Data Type:** Nominal/Categorical data regarding people.

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->
*   **Scenario:** Highlighting a specific disparity where one group is catching up or falling behind.
*   **Reason:** Saturation can be used intentionally to draw the eye to the story (e.g., the "People of Color" line in a chart about progress), provided the other colors are neutral grays.

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->
*   **The Sacrifice:** Reduced contrast and visual impact.
*   **The Risk:** Lower saturation makes colors harder to distinguish for colorblind readers.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->
*   **The Wrong Fix:** Using pastels without adding borders.
*   **Why it fails:** Pale colors often fail accessibility contrast tests against white backgrounds.

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->
*   **Visual Sign:** Do the colors scream "Danger" or "Go"?
*   **The Test:** Look at the red category. Does it look like a warning sign, or just a category?

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->
*   **Quick Fix:** Lower the saturation/opacity of your existing palette.
*   **Best Fix:** Use lighter shades combined with darker, saturated outlines/strokes to ensure the shapes remain visible and accessible.
