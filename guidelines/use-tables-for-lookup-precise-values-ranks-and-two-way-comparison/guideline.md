## Choose tables for lookup, precision, ranks, or two-way comparisons <!-- role: advice -->

Use a table when readers need to find a specific value, act on precise numbers, compare values both by row and by column, or read ranks as ranks rather than magnitudes.

## Lookup and precision outperform pattern-seeing in tables <!-- role: reason -->

Tables support targeted retrieval: readers can scan labels and read exact values without estimating from position or shape, making them suitable when the output is a specific number or rank rather than a pattern.

**Mechanism:** Tables reduce interpretation to label matching and exact reading, which helps when users already know what entity they care about (their city, product, or category) and need an exact value or rank.

**Evidence:** Tables are recommended when the goal is to look up specific information, when precise numbers matter for decisions, when readers should compare data in two directions, and when showing ranks where “1 vs 2” indicates ordering rather than magnitude [@muth_tables_2019].

**Notes:** This guideline is about choosing a table over a chart, not about styling the table.

## Situations that should trigger a table-first choice <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Find “my” row/category and retrieve an exact value; make a decision using exact numbers; understand an ordering.
- **Task:** Lookup, ranking, or two-way comparison (across entities and time/categories simultaneously).
- **Data:** Structured rows with identifiers (names, places, teams) and associated values; ranks or exact numeric values.
- **Chart Setting:** Storytelling context where readers may skim rather than study the whole display.
- **Audience:** Mixed literacy; readers likely to focus on their own relevant entry first.
- **Success Criterion:** Fast, reliable retrieval of the correct value/rank without estimation.

## When to choose a chart instead <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** Your goal is to give an overview, reveal patterns, or make outliers and extremes hard to miss. **Why:** Tables are read sequentially and make trends/outliers easier to overlook than in charts [@muth_tables_2019].

## Tradeoffs of using a table <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** You give up immediate visual pattern recognition and memorability compared with charts. **Risk:** Important values can be buried deep in long tables, causing readers to stop before they reach them. **Mitigation:** Keep the table short enough that key information is visible without extensive scrolling [@muth_tables_2019].

## Common selection mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Using a table when the audience needs a quick overview or pattern. **Why it fails:** Readers may miss outliers, maxima/minima, and trends because the information is consumed row-by-row [@muth_tables_2019].

## Quick tests for “table vs chart” <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** People ask “what’s the trend/outlier?” while staring at a long list of numbers. **Quick Check:** If the primary question is “what is the value for X?” a table is appropriate; if it’s “what stands out overall?” it’s not. **Stronger Test:** Ask two readers to answer one lookup question and one pattern question; if the lookup is faster than the pattern task, the table is serving the right job [@muth_tables_2019].

## What to do instead if you need patterns <!-- role: fix -->

- Use a chart when the intent is to communicate an overall picture, trends, or outliers rather than exact retrieval [@muth_tables_2019].
- Reduce the amount of data shown at once so key values are not hidden deep in a long table [@muth_tables_2019].
- Combine a table with embedded visual encodings (like bars or sparklines) if you need both exact values and quick scanning [@muth_tables_2019].
