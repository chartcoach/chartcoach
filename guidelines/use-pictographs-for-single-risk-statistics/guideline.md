## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

If you include a graph for an individual probability, use a pictograph (icon array) rather than a pie chart or bar chart.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

Pictographs make probabilities concrete by simultaneously representing numerator and denominator and supporting both gist and verbatim understanding; they are also associated with reduced bias in how patients interpret risk.

- **The Principle:** Make the denominator visible to support accurate risk sense
- **The Evidence:** Pictographs are more quickly and better comprehended than other formats for individual statistics and help prevent biased judgments; they outperform pie charts for verbatim knowledge and outperform bar graphs for gist (or match pies for gist) [@fagerlinHelpingPatientsDecide2011].

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand “how many out of how many” experience an outcome.
- **Data Type:** Single-point probabilities (e.g., side effects at 2 years; chance of a complication).
- **Audience:** Patients (especially those needing to think “in depth” about statistics).

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

- **Scenario:** Showing change in risk patterns over time.
- **Reason:** The paper notes pictographs may be harder than line graphs for communicating risk patterns over time [@fagerlinHelpingPatientsDecide2011].

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

- **The Sacrifice:** More space than a compact bar/pie; harder to show many groups or long time horizons.
- **The Risk:** Overcrowding if you try to show too many outcomes/options in one array.

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **The Wrong Fix:** Using pie charts or standard bars for single-risk communication because they “look simpler.”
- **Why it fails:** These formats are not as consistently fast/accurate for comprehension as pictographs for individual risks [@fagerlinHelpingPatientsDecide2011].
- **The Wrong Fix:** Showing only the affected group without showing the unaffected remainder.
- **Why it fails:** It hides the denominator and undermines frequency understanding (a core benefit of pictographs) [@fagerlinHelpingPatientsDecide2011].

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

- **Visual Sign:** The graphic does not clearly show both “affected” and “not affected” out of a fixed total.
- **The Test:** Ask a reader to state “X out of 100” after viewing the chart; if they struggle, the display is not leveraging the pictograph advantage.

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

- **Quick Fix:** Convert the probability into an icon array with a fixed denominator (e.g., 100) and color the affected vs unaffected.
- **Best Fix:** Use pictographs plus clear labels in plain language so the reader can extract both gist and exact counts [@fagerlinHelpingPatientsDecide2011].
