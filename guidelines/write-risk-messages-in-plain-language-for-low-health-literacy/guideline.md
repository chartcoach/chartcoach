## Use plain language for all risk and benefit explanations <!-- role: advice -->

Write and speak about risks and benefits using plain language with minimal clinical and statistical jargon. Keep the content tightly structured and easy to scan so patients can follow the logic.

## Plain language reduces comprehension barriers <!-- role: reason -->

Plain language lowers reading and interpretation demands, making it more likely that people with limited health literacy can extract the meaning needed for decisions.

**Mechanism:** Reduced jargon and improved structure decrease decoding effort, freeing attention for understanding the risk/benefit tradeoffs.

**Evidence:** Plain-language health materials are associated with better understanding of the content among audiences with limited literacy [@fagerlinHelpingPatientsDecide2011].

**Notes:** This applies to both written materials and clinician–patient conversations.

## When plain-language risk communication is needed <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Understand treatment risks/benefits well enough to make a preference-sensitive choice.
- **Task:** Interpret and compare medical options using probabilistic information.
- **Data:** Probabilities of outcomes, side effects, and benefits.
- **Chart Setting:** Any medium (handouts, web decision aids, verbal explanation).
- **Audience:** Patients with varying health literacy and numeracy; assume many are not comfortable with medical/statistical terms.
- **Success Criterion:** Patients can accurately restate key risks and benefits and use them in tradeoffs.

## When not to follow it <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** A specific technical term is necessary for clinical correctness and has no plain substitute. **Why:** Removing it could introduce ambiguity or clinical misunderstanding.

## Tradeoffs and risks <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** Precision and brevity can be harder to balance, and materials may become longer. **Risk:** Over-simplification can erase clinically important distinctions. **Mitigation:** Keep essential terms but define them once in simple words.

## Common failure modes <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Using clinical or statistical jargon without explanation (for example, presenting terms like relative risk or confidence interval as if they are self-explanatory). **Why it fails:** Many patients cannot decode the terminology well enough to use the information in decisions.

## Quick tests <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** People ask what common terms mean or misunderstand what the numbers refer to. **Quick Check:** Remove or define every specialized term and see if the message still reads clearly. **Stronger Test:** Ask a patient to explain the message back in their own words and listen for mismatches.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Define unavoidable medical terms immediately in everyday language.
- Reorganize content into short sections with clear headings and ample whitespace.
- Replace abstract statistical phrasing with concrete outcome statements tied to the decision.
- Provide a brief recap that restates the key risks and benefits in the same simple terms.
