## The Rule <!-- role: advice -->

Design and facilitate tasks assuming students will use the graphing calculator mainly for verification when they understand the underlying concepts, and mainly for exploratory parameter-trying when they do not.

## The Logic <!-- role: reason -->

Mesa observed that for an inequality/construction task, students expected the calculator to confirm their proposed functions; for the polynomial-from-graphs task, students used the calculator to test many parameter values rapidly (a “cybernetic” try–see–adjust loop), especially because they lacked key structural knowledge (roots/factors) [@mesaSolvingProblemsFunctions2008].

- **The Principle:** Tool use follows perceived function: confirm known reasoning vs. compensate for missing structure via trial-and-error.
- **The Evidence:** [@mesaSolvingProblemsFunctions2008]

## Where to Apply <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Plan instruction and prompts around likely tool behaviors.
- **Data Type:** Function problems involving inequalities, intersections, or fitting expressions to graphs.
- **Audience:** Instructors and task designers using graphing calculators.

## When to Break It <!-- role: exceptions -->

- **Scenario:** Students are explicitly trained to use the calculator in concept-driven ways (e.g., structured prompts linking features to parameters).
- **Reason:** Instructional norms can re-purpose exploration into principled investigation, changing default behaviors [@mesaSolvingProblemsFunctions2008].

## The Price <!-- role: costs -->

- **The Sacrifice:** Accepting this pattern may reduce expectations that the calculator automatically produces conceptual insight.
- **The Risk:** Unstructured exploration can consume large time without progress if not mathematically guided [@mesaSolvingProblemsFunctions2008].

## Common Mistakes <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **The Wrong Fix:** Assume “more exploration with technology” implies deeper understanding.
- **Why it fails:** Mesa found extensive calculator-based exploration that was largely unproductive until a conceptual cue reorganized it (e.g., linking roots to factoring) [@mesaSolvingProblemsFunctions2008].

## How to Check <!-- role: check -->

- **Visual Sign:** Long sequences of parameter tweaks with little articulation of mathematical reasons; or calculator used only at the end to “check.”
- **The Test:** Ask students to state a principle predicting the next change before they press keys; if they can’t, the calculator is functioning as unguided exploration rather than concept-driven inquiry [@mesaSolvingProblemsFunctions2008].

## How to Fix <!-- role: fix -->

- **Quick Fix:** Require a written/voiced prediction (“If we change \_\_\_, the graph should \_\_\_ because \_\_\_”) before each calculator trial.
- **Best Fix:** Embed prompts that surface the key structural relations (e.g., zeros ↔ factors; intersection points ↔ equality) so exploration is constrained by mathematics rather than resemblance alone [@mesaSolvingProblemsFunctions2008].
