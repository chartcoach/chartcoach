## Prefer engaging, distinctive designs when the goal is retention or persuasion <!-- role: advice -->

Prefer designs that encourage attention, contemplation, or distinctiveness when the success criterion is learning, retention, motivation, or persuasion rather than fast and accurate value extraction. Do not assume the most “efficient” precision-driven chart is best for these outcomes.

## Higher-level outcomes can benefit from “inefficient” visualizations <!-- role: reason -->

When a visualization’s purpose is rhetorical or educational, performance measures like speed and accuracy in reading values can be misaligned with the real goal. Visual difficulty can slow viewers and increase retention, and distinctive or pictorial features can make charts more memorable and differentiated from others. Conversely, minimalist precision-driven graphics can convey an undue sense of authority or objectivity, shaping beliefs in ways that are not captured by value-reading metrics.

**Mechanism:** Attention, depth of processing, and distinctiveness can increase retention and shape attitudes; perceived objectivity and rhetorical force can change trust and persuasion independently of numeric readability.

**Evidence:** Making decoding harder can improve retention for some goals, suggesting that “visual difficulties” can be beneficial in certain contexts [@bertiniWhyShouldntAll2020]. Visualizations with distinctive/pictorial features are more memorable than plain charts, and overly clean precision-driven designs can create an impression of authority that affects how viewers evaluate claims [@bertiniWhyShouldntAll2020].

**Notes:** This guideline concerns goal alignment: the same chart can be “worse” for lookup but “better” for memory, persuasion, or critical reflection.

## Contexts where impact beats precision <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Remember key messages, be motivated to act, or understand a narrative/lesson.
- **Task:** Learning, recall, persuasion, reflection, or communicating uncertainty/limitations.
- **Data:** Any, especially when the message is conceptual rather than exact numeric lookup.
- **Chart Setting:** Presentations, journalism, education, public communication, or advocacy.
- **Audience:** Non-expert or broad audiences where attention and recall are limiting factors.
- **Success Criterion:** Retention, differentiation, attitude change, or appropriate skepticism—not fastest accurate reads.

## Exceptions: when efficiency should dominate <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** Users must make time-critical, high-stakes decisions requiring precise value extraction. **Why:** Added difficulty or embellishment can slow reading and increase error.

## Costs: tradeoffs of designing for impact <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** You may reduce speed and accuracy of extracting specific values. **Risk:** Distinctive or rhetorical elements can be perceived as manipulative or can distract from the data. **Mitigation:** Ensure the intended message is still supported by the underlying values and provide access to exact numbers when needed.

## Mistakes: common misuses of “memorable” or “slow” design <!-- role: mistakes -->

- **Mistake:** Treating memorability or persuasion as a universal goal for all analytical contexts. **Why it fails:** It can unnecessarily degrade efficiency where precision is the actual requirement.
- **Mistake:** Adding difficulty or stylistic elements without a clear outcome metric beyond aesthetics. **Why it fails:** It may only introduce noise, not retention or understanding [@bertiniWhyShouldntAll2020].

## Check: quick tests for goal mismatch <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** The chart is easy to read but viewers quickly forget it or cannot articulate the takeaway later. **Quick Check:** Ask viewers for the main message after a delay (for example, at the end of a meeting) and see if they recall it without prompts. **Stronger Test:** Compare two designs on delayed recall or message comprehension rather than immediate value-reading accuracy.

## Fix: what to do when precision-driven design underperforms on impact <!-- role: fix -->

- Add structure that encourages contemplation (annotation, narrative framing, or layered detail) when learning is the goal.
- Make key elements visually distinctive so the chart is differentiable in memory from similar charts.
- Use rendering choices that encourage critical reflection when uncertainty or subjectivity is important.
- Pair an engaging explanatory view with a separate precise lookup view if both impact and accuracy are needed.
