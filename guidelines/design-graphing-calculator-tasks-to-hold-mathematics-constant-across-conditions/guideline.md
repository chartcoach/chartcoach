## Hold the mathematical task constant across tool conditions <!-- role: advice -->

When you compare work with and without a graphing calculator, use the same problems and keep the mathematical goals and constraints equivalent across conditions.

## Tool attribution requires comparable mathematics <!-- role: reason -->

If tool and non-tool groups receive different mathematical experiences, any difference in outcomes is confounded by curriculum differences rather than tool effects, making causal claims about the tool unreliable.

**Mechanism:** Matching tasks controls the “knowledge at stake” so differences in strategy, time, and success can be interpreted as tool-mediated rather than content-mediated.

**Evidence:** A within-task, cross-condition setup (same function problems, alternating calculator availability) revealed no major differences attributable solely to calculator presence, suggesting task/knowledge factors dominate unless controlled [@mesaSolvingProblemsFunctions2008].

**Notes:** This rule is about research and evaluation design, not about banning tools in instruction.

## Applies to tool-effect comparisons in function problem solving <!-- role: context -->

- **User Goal:** Isolate the effect of a graphing calculator on solution processes or outcomes.
- **Task:** Compare strategies, time, and success with vs. without tool support.
- **Data:** Function representations (symbolic/graphical/tabular) and problem-solving protocols.
- **Chart Setting:** Graphing-calculator environment vs. paper-and-pencil environment.
- **Audience:** Researchers, curriculum evaluators, instructors running classroom comparisons.
- **Success Criterion:** Differences can plausibly be attributed to tool availability rather than different mathematical content.

## When not to follow this rule <!-- role: exceptions -->

**Break it when:** You are evaluating an entire technology-based curriculum package as a holistic intervention rather than the isolated effect of the graphing calculator. **Why:** The intended “treatment” includes redesigned tasks and pedagogy, so holding tasks constant is no longer the evaluation target.

## Tradeoffs and risks <!-- role: costs -->

**Sacrifice:** You may reduce ecological validity relative to intact classroom curricula. **Risk:** Over-controlling tasks can understate benefits that emerge only with redesigned instruction. **Mitigation:** Pair controlled comparisons with separate studies of full curriculum implementations.

## Common failure modes <!-- role: mistakes -->

**Mistake:** Comparing a calculator-based unit to “normal instruction” and attributing score differences to the calculator. **Why it fails:** The mathematical opportunities are not comparable, so the tool is confounded with curriculum and pedagogy [@mesaSolvingProblemsFunctions2008].

## Quick tests <!-- role: check -->

**Failure Sign:** The non-calculator condition is doing different problems or a different unit. **Quick Check:** Confirm both conditions solve the same tasks with the same constraints and time expectations. **Stronger Test:** Have an external reviewer judge whether the “mathematics at stake” is equivalent across conditions.

## What to do instead <!-- role: fix -->

- Use identical problems and alternate calculator access by problem (or by session) while keeping time and instructions stable.
- Keep prompts, hints, and allowable representations consistent across conditions.
- Analyze solution protocols (strategy episodes, verification/exploration behavior) to separate tool effects from knowledge availability.
- Add a follow-up condition that changes instruction (not the tool) to test whether instruction, rather than the calculator, drives differences.
