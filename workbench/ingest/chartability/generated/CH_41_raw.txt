## Guidance

Ensure that chart geometries and large text have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against their background, and regular text has a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background.

## Why

Sufficient contrast is fundamental for readability and perceivability, especially for users with low vision, color vision deficiencies, or older adults. Low contrast significantly increases cognitive load, making it difficult for many users to identify, read, and interpret visual information, leading to frustration and potential misinterpretation of data.

### Core Principle

All essential visual information, including text and graphical elements, must be clearly distinguishable from its background to ensure perceivability for a diverse range of users.

## When it applies

-   When displaying any text (labels, annotations, titles, legends) on a chart.
-   When using graphical elements (lines, bars, points, areas, icons) to convey information.
-   In all interactive and static visualizations.
-   Whenever color is used to distinguish foreground from background elements.

## Exceptions

None, as contrast is a fundamental aspect of perceivability. While the WCAG 2.x contrast model has known limitations (as noted by Myndex Research and ongoing work for WCAG 3.0), the core principle of ensuring sufficient visual separation between elements and their backgrounds remains critical. Specific contrast ratios may vary slightly based on evolving standards, but the requirement for robust contrast is universal for accessible design.

## Trade-offs

Achieving high contrast can sometimes constrain aesthetic choices, limiting the use of very light or very dark colors within certain palettes, or requiring a more pronounced visual design than initially intended. It may also necessitate careful selection of background colors or alternative encoding strategies to maintain both accessibility and visual appeal.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The Squint Test:** If you squint at the visualization, do text labels or data elements disappear, blur, or blend into the background?
-   **Color Picker Test:** Using a color picker and a contrast checker (e.g., WebAIM's), do text elements fail to meet 4.5:1 contrast, or do non-text elements (like chart lines, bar fills, or interactive states) fail to meet 3:1 contrast against their adjacent background?
-   **User Feedback:** Users report difficulty reading text, distinguishing chart elements, or experiencing eye strain.
-   **No Clear Boundaries:** It's hard to tell where one graphical element ends and another (or the background) begins, especially for adjacent elements or subtle changes in interactive states.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick approach:** Use a contrast checker tool (like WebAIM's) to identify and adjust the most critical low-contrast text and element colors. Prioritize adjusting font colors, then the colors of primary data elements (e.g., bars, lines, dots) and their interactive states to meet minimum WCAG 2.1 AA standards (4.5:1 for regular text, 3:1 for large text and non-text elements).
-   **Moderate approach:** Implement a comprehensive design system or color palette that is pre-vetted for accessibility, ensuring all default text and element colors meet or exceed contrast requirements. Consider offering a high-contrast mode or light/dark theme toggle as a user preference to accommodate diverse visual needs.
-   **Comprehensive approach:** Adopt an accessibility-first design methodology, integrating automated contrast checks into the development pipeline. Design flexible color systems that dynamically adapt to user preferences for contrast or theme settings (e.g., OS-level high contrast modes). Continuously research and adapt to evolving contrast standards (like APCA in WCAG 3.0) to ensure future-proof accessibility.
