## Guidance

Geometries and large text must have a contrast ratio greater than 3:1 against their background. Regular text must have a contrast ratio greater than 4.5:1 against its background.

## Why

Sufficient contrast is fundamental for readability and perceivability, especially for users with low vision, certain color vision deficiencies, or those viewing content in challenging lighting conditions (e.g., bright sunlight, dim environments, reflections on screens). Poor contrast forces increased cognitive effort to distinguish elements, making information harder to process and understand, thereby increasing cognitive load.

### Core Principle

Content must be perceivable for all users, regardless of visual ability or viewing conditions.

## When it applies

- All text elements (labels, titles, annotations, legends) within a visualization.
- All graphical objects and geometries (bars, lines, points, areas) within a visualization, especially those conveying essential information.
- Any interactive elements (buttons, focus indicators) or their various states (e.g., hover, focus, selected).

## Exceptions

- Logotypes or brand names, which are primarily decorative and convey identity rather than information, are not subject to minimum contrast requirements.
- Incidental elements that are purely decorative and convey no information.
- Text that is part of an inactive user interface component.
- The WCAG 2.0/2.1 contrast model has known limitations, and newer perceptual models (like APCA) being developed for WCAG 3.0 aim to provide more perceptually accurate contrast calculations, potentially allowing for broader color choices in the future while maintaining accessibility.

## Trade-offs

- **Aesthetic Limitations:** Strictly adhering to contrast ratios may limit aesthetic or branding choices, potentially impacting the visual appeal of the visualization.
- **Color Palette Constraints:** Achieving high contrast across many elements can lead to less nuanced or vibrant color palettes, especially for complex categorical or sequential data.
- **Design System Conflicts:** May conflict with existing design systems or brand guidelines that prioritize visual design over strict accessibility contrast requirements, requiring negotiation and compromise.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Squint Test:** If you squint at the visualization, do any text or graphical elements blend into the background or become indistinguishable?
- **Text Difficulty:** Is any regular text (e.g., axis labels, legend text) difficult to read without conscious effort, even for users with normal vision, due to subtle colors?
- **Element Blurring:** Do chart geometries (bars, lines, points, fills) appear indistinct or fuzzy against their background or adjacent elements, making it hard to differentiate them?
- **Tool Failure:** Does a contrast checker tool (e.g., WebAIM Contrast Checker) report failures for text, large text, or non-text elements when tested?

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** Use a contrast checker tool (like WebAIM Contrast Checker) to identify specific text and non-text elements that fail the WCAG 2.1 contrast ratios (4.5:1 for regular text, 3:1 for large text and graphics). Adjust the foreground or background colors of these problematic elements until they meet the minimum requirements. Prioritize critical text and data-encoding elements first.
- **Moderate approach:** Redesign the color palette for the entire visualization. Utilize accessible color palette generators (e.g., Viz Palette, Chroma.js) that allow for previewing contrast and simulating color vision deficiencies. Aim to select colors that inherently provide sufficient contrast for all critical visualization components (text, data marks, backgrounds) under various conditions.
- **Comprehensive approach:** Establish a robust accessibility-first design system that mandates accessible color palettes and contrast ratios by default for all visualization components. Integrate automated contrast checks into the development workflow to catch issues early. Consider providing users with optional high-contrast themes or customization options, acknowledging that a single color scheme may not meet all individual needs perfectly, especially as new contrast evaluation models emerge.
