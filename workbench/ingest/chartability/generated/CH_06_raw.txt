## Guidance

Ensure all text and graphical elements in your visualization have sufficient contrast against their background. Specifically, regular text must meet a 4.5:1 contrast ratio, while geometries, large text (18pt/24px or 14pt/18.66px bold), and UI components require a 3:1 ratio.

## Why

Low contrast makes content difficult or impossible to perceive for users with low vision, color vision deficiencies, or even in challenging viewing conditions (e.g., bright sunlight, older screens). It significantly increases cognitive load, forcing users to strain to distinguish elements, hindering comprehension. This is a fundamental aspect of perceivability and one of the most common accessibility failures in visualizations.

### Core Principle

Perceivability: Visual information must be distinguishable by the human eye, regardless of individual sensory capabilities or environmental conditions.

## When it applies

-   All chart types containing text, labels, data marks, or interactive components.
-   When displaying information that must be clearly differentiated from its background.
-   Across all display mediums (screen, print) and viewing conditions.

## Exceptions

None known for basic contrast ratios as they are fundamental for perceivability. While advanced models like APCA (being developed for WCAG 3.0) aim for more nuanced calculations based on human perception, the 4.5:1 and 3:1 ratios are current baseline requirements that must be met.

## Trade-offs

-   May limit aesthetic color choices, potentially impacting brand adherence or preferred visual style.
-   Requires careful selection and testing of all color combinations for foreground and background elements.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The Squint Test:** Can you easily distinguish all text and graphical elements from their background when squinting? If elements blur together, contrast is likely insufficient.
-   **Faint Text/Lines:** Text or lines appear washed out or blend into the background, especially for labels, axis ticks, grid lines, or minor annotations.
-   **Indistinguishable Data Marks:** Data points, bars, or other chart geometries are hard to differentiate from the chart area or adjacent elements.
-   **Tool Failure:** A contrast checker reports failures for text (below 4.5:1) or non-text elements (below 3:1).

## How to Improve

-   **Quick approach:** Use a contrast checker (such as WebAIM Contrast Checker) to identify failing color combinations. Adjust foreground or background colors of problematic elements to meet the minimum contrast ratios. For text, increase the luminosity difference; for graphical elements, ensure sufficient distinction between the element and its surrounding colors.

-   **Moderate approach:** Integrate contrast checking into your design system or development workflow. Create and adhere to a palette of pre-approved high-contrast color combinations for all text and data elements. Prioritize color choices based on meeting accessibility standards first, then on aesthetic preferences.

-   **Comprehensive approach:** Adopt an accessibility-first approach to color. Design color palettes from the ground up with contrast in mind, ensuring all potential foreground/background combinations pass WCAG requirements. Test visualizations with a diverse group of users, including those with low vision, to validate real-world perceivability. Stay informed on emerging standards like WCAG 3.0 which may offer more perceptually accurate contrast models and incorporate them as they become stable.
