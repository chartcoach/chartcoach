## Guidance

Ensure graphical objects (geometries, icons) and large text have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against their background, and regular text has a minimum contrast ratio of 4.5:1.

## Why

Sufficient contrast is fundamental for readability and perceivability, especially for individuals with low vision, color vision deficiencies, and older adults. Failing to meet contrast requirements makes it difficult to distinguish text, lines, and shapes, leading to increased cognitive load and preventing users from understanding the visualization's content. This is empirically one of the most common accessibility failures observed in practice.

### Core Principle

Content must be perceivable by all users. Visual distinction, primarily achieved through sufficient contrast, is a prerequisite for understanding. Contrast ensures that elements are not only present but also discernable without undue effort.

## When it applies

-   Any visualization or interface containing text (labels, titles, annotations, legends) or graphical elements (bars, lines, points, backgrounds, boundaries).
-   When visual clarity is essential for understanding data relationships and trends.
-   For all audiences, especially those with low vision, certain color vision deficiencies, or cognitive disabilities where visual processing is impaired.
-   Across all mediums (print, screen, static, interactive) as the need for clear visual distinction is universal.

## Exceptions

None. While methods for measuring contrast are evolving (e.g., ongoing work for WCAG 3.0 to integrate perceptual size and a new contrast algorithm), the fundamental requirement for sufficient visual distinction remains universally applicable. Current WCAG 2.1 standards are the minimum baseline.

## Trade-offs

Applying strict contrast ratios might sometimes constrain aesthetic choices, especially when working with brand colors or subtle color palettes. However, these aesthetic trade-offs are typically outweighed by the significant gains in accessibility and usability for a broader audience, reducing barriers and cognitive effort for all.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The Squint Test:** Text or graphical elements are difficult to read or distinguish from their background when you squint.
-   **Fading Elements:** Parts of the visualization seem to blend into the background or adjacent elements, particularly in dimly lit environments, on lower-quality screens, or for users with visual impairments.
-   **Tool Failure:** Automated contrast checkers (like WebAIM Contrast Checker) report contrast ratios below 4.5:1 for regular text or 3:1 for large text and graphical objects.
-   **User Feedback:** Users report difficulty reading labels, distinguishing data points, or perceiving the boundaries of graphical elements.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick approach:** Use a contrast checker tool (e.g., WebAIM Contrast Checker) to identify problematic foreground and background color combinations. Adjust the lightness or darkness of colors to meet the minimum required ratios. Even small shifts in hue, saturation, or lightness can significantly improve contrast.
-   **Moderate approach:** Implement a robust color palette selection process that prioritizes contrast from the outset. For interactive charts, ensure that hover or focus states also maintain sufficient contrast with their surrounding elements and previous states. Consider providing a high-contrast mode as an optional user setting that can be toggled.
-   **Comprehensive approach:** Integrate contrast requirements into your design system and development workflow, making them a default consideration for all visual elements. Automate contrast checks in your continuous integration/continuous deployment (CI/CD) pipeline. Regularly review and test visualizations with diverse users, including those with low vision, to ensure real-world perceivability beyond automated checks.
