## Guidance

Geometries and large text must have at least a 3:1 contrast ratio against their background, while regular text must have at least a 4.5:1 contrast ratio.

## Why

Sufficient contrast is fundamental for readability and perceivability, especially for individuals with low vision, color vision deficiencies, or older adults. Low contrast makes it difficult to distinguish text, data points, or other critical elements from their background, leading to increased cognitive load, misinterpretation, and exclusion. Adhering to these ratios ensures that a broad range of users can access and understand the visualization's content.

### Core Principle

Perceivability: All essential visual information must be sufficiently distinguishable from its surroundings to be accessible and understood by all users.

## When it applies

- When using any text within a visualization (titles, labels, annotations, legends).
- When using graphical elements that convey meaning (lines, bars, points, areas, icons).
- Whenever foreground and background colors are used to differentiate information.

## Exceptions

None known for basic readability and perceivability of static elements. While future standards like WCAG 3.0 may incorporate size and perceptual angle into contrast calculations, the current WCAG 2.1 requirements remain a critical baseline.

## Trade-offs

A common trade-off can arise with aesthetic preferences or brand guidelines that might favor softer, lower-contrast color schemes. Achieving higher contrast may sometimes require adjusting a pre-selected color palette or slightly altering design elements, which can be perceived as an aesthetic compromise. However, the functional benefits for accessibility far outweigh these concerns.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Squint Test Failure:** When squinting at the visualization, text or graphical elements become indistinguishable from their background.
- **Unreadable Text:** Users report difficulty reading labels, titles, or annotations.
- **Ambiguous Elements:** Different data points, lines, or shapes are hard to tell apart from each other or the background, even for users without visual impairments.
- **Automated Audit Failure:** Tools like WebAIM Contrast Checker report contrast ratios below 4.5:1 for regular text or 3:1 for large text/graphics.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** Use a contrast checker tool (e.g., WebAIM Contrast Checker) to identify specific foreground-background color pairs that fail the requirements. Adjust the lightness/darkness of the offending colors until they meet the minimum contrast ratios. For elements that must maintain specific hues (e.g., brand colors), consider altering the background color or adding a high-contrast border.

- **Moderate approach:** Integrate contrast checking into your design and development workflow. Select color palettes with accessibility in mind from the outset, using tools like Viz Palette or Chroma to preview contrast and color vision deficiency simulations. Prioritize legibility over subtle aesthetics, particularly for critical information.

- **Comprehensive approach:** Design for flexible contrast options, allowing users to select high-contrast themes if needed. Explore alternative encoding methods (e.g., textures, shapes, patterns) in addition to color, which can provide redundancy and improve distinguishability even when color contrast is limited. Stay informed about upcoming standards like WCAG 3.0 which may offer more nuanced guidance on perceptual contrast.
