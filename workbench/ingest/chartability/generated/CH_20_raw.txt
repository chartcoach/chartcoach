## Guidance

Ensure geometries and large text have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against the background, and regular text has a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1.

## Why

Low contrast is a primary barrier for users with low vision, certain color vision deficiencies, or older users, making text and graphical elements difficult or impossible to read and distinguish. Adhering to contrast standards significantly improves readability and accessibility for a broad audience by reducing visual strain and improving content recognition.

### Core Principle

Ensure perceivability for all users by providing sufficient contrast between foreground and background elements, reducing visual strain and improving readability.

## When it applies

-   When displaying any text in a visualization (labels, titles, annotations, legends).
-   When using graphical elements like bars, lines, shapes, or interactive components in a visualization.
-   When designing for users with low vision or certain color vision deficiencies.
-   When creating interactive components where focus, hover, or selected states change color.

## Exceptions

-   Logotypes or brand names, though adhering to contrast is still recommended for maximum accessibility.
-   Incidental elements that are purely decorative and convey no information.
-   Subtle, non-essential visual effects that do not communicate meaningful data or function.

## Trade-offs

-   May limit aesthetic color choices, especially for subtle branding or complex palettes with many distinct colors.
-   Achieving high contrast might sometimes conflict with other visual design goals like visual harmony or data density if not carefully managed.
-   Strict adherence can sometimes make it challenging to use a wide range of colors in data encoding, requiring designers to be more creative with other visual channels like texture or shape.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The Squint Test:** If you squint at the visualization, do text or important graphical elements (like bars, lines, or points) become indistinguishable from their background or from adjacent elements?
-   **Automated Scan Warnings:** Accessibility checkers like WebAIM Contrast Checker, Axe-core, or Lighthouse report contrast failures for text or non-text elements.
-   **Subtle Color Blending:** Adjacent colors in a legend, tooltips, or chart elements are very close in luminosity, making them hard to differentiate.
-   **Readability Complaints:** Users report difficulty reading labels, titles, or distinguishing chart elements and interactive components.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick approach:** Use a contrast checker (e.g., WebAIM Contrast Checker) to identify failing color combinations. Adjust the foreground or background colors (e.g., make text darker, background lighter) until minimum WCAG ratios are met. Prioritize text and critical data marks first.
-   **Moderate approach:** Redesign the color palette for the entire visualization with accessibility in mind from the outset. Consider switching to a high-contrast theme or dark mode option if appropriate. Utilize tools like Viz Palette or Chroma.js to preview and ensure contrast across the full range of colors used for data representation.
-   **Comprehensive approach:** Integrate contrast checks into your design system and development pipeline to prevent issues early. Provide users with configurable contrast settings or allow them to choose between several pre-defined accessible themes. Stay informed about upcoming standards like WCAG 3.0 (APCA) which offer a more perceptually accurate contrast model. For complex scenarios, consider redundant encodings (e.g., adding text labels or shapes) to supplement color.
