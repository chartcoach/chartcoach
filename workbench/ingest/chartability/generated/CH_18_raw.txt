## Guidance

All text and significant graphical elements (e.g., chart geometries, icons, interactive controls) must meet minimum contrast ratios against their background: regular text (under 18pt or 24px) requires a 4.5:1 contrast ratio, while large text (18pt/24px or larger) and graphical objects require a 3:1 contrast ratio.

## Why

User must be able to easily identify content using their senses: sight, sound, and touch. Ensuring adequate contrast is fundamental for visual perception, particularly for people with low vision, color vision deficiencies, or age-related vision changes. Low contrast increases cognitive load, making it harder and slower for all users to distinguish information, read labels, and understand visual relationships within a chart. This is the most common accessibility failure in data visualizations, significantly hindering inclusive design.

### Core Principle

Perceivability means that information and user interface components must be presentable to users in ways they can perceive. Contrast is a primary factor in the perceivability of visual content, directly impacting readability and discriminability.

## When it applies

-   Whenever text is used in a visualization (titles, labels, annotations, legends, etc.).
-   Whenever graphic elements convey meaning (bars, lines, scatter points, map regions, icons, interactive states).
-   When a visualization is intended for a broad audience, including those with low vision or cognitive impairments.
-   On all digital mediums (web, applications, mobile).

## Exceptions

-   **Logotypes or brand names:** Text that is part of a logotype or brand name is exempt.
-   **Purely decorative elements:** Inactive or purely decorative graphical objects that do not convey information are exempt.
-   **Incidental elements:** Images or parts of images where the specific appearance of the text or graphics is not essential to the information conveyed (e.g., a photograph containing text, or a complex visualization where an individual data point is very small).
-   **Future Standards:** Note that current WCAG 2.x contrast models have known limitations, and future standards like WCAG 3.0 are expected to use a more perceptually accurate contrast calculation (e.g., APCA/SPCA). However, until such standards are formalized, current WCAG 2.1 requirements must be met.

## Trade-offs

-   **Aesthetic choices:** Adhering to contrast ratios may sometimes limit aesthetic color choices, requiring designers to prioritize readability over purely stylistic preferences.
-   **Brand guidelines:** Strict brand color palettes may need adjustments or supplementary accessible alternatives to meet contrast requirements.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **"The Squint Test":** If you squint your eyes and elements (text or graphics) blend into the background or each other, contrast is likely too low.
-   **Failing a Contrast Checker:** Using a tool like WebAIM Contrast Checker (linked above) reports a ratio below 4.5:1 for regular text or 3:1 for large text/graphics.
-   **Eye Strain:** Users report difficulty reading text or distinguishing elements, or experience eye strain, especially after prolonged viewing.
-   **Common Audit Failure:** Your visualization design repeatedly fails automated or manual accessibility audits for contrast issues (Elavsky et al., 2022 found this to be 88% of audits).

## How to Improve

-   **Quick approach: Adjust Color Values:** Use a contrast checker tool (like WebAIM Contrast Checker) to test foreground and background color pairs. Incrementally adjust the lightness/darkness of either the foreground or background color until the minimum contrast ratio is met. Prioritize adjusting background colors if the foreground color is part of a semantic color palette.

-   **Moderate approach: Revise Color Palette and Text Styles:** Re-evaluate your entire color palette, ensuring all primary and secondary colors have sufficient contrast when paired with common backgrounds. Increase font sizes to utilize the more lenient 3:1 ratio for "large text" (18pt/24px or larger). Ensure any interactive states (hover, focus, selected) also meet contrast requirements against their default state.

-   **Comprehensive approach: Design with Accessibility First:** Integrate contrast requirements into the initial design phase. Develop an accessible design system where all default color pairings, text styles, and interactive elements are pre-vetted for contrast. Provide users with options to adjust contrast or switch to high-contrast themes within the application, demonstrating commitment to flexible presentation.
