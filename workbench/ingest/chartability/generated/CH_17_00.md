## Guidance

Ensure regular text (smaller than 18pt/24px or 14pt/18.5px bold) has a minimum contrast ratio of 4.5:1 against its background. Large text and graphical objects (such as chart geometries, icons, and interactive elements) must have a minimum contrast ratio of 3:1 against adjacent colors or their background.

## Why

Adequate contrast is fundamental for readability and perceivability, especially for users with low vision, age-related vision changes, or certain color vision deficiencies. Low contrast significantly increases cognitive load for all users and can make critical information indiscernible, contributing to it being the most common accessibility failure in data visualizations (Elavsky et al., 2022). Failing to meet these standards makes visualizations effectively unusable for a significant portion of the audience.

### Core Principle

Content must be easily identifiable using senses. Visual elements must be sufficiently distinguishable from their surroundings and each other to be perceived accurately by all users, minimizing effort and potential for misinterpretation.

## When it applies

- To all text, user interface components, and graphical objects (e.g., lines, bars, points, axes, legends) within any visualization.
- Across all chart types, data types, tasks, and mediums (static, interactive, print, screen).
- For all audiences, particularly those with low vision or other visual impairments.
- When interactivity changes an element's state or color, the new state must also meet contrast requirements.

## Exceptions

While current WCAG 2.1 standards are well-established and widely adopted, ongoing research for WCAG 3.0 (e.g., APCA/SPCA work) is exploring new, perceptually-based contrast models. These models may offer more nuanced evaluations in the future. However, for now, strict adherence to WCAG 2.1 guidelines is the accepted best practice for ensuring broad accessibility.

## Trade-offs

Strict adherence to contrast ratios may sometimes limit purely aesthetic choices or necessitate adjustments to established branding palettes. Designers might feel constrained in their color selection. However, the primary trade-off is often between subjective visual aesthetics and fundamental perceivability and inclusivity. Creative design solutions can often bridge this gap, maintaining brand identity while meeting accessibility needs.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The Squint Test:** If you squint at the visualization, do essential text labels, data points, or chart elements blend into the background or become hard to distinguish from adjacent elements?
-   **Tool Failure:** A contrast checker reports ratios below 4.5:1 for regular text or 3:1 for large text/graphical components.
-   **User Feedback:** Users report difficulty reading labels, discerning chart elements, experiencing eyestrain, or needing to zoom in excessively to read content.
-   **Implicit Information Loss:** Critical data patterns or comparisons conveyed through graphical elements are difficult to perceive for some users, even if the general shape is visible.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick approach:**
    Use a contrast checker (e.g., WebAIM Contrast Checker) to identify all elements failing contrast requirements. For text, incrementally adjust foreground or background colors until a 4.5:1 ratio is met. For graphical elements, aim for 3:1. Prioritize critical labels, interactive elements, and data marks that convey primary information.

-   **Moderate approach:**
    Redesign your color palette from the ground up, starting with WCAG-compliant color pairs. When choosing colors for text, backgrounds, and graphical elements, explicitly verify their contrast ratios. For interactive elements, ensure hover, focus, and selected states also maintain or improve contrast (e.g., a hovered element should not have *lower* contrast than its default state).

-   **Comprehensive approach:**
    Integrate contrast checking directly into your design system and automated testing pipelines to proactively prevent failures. Provide built-in high-contrast theme options for users. Beyond color adjustments, consider redundant encodings like textures, patterns, or distinct shapes for critical graphical elements to further enhance perceivability, especially for color-encoded data, even when color contrast ratios are met.
