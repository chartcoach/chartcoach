## Guidance

Ensure all text and visual elements within a data visualization have sufficient contrast against their background. Specifically, geometries and large text (18pt/24px regular, or 14pt/18.66px bold) must have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1, and regular text must meet a minimum contrast ratio of 4.5:1.

## Why

This guideline is fundamental to perceivability, ensuring that users can easily identify and distinguish content using their sight. Low contrast makes text and graphical elements difficult to read for everyone, and impossible for individuals with low vision, color vision deficiencies, or those viewing content on suboptimal screens or in challenging lighting conditions. Meeting contrast requirements reduces cognitive load and improves readability, making the visualization accessible to a broader audience. It is one of the most common failures in accessibility audits, indicating its critical importance and frequent oversight.

### Core Principle

Visual information must be sufficiently distinct from its background to be accurately and effortlessly perceived by the human eye, regardless of individual visual acuity or environmental conditions.

## When it applies

-   Whenever text is displayed, including titles, labels, annotations, and legends.
-   Whenever graphical elements (geometries, lines, icons, interactive states) are used to convey information or are essential for understanding.
-   Across all chart types and interactive components.
-   For all audiences, especially those with low vision, age-related vision changes, or when content is consumed on varied devices and lighting.

## Exceptions

None known for the fundamental requirement of sufficient contrast. While specific contrast models (like WCAG 2.1 vs. upcoming WCAG 3.0 APCA) may evolve, the principle of ensuring perceivable distinction between foreground and background remains paramount.

## Trade-offs

-   **Aesthetics vs. Accessibility:** Achieving high contrast may sometimes constrain color choices, potentially impacting desired brand aesthetics or complex color schemes. Finding accessible color palettes often requires careful design.
-   **Color Encoding:** Relying on subtle color differences to encode data can inadvertently lead to low contrast issues, especially if the palette is not explicitly designed with contrast in mind.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The Squint Test:** If you squint at your visualization, and parts of the text or important data points disappear or blend into the background.
-   **Contrast Checker Failure:** A contrast checker tool (e.g., WebAIM Contrast Checker) reports ratios below 4.5:1 for regular text or 3:1 for large text/non-text elements.
-   **User Complaints:** Feedback from users indicating difficulty reading text or distinguishing chart elements.
-   **Color Palette Issues:** The color palette for your data visualization relies on colors that are visually close in luminance.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick approach:** Use a contrast checker tool to identify problematic foreground/background color pairs. Adjust the lightness/darkness of either the text/element color or the background color until the required contrast ratio is met. Prioritize adjusting colors that are causing the most significant legibility issues.

-   **Moderate approach:** Re-evaluate your entire color palette, selecting colors that inherently offer better contrast. Consider using a default high-contrast mode for your visualization or providing a toggle for users to switch to a high-contrast theme. For non-text elements, ensure they have clear outlines or borders if their fill color has low contrast with the background.

-   **Comprehensive approach:** Design and implement a robust theming system that allows for multiple accessible color schemes (e.g., light, dark, high-contrast, specific colorblind-friendly modes). Incorporate contrast checking directly into your design or development workflow. For interactive visualizations, ensure that focus states, hover states, and selections also meet contrast requirements against their inactive states.
