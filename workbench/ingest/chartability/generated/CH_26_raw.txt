## Guidance

Ensure geometries and large text (larger than 18pt or 14pt bold) have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against their background, and regular text has a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1.

## Why

Sufficient contrast is fundamental for perceivability and accessibility, especially for users with low vision, certain color vision deficiencies, or older adults. It allows users to clearly distinguish important information, data points, and interactive elements from their background, reducing cognitive load and improving comprehension. Without adequate contrast, visual elements can blend together, making a visualization difficult or impossible to interpret.

### Core Principle

Information must be perceivable through basic sensory input; visual elements must be distinct enough to be recognized by sight and understood.

## When it applies

-   When using any text (labels, annotations, titles, axis tick values), geometries (bars, lines, points, areas), or graphical objects (icons, interactive states) in a data visualization.
-   For both static and interactive elements, ensuring all meaningful content is differentiable from its background.

## Exceptions

WCAG 2.1 contrast ratios are the current widely adopted standard. However, ongoing work for WCAG 3.0, including research like APCA/SPCA, aims to integrate more perceptually accurate models that account for size and other factors, potentially leading to different recommendations in the future. Until WCAG 3.0 is finalized and widely adopted (expected 2025 or later), current best practice is to adhere to WCAG 2.1.

## Trade-offs

Strict adherence to contrast ratios can sometimes limit aesthetic choices or restrict the use of certain brand colors. Designers may need to adjust color palettes, introduce non-color encodings (e.g., textures, patterns) for redundancy, or use different shades of brand colors to maintain both accessibility and visual appeal. This may require additional design and development effort.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The Squint Test:** If you squint at the visualization, do text or chart elements become difficult to discern or blend into the background?
-   **Unreadable Text:** Text (titles, labels, annotations) is difficult to read or requires excessive effort to distinguish from its background.
-   **Indistinguishable Elements:** Data points, lines, or areas are hard to differentiate from their surroundings or from each other.
-   **Automated Audit Failures:** Automated accessibility checkers (like WebAIM Contrast Checker or Lighthouse) report contrast failures for text or graphical elements.
-   **User Feedback:** Users report difficulty reading text or distinguishing visual components.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick approach:** Use a contrast checker tool (e.g., WebAIM Contrast Checker) to evaluate the contrast ratio between foreground (text, element) and background colors. Adjust one or both colors by changing their lightness or darkness until they meet the 4.5:1 ratio for regular text and 3:1 for large text/graphical objects.
-   **Moderate approach:** When color is a primary encoding for data, implement non-color visual encodings (e.g., textures, patterns, varying line weights, distinct shapes) *in addition* to color. This provides redundancy, especially for elements where achieving high color contrast is challenging or when differentiating categories for users with color vision deficiencies.
-   **Comprehensive approach:** Integrate contrast checking and accessible color palette selection into the initial design system and development workflow. Prioritize palettes that are rigorously tested to meet WCAG standards across various states (e.g., normal, hover, selected) and contexts. Consider offering user-selectable high-contrast modes within the application.
