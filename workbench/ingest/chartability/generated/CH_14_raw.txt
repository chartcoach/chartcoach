## Guidance

Ensure all text and visual elements in your visualization have sufficient contrast against their background. Regular text must meet a 4.5:1 contrast ratio, while geometries and large text should achieve at least a 3:1 ratio.

## Why

Adequate contrast is fundamental for perceivability, particularly for users with low vision or certain color vision deficiencies. Without sufficient contrast, critical information such as labels, data points, and interactive elements can become indistinguishable from the background or other elements, leading to misinterpretation or complete loss of access. This also reduces cognitive load for all users by making elements easier to discern at a glance.

### Core Principle

Information in a visualization must be discriminable and legible for all users to prevent perceptual and cognitive barriers. Sufficient contrast ensures that visual elements can be reliably perceived and understood.

## When it applies

-   To all text content within a visualization, including titles, labels, annotations, and legends.
-   To all functional and meaningful non-text elements, such as chart geometries (e.g., bars, lines, points), icons, and interactive controls.
-   When creating visualizations for any audience, especially those with low vision or older adults who may experience reduced contrast sensitivity.

## Exceptions

-   Purely decorative elements that convey no information are not strictly required to meet contrast ratios, though good practice often extends to them for aesthetic consistency.
-   Logotypes or brand names, which are not interactive and do not convey information, may be exempt.
-   The current WCAG 2.x contrast model has known limitations (e.g., with light text on dark backgrounds or certain colors); future standards (WCAG 3.0, APCA) aim to address these but are not yet universally adopted.

## Trade-offs

-   Adhering to strict contrast ratios may limit aesthetic color choices or require adjustment of brand palettes.
-   Achieving high contrast across complex multi-layered visualizations can be challenging, potentially leading to a more utilitarian visual style.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The Squint Test:** If you squint or blur your vision, do essential text or chart elements disappear or merge into the background?
-   **Washed Out:** Do elements appear 'washed out' or indistinct, especially when compared to surrounding interface elements?
-   **Tool Warnings:** Does a contrast checker (e.g., WebAIM) report failures for text or significant graphic components?
-   **User Feedback:** Are users reporting difficulty reading text or distinguishing between data series?

## How to Improve

-   **Quick approach:** Use a contrast checker tool (e.g., WebAIM Contrast Checker) to identify failing color combinations. Adjust foreground or background colors incrementally until the required 4.5:1 (for regular text) or 3:1 (for large text/geometries) ratio is met. Prioritize high-contrast text and key interactive elements.
-   **Moderate approach:** Adopt a pre-vetted color palette known for its accessibility and contrast properties, such as those designed for colorblind-friendliness which often inherently manage contrast better. Design your visualization with distinct foreground and background layers, ensuring elements never compete for attention by having similar luminance.
-   **Comprehensive approach:** Integrate contrast checking into your design system and development workflow. Automate checks during design and build phases. Offer user-configurable themes (e.g., dark mode, high contrast mode) that ensure all elements respect contrast requirements by adjusting dynamically. Consider alternative encodings (e.g., textures, shapes) to redundantly convey information alongside color, further enhancing perceivability.
