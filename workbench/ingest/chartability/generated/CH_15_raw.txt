## Guidance

Ensure geometries and large text have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against their background, and regular text has a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1.

## Why

Poor contrast makes visual content difficult or impossible to perceive for a significant portion of the population, including people with low vision, color vision deficiencies, dyslexia, or those experiencing temporary visual impairments (e.g., glare, fatigue). Meeting these contrast requirements is fundamental for basic perceivability and is a core accessibility standard. Low contrast is a prevalent accessibility failure in data visualizations.

### Core Principle

Perceivability: All information and user interface components must be presentable to users in ways they can perceive, regardless of sensory abilities or environmental conditions.

## When it applies

- When designing any visual content, including text, chart geometries (bars, lines, points), axes, legends, and interactive elements.
- For all visualization types and contexts, whether static or interactive, print or screen.
- When the audience may include individuals with low vision, color vision deficiencies, or cognitive impairments.

## Exceptions

- **Logotypes or branding:** Text that is part of a logo or brand name does not require a minimum contrast ratio.
- **Purely decorative elements:** Visual elements that are purely decorative, convey no information, and are not essential for understanding the content, do not require a minimum contrast ratio.
- **Incidental text/images:** Text or images that are part of a complex image, such as a photograph, and are not intended to convey specific information (e.g., text on a billboard in a background photo), are exempt.
- **Unfocused states:** Components in an inactive state (e.g., a disabled button) do not have contrast requirements.

## Trade-offs

- **Aesthetic limitations:** Adhering to strict contrast ratios may limit the aesthetic range of color palettes, especially for categorical data where many distinct colors are needed.
- **Branding constraints:** Existing brand guidelines might not always align with accessibility contrast requirements, necessitating compromise or creative solutions.
- **Visual complexity:** Adding outlines or other visual cues to achieve contrast can sometimes introduce visual noise if not implemented carefully.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Eyestrain or difficulty reading:** Users report fatigue or struggle to read axis labels, annotations, or tooltips.
- **Elements blend into background:** Chart lines, bars, or points appear to merge with the background or other adjacent elements.
- **Automated contrast checker failures:** Tools like WebAIM Contrast Checker report contrast ratios below WCAG 2.1 AA thresholds for text (4.5:1) or non-text elements (3:1).
- **Difficulty distinguishing interactive states:** It's hard to tell when an element is hovered, focused, or selected due to insufficient contrast change.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:**
  - Use a contrast checker (e.g., WebAIM Contrast Checker) to identify failing color combinations.
  - Adjust the lightness or darkness of foreground or background colors minimally until they meet the required WCAG 2.1 contrast ratios (4.5:1 for regular text, 3:1 for large text and non-text elements). Prioritize increasing contrast for small text and critical information.
  - If direct color changes are not feasible (e.g., due to brand guidelines), consider adding a thin, high-contrast outline or shadow to text or graphical elements to improve their separation from the background.

- **Moderate approach:**
  - Revise the visualization's color palette using an accessibility-conscious color palette generator or validator (e.g., Viz Palette, Chroma.js) to ensure all color combinations, especially those used for text and critical chart elements, meet contrast standards.
  - For interactive elements, design distinct visual states (hover, focus, selected) that clearly meet the 3:1 contrast requirement against the default state, ideally using both color and non-color (e.g., thickness, outline) indicators.
  - For complex or dense visualizations, ensure overlapping or adjacent elements have sufficient contrast or clear separators (e.g., 1px whitespace, distinct borders).

- **Comprehensive approach:**
  - Integrate accessibility contrast guidelines directly into the organization's design system and component libraries, making compliant color choices the default.
  - Develop separate, optimized color palettes for different contexts (e.g., light mode, dark mode, high-contrast mode) that users can select, and ensure the visualization adapts dynamically.
  - Conduct user testing with individuals who have diverse visual abilities to gather direct feedback on perceived contrast and readability.
  - Implement programmatic checks in the development pipeline to flag contrast issues before deployment, ensuring consistent adherence to accessibility standards.
