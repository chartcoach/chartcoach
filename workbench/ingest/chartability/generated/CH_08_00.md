## Guidance

Geometries and large text (18pt or 14pt bold and larger) must have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against their background. Regular text must have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background.

## Why

Low contrast is a pervasive accessibility issue, impacting users with low vision, cognitive impairments, and older adults by making content difficult to perceive and distinguish. It is the most common failure observed in visualization accessibility audits (Elavsky et al., 2022). Ensuring sufficient contrast makes all visual content, including data points, labels, and interactive elements, more easily identifiable and reduces the cognitive strain required for reading and understanding the visualization.

### Core Principle

Perceivability is fundamental for all users to identify content and extract meaning using their senses. When visual elements lack sufficient contrast, they become imperceptible, making the information inaccessible.

## When it applies

This guideline applies to all visual text and graphical elements within any data visualization, regardless of chart type, data type, or interaction level. This includes, but is not limited to, axis labels, titles, data labels, legend text, chart geometries (bars, lines, points), and interactive states.

## Exceptions

While WCAG 2.1 provides the current standard for contrast, ongoing research (e.g., Myndex Research's APCA/SPCA work) and future updates (e.g., WCAG 3.0) may introduce more perceptually uniform contrast evaluation models. These may alter specific numerical ratio requirements in the future, but the core principle of ensuring clear perceivable contrast will remain essential. Additionally, incidental elements (e.g., decorative borders that convey no information) are exempt, as are active user interface components that have not been customized by the author (relying on user agent defaults).

## Trade-offs

Achieving sufficient contrast can sometimes limit purely aesthetic choices in color palettes. Designers may need to prioritize functional clarity and accessibility over highly stylized or low-contrast color schemes. However, many effective and aesthetically pleasing high-contrast palettes exist.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The Squint Test:** If you squint or view the visualization from a distance, do text or graphical elements blend into the background or become difficult to discern?
-   **Automated Audit Failure:** Does an accessibility checker (like WebAIM Contrast Checker) report contrast failures for any text or non-text elements within the visualization?
-   **User Feedback:** Users report difficulty reading labels, distinguishing chart elements, or experiencing eye strain or fatigue while interpreting the visualization.
-   **"Ghosted" Elements:** Data points or lines appear very faint or almost invisible against the background, even to users with good vision.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick approach:** Use a contrast checker tool (e.g., WebAIM Contrast Checker) to test foreground and background color combinations for all text and graphical elements. Adjust colors as needed until they meet or exceed the 3:1 (for large text/geometries) and 4.5:1 (for regular text) WCAG 2.1 AA requirements.

-   **Moderate approach:** When selecting new color palettes, prioritize contrast from the outset. Design color systems where primary data colors and text colors are tested against all possible backgrounds. Consider providing users with alternative high-contrast themes or options to switch to a dark mode.

-   **Comprehensive approach:** Integrate automated contrast testing into your development and design workflow. Beyond numerical ratios, conduct user testing with individuals with low vision to ensure real-world perceivability and gather qualitative feedback. Stay updated on emerging contrast standards (e.g., WCAG 3.0's APCA method) for more perceptually accurate evaluations that reflect how humans actually perceive contrast.
