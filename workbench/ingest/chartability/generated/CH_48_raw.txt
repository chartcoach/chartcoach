## Guidance

Geometries and large text must have a contrast ratio of greater than 3:1 against their background. Regular text (including labels, titles, and annotations) must have a contrast ratio of greater than 4.5:1 against its background.

## Why

Ensuring sufficient contrast is fundamental for readability and accessibility. It allows users, particularly those with low vision or color vision deficiencies, to easily distinguish between text, data elements, and the chart's background. Failure to meet these standards significantly impedes comprehension and makes the visualization unusable for a large segment of the audience.

### Core Principle

Visual information must be discriminable. The human visual system requires a minimum level of luminance difference to perceive distinct elements, especially for fine details like text and small graphical objects.

## When it applies

- When displaying any text (labels, titles, annotations, legends) within a visualization.
- When using graphical elements (bars, lines, points, areas) that convey meaningful information.
- For all visualizations, regardless of context, data type, or audience, as it's a foundational accessibility requirement.

## Exceptions

None (for WCAG 2.1 standards). While future standards like WCAG 3.0 may introduce more nuanced contrast models that account for size and other factors, current guidelines unequivocally require these minimum ratios.

## Trade-offs

Strict adherence to contrast ratios can sometimes limit aesthetic choices or brand color palettes. Designers might need to adjust their preferred colors or introduce additional visual cues (e.g., textures, borders) to compensate for color-based distinctions if contrast is difficult to achieve.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Squint Test:** If you squint at the visualization, do the text or graphical elements disappear or blend into the background?
- **Tool Failure:** A contrast checker tool reports a ratio below 4.5:1 for regular text or 3:1 for large text/graphical objects.
- **User Feedback:** Users report difficulty reading labels or distinguishing data points, especially in varying light conditions or on different screens.
- **High Failure Rate:** This is statistically the most common accessibility failure in data visualization, suggesting a general blind spot in design processes.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** Use a contrast checker (like WebAIM's) to identify all problematic foreground/background combinations. Adjust the luminance of the foreground or background color until the minimum required ratio is met. Prioritize critical text and interactive elements.
- **Moderate approach:** Beyond individual elements, develop a high-contrast color palette for your entire visualization system. Ensure that all standard text styles, element colors, and interactive states meet WCAG requirements by default. Provide alternatives (e.g., textures) for categorical color schemes in case contrast is insufficient or for users with color vision deficiencies.
- **Comprehensive approach:** Integrate contrast checking into your design system and development pipeline as an automated test. Educate designers and developers on the importance of contrast and provide them with pre-approved accessible palettes. Continuously monitor and adapt to evolving standards, such as the upcoming WCAG 3.0, which promises more perceptually accurate contrast models.
