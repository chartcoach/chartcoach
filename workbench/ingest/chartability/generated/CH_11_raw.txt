## Guidance

Geometries and large text must have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against their background. Regular text (smaller than 18pt/24px or 14pt/18.66px bold) must have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background.

## Why

Low contrast is a primary barrier to content readability and comprehension, particularly for individuals with low vision, color vision deficiencies, or older users. Sufficient contrast ensures that all users can easily distinguish textual and graphical information from their backgrounds, reducing visual strain and the cognitive load required to interpret the visualization. It is a foundational aspect of accessible design.

### Core Principle

Ensure distinguishability for all users by providing clear visual separation between elements and their backgrounds.

## When it applies

-   Any data visualization containing textual labels (titles, axis labels, legends, annotations, tooltips) or graphical elements (bars, lines, scatter points, map regions, borders, icons).
-   For all audiences, especially those with low vision, certain color vision deficiencies, or presbyopia (age-related vision changes).
-   Across all mediums (static images, interactive web charts, printed materials, screen displays).

## Exceptions

-   Decorative elements that convey no information may be exempt from these contrast requirements.
-   Logotypes (text that is part of a brand name or logo) are exempt.
-   Incidental elements or parts of a graphical object that are not required for understanding (e.g., a background texture that doesn't convey data).

## Trade-offs

-   Strict adherence to contrast ratios can sometimes limit aesthetic choices or require adjustments to established brand-specific color palettes.
-   Achieving the desired contrast might necessitate compromising on certain visual styles or effects if not carefully integrated into the design process.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The Squint Test:** If you squint at the visualization, do text labels or data elements blend into their background, making them hard to differentiate or causing visual fatigue?
-   **Unclear Distinctions:** Are chart elements (e.g., bar segments, map regions, line paths) difficult to tell apart from each other or from the overall chart background?
-   **Tool Failure:** A contrast checker tool (like WebAIM's) indicates a failure for any text or non-text element against its background, meaning it does not meet the 4.5:1 or 3:1 ratio.
-   **User Feedback:** Users report difficulty reading text, seeing chart elements clearly, or experience visual strain or headaches after viewing the content.
-   **Common Failure:** Elavsky et al. (2022) found low contrast to be the most common accessibility failure in data visualization audits, suggesting it's a frequent overlooked issue.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick approach:**
    1.  Use a contrast checker tool (e.g., WebAIM Contrast Checker) to identify all failing foreground/background color pairs.
    2.  Adjust either the foreground (text/element) color or the background color slightly to meet the minimum required contrast ratio (4.5:1 for regular text, 3:1 for large text/geometries).
    3.  Prioritize critical textual information (titles, labels) and primary data elements first, as these are essential for basic comprehension.

-   **Moderate approach:**
    1.  Integrate contrast checking into your design system or development workflow using automated tools.
    2.  Select color palettes that are inherently high-contrast or provide accessible alternatives for different themes (e.g., light mode, dark mode, high-contrast mode).
    3.  For graphical elements, consider using additional visual cues like borders, outlines, or distinct textures alongside color to aid distinguishability, especially for elements with marginal contrast or for users with specific color vision deficiencies.

-   **Comprehensive approach:**
    1.  Adopt a design-first approach to accessibility by integrating contrast considerations from the initial wireframing and color palette selection stages.
    2.  Develop and document specific high-contrast themes or modes for your visualizations that users can toggle based on their preferences.
    3.  Continually test your visualizations with a diverse group of users, including those with low vision, to validate readability and usability in real-world scenarios.
    4.  Stay informed about evolving accessibility standards, such as ongoing work for WCAG 3.0 (APCA/SPCA models), which aims to provide more perceptually accurate contrast evaluation, and prepare to adapt as these become standard.
