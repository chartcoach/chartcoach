## Guidance

Ensure text and visual elements have sufficient contrast against their background.

*   Geometries and large text (18pt / 24px regular, or 14pt / 18.66px bold) must have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against their background.
*   Regular text must have a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background.

## Why

Sufficient contrast is fundamental for readability and perceivability, especially for users with low vision, color vision deficiencies, or those experiencing visual fatigue. It reduces the cognitive effort required to distinguish elements and understand the visualization. This principle ensures that content can be easily identified and differentiated by a broad range of users.

### Core Principle

Humans need to be able to easily distinguish content using their senses; adequate contrast ensures visual information is perceivable.

## When it applies

*   When using any text, labels, or annotations in a visualization.
*   When using any graphical elements such as bars, lines, shapes, icons, or interactive components.
*   When selecting color palettes for data encoding, backgrounds, or foreground elements.
*   For all chart types, data types, and audiences, across all mediums (static, interactive, print, screen).

## Exceptions

None known regarding the fundamental need for sufficient contrast. However, the exact methods for measuring contrast and specific ratio requirements are subject to ongoing research and evolving standards (e.g., future WCAG 3.0 models may use different calculations like APCA/SPCA).

## Trade-offs

Achieving high contrast can sometimes constrain color palette choices, requiring careful selection to maintain both distinction for data categories and aesthetic appeal. In some cases, meeting strict contrast requirements might reduce the total number of distinct colors available in a palette, which could be a consideration for visualizations with a very high number of categorical variables.

## Signs of Trouble

*   **The Squint Test:** If you squint at your chart, do different text labels or graphical elements blend into the background or each other, making them difficult to discern?
*   **Faint Text/Elements:** Text or graphical components appear faded, muted, or blend into the background, especially in lighter or darker themes or under varying lighting conditions.
*   **Automated Audit Failure:** Accessibility checker tools (e.g., WebAIM Contrast Checker) report failures for text or non-text contrast ratios.
*   **User Feedback:** Users explicitly report difficulty reading text, distinguishing visual elements, or perceiving data points.
*   **Common Audit Failure:** Low contrast is a very common accessibility failure, indicating it's a frequent oversight in design.

## How to Improve

*   **Quick approach:**
    Immediately use a contrast checker tool (e.g., WebAIM Contrast Checker) to test all foreground/background color pairs for text and non-text elements. Adjust colors until they meet the minimum 4.5:1 ratio for regular text and 3:1 for large text and graphical elements. Prioritize critical text (titles, labels) and interactive components. This is a pragmatic workaround for existing designs.

*   **Moderate approach:**
    Integrate contrast checking into your design system and development workflow. Establish a palette of WCAG-compliant colors for text, backgrounds, and data elements, ensuring sufficient contrast across all themes (e.g., light and dark modes). Automate contrast checks in design tools or during development to catch issues early.

*   **Comprehensive approach:**
    Make contrast a foundational principle of your visualization design. Design color palettes with accessibility in mind from the outset, considering not just hue but also lightness and saturation to ensure high contrast. Explore providing user-adjustable contrast themes or alternative visual treatments (e.g., outlines, textures in addition to color) to enhance perceivability for a wider range of users, moving beyond simple color contrast for more robust solutions.
