## Guidance

Ensure geometries and large text in your visualization have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against their background, and regular text maintains a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 against its background.

## Why

Sufficient contrast is fundamental for the perceivability of visual information, allowing users to easily identify content using their sight. It is crucial for people with low vision, color vision deficiencies, and certain cognitive disabilities to distinguish between different elements, text, and their backgrounds. This reduces cognitive load and improves overall readability and comprehension.

### Core Principle

Visual information must be perceivable and distinguishable for all users, regardless of visual acuity or color perception.

## When it applies

- When displaying any text, labels, or annotations within a visualization.
- When using graphical objects (geometries, icons, UI components) to convey information or for interaction.
- For all chart types and across both static and interactive mediums.

## Exceptions

None known. Adhering to contrast guidelines is a baseline requirement for accessibility, and it is rarely advisable to deviate.

## Trade-offs

Strict adherence to contrast ratios may limit aesthetic color choices, especially when trying to use a broad range of categorical colors or subtle gradients. The current WCAG 2.x contrast model has known perceptual limitations, and a more advanced model (like APCA/SPCA for WCAG 3.0) is under development, which may shift acceptable ratios in the future.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Fuzzy Text:** Text is difficult to read or appears to blend into its background.
- **Indistinguishable Elements:** Chart elements like bars, lines, or shapes are hard to tell apart from each other or the chart's background.
- **Tool Failure:** Contrast checker tools (e.g., WebAIM Contrast Checker) report ratios below the required 4.5:1 for regular text or 3:1 for large text/graphical elements.
- **Common Failure:** Low contrast is a very common accessibility failure, suggesting it's an easy trap to fall into during design.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:**
    - Use a contrast checker tool (e.g., WebAIM Contrast Checker) to identify problematic color combinations.
    - Adjust foreground or background colors incrementally until the required contrast ratios are met. Prioritize text contrast (4.5:1).
    - If a color is essential, consider adding a thin, contrasting stroke or outline around it to improve its visibility against the background.

- **Moderate approach:**
    - Develop a predefined set of WCAG-compliant color palettes for your visualizations. This ensures that all design choices start from an accessible foundation.
    - Implement a design system that automatically checks contrast ratios for all UI and data visualization elements during development, preventing low-contrast issues before they reach production.
    - For graphical elements that fail 3:1 contrast, consider supplementing with textures or patterns for categorical distinctions, or bold outlines.

- **Comprehensive approach:**
    - Integrate accessibility into your visualization design process from the earliest stages, making contrast a core consideration in color scheme selection and visual encoding.
    - Educate designers and developers on the importance of contrast and how to effectively apply WCAG guidelines.
    - Research and implement more advanced contrast evaluation methods, such as those being developed for WCAG 3.0 (e.g., APCA), to ensure future-proof and perceptually accurate accessibility.
