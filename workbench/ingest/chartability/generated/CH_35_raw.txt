## Guidance

Ensure that graphical objects, user interface components, and large text (18pt/24px or 14pt/18.66px bold and larger) have a contrast ratio of at least 3:1 against their background. Regular text (smaller than 18pt/24px, or 14pt/18.66px bold) must meet a minimum contrast ratio of 4.5:1 against its background.

## Why

Sufficient contrast is fundamental for readability and perceivability across diverse user abilities. Low contrast elements are difficult to distinguish from their background or neighboring elements, severely hindering comprehension and making it impossible for many users, particularly those with low vision or certain color vision deficiencies, to engage with the content. High contrast significantly reduces cognitive load by making information instantly recognizable.

### Core Principle

Ensure visual elements are adequately distinguishable from their surroundings to enable perception by all users, especially those with visual impairments.

## When it applies

- When any text (labels, titles, annotations) or graphical elements (bars, lines, points, UI controls, icons) are displayed.
- For all visualizations and interactive components where visual distinction is necessary for understanding.
- When designing color palettes, especially for categorical data or for differentiating states (e.g., hover, selection).

## Exceptions

- **Purely decorative elements:** Elements that are purely decorative and convey no information are exempt.
- **Logotypes:** Text that is part of a logo or brand name has no minimum contrast requirement.
- **Incidental text/graphics:** Text or graphics that are part of a picture containing significant other visual content, or those not visible to any user, are exempt.

## Trade-offs

Achieving high contrast often requires careful color palette selection, which might sometimes limit aesthetic choices or conflict with specific branding guidelines. However, these design constraints are generally outweighed by the fundamental need for accessibility and clear communication. Compromising on contrast risks excluding a significant portion of the audience.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Squint Test:** If you squint at the visualization, do text or important graphical elements disappear or become indistinct?
- **Color Overlap:** Are adjacent graphical elements (e.g., stacked bars, pie slices) or text close in luminosity to their background or neighboring elements?
- **Automated Checker Warnings:** Do tools like WebAIM Contrast Checker report failures for text (4.5:1) or non-text contrast (3:1) against WCAG AA standards?
- **User Feedback:** Do users report difficulty reading labels, distinguishing chart elements, or identifying interactive components?

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** Use a contrast checker (e.g., WebAIM Contrast Checker) to identify failing elements. Adjust the luminosity (lightness/darkness) of foreground or background colors to meet the minimum WCAG 2.1 AA requirements (4.5:1 for regular text, 3:1 for large text and graphical objects). Prioritize fixing critical text and interactive elements first.

- **Moderate approach:** Integrate contrast checking into the design and development workflow, perhaps as an automated step in your CI/CD pipeline or design system. Select color palettes specifically designed for high contrast and accessibility from the outset, considering both text and graphical elements. Design a primary color palette and a secondary palette for interactive states, ensuring all combinations meet WCAG criteria.

- **Comprehensive approach:** Implement a dynamic contrast adjustment feature that allows users to increase contrast themselves (e.g., a high-contrast mode or switchable theme). Beyond WCAG 2.1, monitor and consider newer contrast algorithms (like APCA/sRGB) as they become standardized, potentially offering more perceptually accurate contrast evaluations for future designs.
