## Guidance

Consciously consider how your design and framing choices may influence interpretation, including the risks of being overly neutral, detached, or misleading.

## Why

No visualization is truly neutral. Design choices—what data to show, how to order it, what colors to use, what to emphasize—all act as rhetorical frames that guide the viewer's interpretation. Appearing overly detached may make the data seem irrelevant, while ignoring the influence of your design decisions can unintentionally mislead or obscure the real-world impact of the data. Acknowledging your role as a framer is an ethical responsibility.

### Core Principle

Every visualization has a point of view. A responsible designer makes that point of view intentional and transparent, rather than accidental and hidden.

## When it applies

- In all visualizations, but especially those on sensitive or politicized topics like climate change, public health, or social justice.
- When you are tempted to think of your work as a "neutral" or "objective" presentation of data.
- During design reviews, as a point of reflection for the team.

## Exceptions

- There are no exceptions to being aware of framing effects. However, the *degree* of rhetorical framing may vary. A scientific chart for peer review might aim for minimal framing, while a journalistic graphic for the public may use stronger framing to tell a clear story.

## Trade-offs

- **Perceived Objectivity vs. Engagement:** A more emotionally resonant or strongly framed design may be more engaging but could be perceived as less objective by some audiences.
- **Clarity vs. Nuance:** A strong frame simplifies the narrative, which can improve clarity but might obscure some of the data's underlying complexity or alternative interpretations.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "data speaks for itself" fallacy:** A belief that the visualization is an unvarnished window into the data, free of any authorial perspective.
- **Unintended consequences:** The audience takes away a message that is alarming, fatalistic, or misleading, even if the data is technically accurate.
- **Detached presentation:** A visualization of a human crisis is presented so abstractly that it fails to connect with the viewer or convey the gravity of the situation.
- **Inconsistent messaging:** The visual framing (e.g., using alarming colors) conflicts with a neutral or understated title.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** During a design review, ask: "What is the story this chart is telling? What is it leaving out? How might someone else interpret this differently?"

- **Moderate approach:** Create two different versions of the visualization with different framing (e.g., one emphasizing the negative trend, one emphasizing a positive aspect or solution). Compare them to understand the impact of your choices.

- **Comprehensive approach:** Write a "designer's statement" or positionality memo that explicitly documents your key framing decisions and their intended effects. For sensitive topics, test the visualization with a diverse audience to check for unintended interpretations before publishing.
