## Guidance

Do not select a chart type simply because it is conventional for a topic or visually appealing. Prioritize clarity and ease of understanding for the intended audience.

## Why

Just because a chart type is widely used in a particular domain (e.g., Sankey diagrams for flow) or looks aesthetically pleasing (e.g., circular layouts) does not mean it is effective for communication. Studies show that viewers' preferences for a chart do not always align with their ability to interpret it correctly. Convention and aesthetics are poor substitutes for perceptual effectiveness. Always question if a simpler, more "boring" chart might actually communicate the message more clearly.

### Core Principle

Effectiveness trumps aesthetics and convention. The best chart is the one that the audience can understand most accurately and efficiently.

## When it applies

- When you are tempted to use a complex or novel chart type you've seen in other publications.
- When your initial design elicits feedback like "that looks so cool," but you haven't tested if it's understandable.
- During early-stage prototyping, when choosing between a standard chart and a more novel, aesthetically driven one.

## Exceptions

- If a specific, complex chart type is a deeply ingrained convention within a narrow expert community, using it may be the most efficient way to communicate with that specific group.
- When the primary goal is artistic expression or to create a "data sculpture," aesthetic considerations may legitimately take precedence over clarity.

## Trade-offs

- **Engagement vs. Performance:** A novel, beautiful chart might draw more initial attention, but a simple bar chart will likely lead to better comprehension. You may trade initial "wow factor" for long-term understanding.
- **Client/Stakeholder Expectations:** A stakeholder might have a specific, "fancy" chart type in mind. Pushing for a simpler, more effective option may require persuasion and evidence.

## Signs of Trouble

- **"This is how we've always done it":** A chart type is chosen based on tradition rather than its fitness for the task.
- **Preference-Performance Gap:** In testing, users say they "like" the look of a chart but perform poorly on tasks using it.
- **Aesthetics over accuracy:** The design prioritizes visual novelty to the point where it distorts data or makes comparison difficult (e.g., 3D pie charts, irregular shapes).

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** For any chart you design, ask: "What is the primary comparison or task this chart needs to support?" Then, check if your chosen chart type is perceptually optimal for that task (e.g., use bar charts for comparing magnitude, not bubble charts).

- **Moderate approach:** Create two versions of your visualization: one using the fancy or conventional chart type, and one using a simple bar, line, or scatter plot. Test both with a few users and see which one they understand better and faster.

- **Comprehensive approach:** In a design study or A/B test, formally measure both subjective preference and objective performance (e.g., task completion time, error rate) for different chart types. Use this data to make an evidence-based decision and to convince stakeholders.
