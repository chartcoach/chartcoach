## Guidance

Use color with care, avoiding overly emotional tones that might seem manipulative or palettes that could mislead viewers.

## Why

Color is one of the first things a viewer notices, and it plays a powerful role in shaping their initial interpretation and emotional response. Overly dramatic colors (e.g., bright red for negative outcomes) can be perceived as biased or emotionally manipulative, potentially harming credibility. A too-neutral palette can feel disengaging. Furthermore, the meaning of color is highly contextual and can be easily misread. A balanced, intentional approach is necessary to guide interpretation without distracting or misleading.

### Core Principle

Color should clarify, not sensationalize. Its purpose is to encode information or create a deliberate, appropriate tone—not to provoke a purely emotional reaction at the expense of reason.

## When it applies

- When choosing a color palette for any chart, especially on emotive topics.
- When encoding quantitative data (e.g., using a sequential or diverging palette).
- When using color to represent categorical data (e.g., different political parties, product lines).

## Exceptions

- In advocacy or persuasive contexts, a more emotionally charged color palette may be an intentional rhetorical choice to convey urgency or alarm. This is a valid strategy, but it should be a conscious decision, not an accident.

## Trade-offs

- **Engagement vs. Neutrality:** An emotionally resonant palette might be more engaging but could be seen as less objective. A neutral palette is safer but may fail to attract attention or convey the importance of the topic.
- **Convention vs. Clarity:** A conventional color scheme (e.g., red for bad, green for good) is easy to understand but might be an oversimplification of the data's nuance.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Manipulative Tone:** The color choices feel overly dramatic, like a horror movie poster (e.g., stark red on a black background for a chart about disease).
- **Misinterpretation:** Viewers misread the meaning of a color based on their own assumptions (e.g., "I thought red meant 'hot'").
- **Vibrating Colors:** Two saturated, complementary colors are placed next to each other, creating a distracting visual effect.
- **Poor Discriminability:** Colors in the palette are too similar to each other, making it hard to tell categories apart, especially for small marks like dots or thin lines.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** Test your palette with a colorblindness simulator (like Coblis or Viz Palette) to ensure it's accessible. Desaturate overly bright or dramatic colors to tone them down.

- **Moderate approach:** Use established, perceptually uniform color palettes from tools like ColorBrewer or d3-scale-chromatic. For quantitative data, use a single-hue sequential palette. For categorical data, choose a palette designed for that purpose.

- **Comprehensive approach:** Conduct user testing to check for unintended color associations with your target audience. Create a palette that is not only perceptually sound and accessible but also aligned with the desired tone and brand identity, while being sensitive to cultural color connotations.
