## Guidance

Provide clear, simple explanations for concepts that may be unfamiliar to your audience, such as uncertainty ranges, statistical baselines, or future scenarios.

## Why

Concepts that are standard in scientific or statistical fields are often opaque to the general public. Displaying an uncertainty range (the "cone of uncertainty") without explanation can be misinterpreted by lay viewers as unreliability or sloppy work, undermining the visualization's credibility. Similarly, technical baselines or scenarios can cause confusion. Explaining these concepts in plain-language text maintains transparency and trust without cluttering the visual itself.

### Core Principle

Define your terms. If your chart's interpretation depends on understanding a specific concept, you are responsible for explaining that concept.

## When it applies

- When visualizing predictive models, forecasts, or any data with a margin of error.
- When a chart uses a specific baseline for comparison (e.g., "compared to pre-industrial levels").
- When presenting different potential future scenarios (e.g., climate change models).
- When using any statistical representation that is not common knowledge.

## Exceptions

- For an expert audience that shares a common understanding of these concepts, a detailed explanation may be unnecessary.

## Trade-offs

- **Clutter vs. Clarity:** Adding explanations directly on the chart can create clutter. Placing them in surrounding text risks them being overlooked.
- **Oversimplification:** A simple explanation of a complex statistical concept might lose some of its technical precision.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Misinterpretation of uncertainty:** Viewers see an uncertainty range and conclude, "So the scientists don't really know what they're doing."
- **Ignoring the cone:** Viewers focus only on the central line of a forecast and ignore the surrounding range of possibility.
- **Confusion over baselines:** Viewers don't understand what the data is being compared to, leading them to misjudge the magnitude of a change.
- **The "Glossary Needed" problem:** The chart is only understandable after reading a separate document defining its terms.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** Add a concise sentence to the caption defining the concept. For example: "The shaded area shows the range of scientifically plausible future scenarios."

- **Moderate approach:** Use an annotation on the chart itself to label and explain the element. For example, an arrow pointing to an uncertainty band with the text, "This shaded area represents the 95% confidence interval."

- **Comprehensive approach:** For interactive visualizations, provide the explanation in a tooltip that appears when a user hovers over the element. For static visuals, consider a small, secondary "explainer" diagram that breaks down the concept visually.
