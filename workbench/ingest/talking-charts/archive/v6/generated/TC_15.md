## Guidance

Do not assume even simple charts are universally understood; be prepared to add clarifying elements to prevent misinterpretation.

## Why

While familiar chart types like bar and line charts are generally more accessible, a significant portion of the population may still struggle to read them correctly or feel unfamiliar with them. High error rates in reading simple charts show that designers should not take comprehension for granted. Adding clarifying elements, such as direct labels, clear titles, and annotated units, can bridge gaps in visual literacy and prevent common misinterpretations.

### Core Principle

Assume nothing. The designer's familiarity with charts is not the audience's. Always design with the least experienced viewer in mind.

## When it applies

- When creating any visualization for a broad, general audience.
- Even when using "simple" chart types like bars, lines, and pies.
- When data literacy levels in your target audience are unknown or expected to be varied.

## Exceptions

- For a specialized, highly data-literate audience (e.g., a team of data analysts), extensive clarification of a simple bar chart is likely unnecessary and could feel patronizing.

## Trade-offs

- **Clutter vs. Clarity:** Adding more labels and annotations can increase visual clutter. The challenge is to add clarification where it's most needed without overwhelming the chart.
- **Pace:** Adding explanations may slow down interpretation for expert users, but it makes the chart accessible to a wider audience.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Assumed Knowledge:** The chart design assumes viewers know how to read axes, understand what a legend is, or interpret a slope on a line chart.
- **Ambiguous Axes:** An axis is labeled with a number but no unit (e.g., is "20" twenty dollars, twenty percent, or twenty thousand people?).
- **Feedback shows basic errors:** User testing reveals people are making simple mistakes, like misreading the value of a bar or confusing two categories.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** Directly label important bars or points with their exact value. This provides an "escape hatch" for those who struggle to use the axis.

- **Moderate approach:** Add a descriptive title that explains how to read the chart. For example, "A Rising Line Shows Increasing Temperatures Over Time." Explicitly label units on axes (e.g., "in millions of USD").

- **Comprehensive approach:** Conduct a "think-aloud" usability test with a few people from your target audience. Ask them to interpret the chart and note any point where they hesitate or express confusion. Use this feedback to add targeted annotations and clarifications precisely where they are needed.
