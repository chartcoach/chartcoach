## Guidance

Adapt the visualization's complexity, framing, and format to the specific platform or publication outlet where it will appear.

## Why

Audiences have different expectations and consumption habits on different platforms. A complex, detailed chart that works well in a print report will likely fail on a mobile social media feed, where attention is scarce. Tailoring the design—such as creating a bite-sized, single-message graphic for Instagram or a detailed, analytical chart for a scientific paper—enhances its resonance and effectiveness with the intended audience.

### Core Principle

Meet your audience where they are. The medium is part of the message, so the design must be appropriate for the context in which it will be viewed.

## When it applies

- When publishing a visualization across multiple channels (e.g., a print article, a website, and social media).
- When designing for platforms with specific constraints or conventions (e.g., mobile screens, Twitter's image preview).
- When targeting different audience segments that have distinct platform preferences.

## Exceptions

- If a visualization is only ever intended for a single, specific context (e.g., an internal dashboard on a desktop monitor), cross-platform adaptation is not necessary.

## Trade-offs

- **Time and Effort:** Creating multiple, platform-tailored versions of a visualization requires significantly more resources than a one-size-fits-all approach.
- **Message Consistency:** While adapting format, it can be challenging to maintain the core message's integrity and nuance across highly simplified and highly detailed versions.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Shrink-and-Pink" approach:** A complex desktop visualization is simply scaled down for mobile, rendering it unreadable.
- **Low engagement:** A visualization shared on social media gets very little interaction because it is too dense or slow to understand.
- **Wrong tone:** An analytical, dry visualization is posted to an emotionally driven platform like Instagram or TikTok and feels out of place.
- **One file to rule them all:** The exact same image file is used across print, web, and social media.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** When sharing a complex chart on social media, don't just post the chart. Post a screenshot of the most important part, circled or highlighted, with the key message typed out in the post's text.

- **Moderate approach:** Create a simplified "social media card" version of your main visualization. This version should have one clear message, a large, readable title, and minimal visual elements.

- **Comprehensive approach:** Develop a multi-platform content strategy. For each data story, design a "pyramid" of assets: a detailed interactive or print version at the top, a summary article for the web, and a series of simple, bold, message-driven graphics or short videos specifically for different social media platforms.
