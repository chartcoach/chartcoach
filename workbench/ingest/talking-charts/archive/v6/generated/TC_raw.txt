## Guidance

Disclose the origin of your data, including its source, collection methods, and any significant processing or filtering steps.

## Why

Missing context about a dataset's origin can lead viewers to question a visualization's credibility and trustworthiness. To build trust, be transparent about where the data comes from and how it has been handled, including any omissions or transformations.

### Core Principle

Transparency builds trust. Providing clear provenance for your data demonstrates accountability and allows viewers to assess the information's reliability for themselves.

## When it applies

- When publishing visualizations for any audience, especially in journalism, science communication, or public policy.
- When creating maps or time-series charts where data gaps might be visible.
- Whenever the credibility of the data is central to the message.

## Exceptions

- For purely artistic or speculative data art where the intent is not to present factual claims, detailed sourcing may be less critical.

## Trade-offs

- Adding detailed methodology can increase visual clutter or text density. It may require careful design to integrate this information without overwhelming the main visual.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Where'd you get this?" question:** Viewers immediately ask where the data came from.
- **Unexplained gaps:** A map has blank regions, or a line chart has breaks, with no explanation.
- **Vague sourcing:** The source is listed as "Internal Data" or "Our Research" without further details.
- **Viewer skepticism:** Feedback indicates that users don't trust the chart's claims.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** Add a "Source: [Data Source Name]" credit at the bottom of the visualization. For data gaps, add a simple annotation like "Data not available."

- **Moderate approach:** Use a caption or footnote to briefly describe the data source, collection period, and any major transformations. For example, "Source: U.S. Census Bureau (2020), adjusted for inflation to 2023 dollars."

- **Comprehensive approach:** Provide layered context. Show the primary source directly on the chart, and use an interactive element or link to a detailed methodology page that explains data collection techniques, processing, transformations, and the rationale for any data exclusion.

## Guidance

Use plain, simple language for all textual elements like titles, captions, labels, and annotations.

## Why

Complex, technical, or abstract language can be a significant barrier to understanding, especially for lay audiences. To prevent confusion and ensure your message is clear, avoid jargon and ambiguous phrasing in all contextual text.

### Core Principle

Clarity trumps jargon. The goal of communication is to be understood, and using accessible language respects the audience's time and cognitive resources.

## When it applies

- When creating visualizations for the general public, news media, or any non-expert audience.
- When explaining complex topics like climate change scenarios, scientific data processing, or statistical concepts.
- In any text that is essential for correctly interpreting the chart.

## Exceptions

- When creating visualizations for a highly specialized, expert audience where technical terms are a precise and efficient shorthand (e.g., an internal report for a team of scientists). Even then, clarity should be prioritized.

## Trade-offs

- Simplifying technical terms may sometimes lead to a minor loss of precision. The goal is to strike a balance where the core meaning is preserved without alienating the audience.
- It can take more time and effort to rephrase complex concepts in simple terms.

## Signs of Trouble

- **"What does that mean?":** Viewers are confused by a specific word or phrase in your caption.
- **Misinterpretation:** People understand the chart's mechanics but draw the wrong conclusion because of a misleading title or label.
- **Audience disengagement:** Viewers skip reading the text because it looks dense or intimidating.
- **Reliance on prior knowledge:** The text assumes the reader already understands a technical concept.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** Run your text through a readability tool (like the Hemingway App) and simplify sentences flagged as complex. Replace jargon with more common words.

- **Moderate approach:** Ask someone from your target audience (or a colleague outside your immediate team) to read the text and explain it back to you. Note any points of confusion and revise.

- **Comprehensive approach:** Develop a content style guide that defines key terms and provides plain-language alternatives. Test all textual elements with a representative sample of your audience before publishing.

## Guidance

Embed essential context directly within the visualization, using concise captions or meaningful illustrations.

## Why

When essential context is missing, viewers are forced to guess a chart's meaning, leading to confusion, irritation, and potential misinterpretation. Placing context directly in the visual field—through text or semantic cues like icons—provides an anchor that supports immediate comprehension.

### Core Principle

Bring the explanation to the data. Don't make viewers hunt for the information they need to understand what they are looking at.

## When it applies

- In static visualizations that will be shared without a surrounding article (e.g., on social media).
- When a chart's topic is not immediately obvious from the title or data labels alone.
- When aiming to make a chart more approachable and less abstract for a general audience.

## Exceptions

- In a series of charts within a report where the context is established once at the beginning, repetition may be unnecessary.
- If the visualization is extremely dense, adding more elements might create clutter. In such cases, context should be placed in the immediate vicinity (e.g., a title or lead-in sentence).

## Trade-offs

- Adding elements can increase visual clutter if not done carefully.
- The meaning of icons or illustrations can be culturally specific and may not be universally understood.

## Signs of Trouble

- **"What am I looking at?":** Viewers are unsure about the fundamental topic of the chart.
- **Over-reliance on title:** The chart is incomprehensible without its title.
- **Guesswork:** Viewers start making assumptions about what the data represents.
- **Icon as decoration:** An icon is present but doesn't add any meaningful information or context related to the data.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** Add a short, descriptive subtitle that explains the chart's main point or topic.

- **Moderate approach:** Use annotations to label key data points or trends directly on the chart. Incorporate a simple, universally understood icon that reinforces the topic (e.g., a water drop icon on a chart about rainfall).

- **Comprehensive approach:** Design a "Semantic Context Chart" where the visual elements themselves are illustrative. For example, instead of plain bars, use shapes or illustrations that represent the data's subject matter (e.g., outlines of countries for geopolitical data), ensuring these elements don't distort the data's scale.

## Guidance

Reduce visual noise to help viewers focus, but do not simplify at the expense of essential information and context.

## Why

Simplifying a visualization helps viewers focus on the core message. However, oversimplification—such as removing information about uncertainty or data limitations—can damage credibility and be perceived as misleading. The key is to strike a balance between clarity and transparency.

### Core Principle

Simplify the presentation, not the truth. A clean design should make the data's complexity more accessible, not hide it.

## When it applies

- When visualizing data that includes uncertainty, error margins, or future projections.
- When working with complex datasets where you must select which variables to show.
- In any situation where there's a trade-off between showing everything and showing the most important thing.

## Exceptions

- For high-level executive summaries or infographics where the goal is to convey a single, simple message quickly, some simplification is expected. However, a link to a more detailed view is still best practice.

- **Clarity vs. Completeness:** The primary trade-off. A simpler chart is easier to read, but a more complete chart is more honest and defensible.
- **Audience Perception:** What one viewer sees as "clean and simple," another may see as "hiding something."

## Signs of Trouble

- **False Precision:** A chart showing a single-line projection for the future without any indication of uncertainty.
- **Critical Omissions:** The chart omits data points or categories that would change the main conclusion.
- **Divided Feedback:** Some users praise the chart's clarity while others question its trustworthiness.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** Add a sentence in the caption acknowledging the simplification. For example, "Projections show the central estimate; see source for the full range of uncertainty."

- **Moderate approach:** Use visual techniques to de-emphasize secondary information. For instance, show uncertainty bands in a lighter, less saturated color behind the main data line.

- **Comprehensive approach:** Create a layered or interactive visualization. Present a simple, clean view by default, but allow users to click or hover to reveal more detailed information, such as uncertainty ranges, alternative scenarios, or methodological notes.

## Guidance

Allow viewers to access more detailed information on demand if they become curious.

## Why

Viewers have different needs: some want a quick overview, while others seek deep, detailed evidence. When context is missing, viewers can become confused or distrustful. Providing optional context—through captions, annotations, or interactive layers—satisfies those who seek more detail without overwhelming everyone else.

Design for variable depth. Present a clear, simple surface but provide pathways to deeper information for those who want to explore.

- In digital and interactive media where hover effects, clicks, or tooltips are possible.
- When a visualization must serve a mixed audience of experts and laypeople.
- When presenting complex data where a single static view cannot capture all the nuance.

- In static, space-constrained formats like print or a presentation slide, providing layers of information is difficult. In these cases, a URL or reference to a more detailed source is a good substitute.
- As noted in one study, lay users may not always use interactive features. They should not be the only means of conveying essential information.

- **Implementation Cost:** Building interactive layers requires more development time and technical skill than creating a static chart.
- **Discoverability:** Users might not discover or use the interactive features, potentially missing the deeper context.

- **Comprehensive approach:** Design a multi-layered interactive narrative (a "scrollytelling" piece) that starts with a high-level overview and progressively reveals more detail and complexity as the user scrolls or clicks. This guides the user from a simple message to the underlying data.

## Guidance

Prominently state the visualization's main message in a title, subtitle, or large caption.

## Why

A chart's title is often the first, and sometimes only, text a viewer reads. It sets the frame for interpretation. A generic title (e.g., "Population by Country") forces the viewer to do the work of finding the insight. A descriptive title (e.g., "China and India account for over a third of the world's population") delivers the message directly, reducing cognitive load and preventing misinterpretation.

### Core Principle

Don't make the reader work for the main insight. A good visualization doesn't just show data; it communicates a finding.

## When it applies

- In almost all communicative visualizations, especially those for general audiences.
- When a chart is complex or information-dense, a clear title can prevent viewers from feeling overwhelmed.
- In news graphics, reports, and presentations where clarity and speed of understanding are paramount.

## Exceptions

- In purely exploratory tools where the user is meant to find their own insights, a descriptive title might be premature or biasing. A neutral, descriptive title (e.g., "Filterable Sales Data") would be more appropriate.

## Trade-offs

- A strong, message-driven title can be seen as biasing the viewer's interpretation, though this is often the goal in rhetorical visualization.
- It requires the designer to have a clear, single message to convey, which may not always be the case during the design process.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Generic Title:** The title only describes the axes (e.g., "Revenue vs. Time").
- **Audience Confusion:** Viewers look at the chart and ask, "So what? What am I supposed to see here?"
- **Varied Interpretations:** Different people look at the same chart and come away with completely different, or even incorrect, main takeaways.
- **The "Empty Chart" Test:** If you were to remove all the data marks (the bars, lines, dots) and only leave the title and axes, would the viewer still know the key finding? If not, the title isn't doing its job.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** Add a subtitle below your generic title that asks and answers a question. Title: "Annual Revenue". Subtitle: "When did revenue first cross the $1B mark?"

- **Moderate approach:** Rewrite the title to be a full, declarative sentence that states the main finding. Instead of "Global Temperature Anomaly (1880-2023)," use "Global Temperatures Have Risen Sharply Over the Past 40 Years."

- **Comprehensive approach:** Design the entire visualization around the message in the title. Use annotations, color, and emphasis to visually guide the viewer's eye to the data that supports the title's claim.

## Guidance

Make the intended message a guiding principle behind your design decisions, rather than an afterthought.

## Why

Designing around a core message from the start leads to a more coherent and effective visualization. When the message is clear, it informs every choice—from the chart type and color palette to annotations and title. This helps audiences grasp the key insight more easily because all elements of the design are working together to support it.

### Core Principle

Form follows function, and in rhetorical visualization, the primary function is to convey a message. The design should serve that message.

## When it applies

- In communicative or narrative visualization, where there is a specific story or finding to convey.
- When working in a team, as a clear message provides a shared goal and decision-making framework.
- At the beginning of the design process, before committing to a specific chart type.

## Exceptions

- In exploratory data analysis, the goal is to *find* the message, not illustrate a pre-determined one. In this context, the design process is one of discovery, and the message may only emerge at the end.

## Trade-offs

- A strong message-first approach can introduce confirmation bias, where the designer might subconsciously emphasize data that supports the message and downplay data that contradicts it.
- It requires upfront clarity about the story you want to tell, which may not be possible if the data is still being analyzed.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Design by default:** The chart type was chosen because it's the default in a software tool, not because it best suits the message.
- **Conflicting elements:** The title says one thing, but the most visually prominent part of the chart shows something else.
- **Endless tweaks:** The design process involves endless, aimless changes because there is no clear communicative goal to anchor decisions.
- **The "Franken-chart":** A visualization tries to tell too many stories at once, resulting in a cluttered and confusing design.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** Before you start designing, write down the single most important message you want the viewer to take away in one sentence. Keep this sentence visible as you work.

- **Moderate approach:** For any design choice (e.g., color, chart type), ask yourself: "Does this choice help clarify the main message, or does it distract from it?" If it distracts, change it.

- **Comprehensive approach:** Structure your workflow to be message-first. Start with data analysis to find potential stories. Select the most important one. Then, storyboard how to tell that story visually. Only then should you move to a design tool to build the visualization, using the storyboard and message as your blueprint.

## Guidance

Consciously consider how your design and framing choices may influence interpretation, including the risks of being overly neutral, detached, or misleading.

## Why

No visualization is truly neutral. Design choices—what data to show, how to order it, what colors to use, what to emphasize—all act as rhetorical frames that guide the viewer's interpretation. Appearing overly detached may make the data seem irrelevant, while ignoring the influence of your design decisions can unintentionally mislead or obscure the real-world impact of the data. Acknowledging your role as a framer is an ethical responsibility.

### Core Principle

Every visualization has a point of view. A responsible designer makes that point of view intentional and transparent, rather than accidental and hidden.

## When it applies

- In all visualizations, but especially those on sensitive or politicized topics like climate change, public health, or social justice.
- When you are tempted to think of your work as a "neutral" or "objective" presentation of data.
- During design reviews, as a point of reflection for the team.

## Exceptions

- There are no exceptions to being aware of framing effects. However, the *degree* of rhetorical framing may vary. A scientific chart for peer review might aim for minimal framing, while a journalistic graphic for the public may use stronger framing to tell a clear story.

## Trade-offs

- **Perceived Objectivity vs. Engagement:** A more emotionally resonant or strongly framed design may be more engaging but could be perceived as less objective by some audiences.
- **Clarity vs. Nuance:** A strong frame simplifies the narrative, which can improve clarity but might obscure some of the data's underlying complexity or alternative interpretations.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "data speaks for itself" fallacy:** A belief that the visualization is an unvarnished window into the data, free of any authorial perspective.
- **Unintended consequences:** The audience takes away a message that is alarming, fatalistic, or misleading, even if the data is technically accurate.
- **Detached presentation:** A visualization of a human crisis is presented so abstractly that it fails to connect with the viewer or convey the gravity of the situation.
- **Inconsistent messaging:** The visual framing (e.g., using alarming colors) conflicts with a neutral or understated title.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** During a design review, ask: "What is the story this chart is telling? What is it leaving out? How might someone else interpret this differently?"

- **Moderate approach:** Create two different versions of the visualization with different framing (e.g., one emphasizing the negative trend, one emphasizing a positive aspect or solution). Compare them to understand the impact of your choices.

- **Comprehensive approach:** Write a "designer's statement" or positionality memo that explicitly documents your key framing decisions and their intended effects. For sensitive topics, test the visualization with a diverse audience to check for unintended interpretations before publishing.

## Guidance

When testing your visualizations, aim to include a diverse group of viewers to capture different perspectives and uncover a wider range of potential interpretation issues.

## Why

A homogeneous group of testers—for example, only young, tech-savvy university students—will give you a narrow and potentially biased view of your visualization's effectiveness. Different life experiences, educational backgrounds, ages, and levels of expertise all shape how a person interprets a chart. Involving a diverse group in your testing process helps you uncover a broader range of blind spots, identify accessibility issues, and ensure your design is understandable and inclusive for different audience segments.

### Core Principle

A diverse audience reveals diverse problems. The more varied your testers, the more robust your final design will be.

## When it applies

- When designing a visualization for a broad and diverse public audience.
- When you want to ensure your design is inclusive and accessible.
- During any form of user testing, from informal feedback to formal usability studies.

## Exceptions

- When designing for a very specific, homogeneous expert group, it is more important that your testers are representative of that narrow group than that they are broadly diverse.

## Trade-offs

- **Recruitment Effort:** Recruiting a diverse panel of testers can be more time-consuming and costly than relying on a convenience sample (like students or colleagues).

## Signs of Trouble

- **Homogeneous Testers:** All of your user feedback comes from people who are just like you (e.g., same age, profession, and education level).
- **The "Student Sample" problem:** Your testing process exclusively relies on university students, who are not representative of the general population.
- **Ignoring Edge Cases:** You dismiss feedback from one person as an "outlier," without considering that they might represent an important but underserved segment of your audience.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** Make a conscious effort to get feedback from at least one person who is significantly different from you in terms of age, technical background, or profession.

- **Moderate approach:** When recruiting for testing, intentionally seek out participants from different demographic groups. For example, include a mix of ages (younger and older), educational levels (academic and non-academic), and levels of familiarity with your topic.

- **Comprehensive approach:** Use a recruiting service or panel that allows you to specify demographic criteria for your testers. Conduct a large-scale survey or use an unmoderated testing tool to gather feedback from a representative sample of your target population, and then segment the analysis to look for differences in interpretation between groups.
