## Guidance

Consider the varying levels of visual and data literacy in your target audience, especially when designing for the public.

## Why

Not everyone has the same level of experience or confidence in reading data visualizations. Concepts that are second nature to a designer or analyst—like reading an axis, interpreting a slope, or understanding an error bar—can be significant hurdles for a layperson. Assuming a high level of literacy can lead to designs that are confusing or intimidating for a large portion of your audience, undermining your communication goals.

### Core Principle

You are not your user. Your expertise in creating charts makes you a poor judge of how a novice will interpret them.

## When it applies

- When designing for a broad, general, or public audience.
- When using any chart element that requires statistical understanding (e.g., uncertainty, regression lines, log scales).
- When deciding between a simple, familiar chart type and a more complex, novel one.

## Exceptions

- When designing for a highly specialized, expert audience (e.g., other data scientists), you can assume a high level of visual and statistical literacy and use more complex, information-dense chart types.

## Trade-offs

- **Simplicity vs. Precision:** Designing for lower literacy levels often means simplifying concepts (like uncertainty), which can sometimes come at the cost of statistical precision.
- **Pacing:** A chart designed for a low-literacy audience may feel "slow" or overly simplistic to an expert.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Jargon in the chart:** The chart or its labels use statistical terms like "standard deviation" or "p-value" without explanation.
- **Complex encodings:** The chart relies on less common visual encodings (e.g., area, angle, saturation) to convey primary information.
- **User feedback:** Viewers say "I'm not a math person" or "This looks too scientific for me."
- **Misinterpretation of core concepts:** Users misunderstand fundamental elements like what the axes represent or the meaning of a legend.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** Avoid statistical jargon. Replace it with plain-language explanations. For example, instead of "uncertainty range," try "range of possible outcomes."

- **Moderate approach:** Use multiple, simpler charts instead of one complex one. Add extensive annotations that explain how to read the chart and what the main takeaway is.

- **Comprehensive approach:** Conduct user testing with people who represent the lower end of the expected literacy spectrum for your audience. Use a "think-aloud" protocol to identify specific points of confusion and design interventions (like better labels or companion text) to address them.
