## Guidance

When location is relevant to the data, use maps or other geo-referencing techniques to help viewers grasp spatial relationships.

## Why

Maps are a universally familiar format that leverages people's existing mental models of the world. They make abstract data tangible by connecting it to known places, allowing viewers to understand the geographical scope of a topic and see how it relates to them personally. This spatial context is highly effective for engagement and comprehension, especially when the story is about regional differences, distribution, or movement.

### Core Principle

Leverage existing mental models. People already have a map of the world in their heads; use it to your advantage to make data more intuitive.

## When it applies

- When the data has a geographic component (e.g., countries, states, cities, latitude/longitude).
- When the story is about regional comparisons, spatial patterns, or proximity.
- When you want to help the audience understand the "where" of a story (e.g., showing the impact of a climate event on their region).

## Exceptions

- When the geographic area of a shape on a map does not correlate with the data's importance, a choropleth map can be misleading (e.g., a large, sparsely populated state appears more significant than a small, dense one). In such cases, a cartogram or a simple bar chart of the locations might be better.
- If the precise geographic location is not important to the story, a map can be a distraction.

## Trade-offs

- **Geographic distortion:** Standard map projections distort area and shape, which can in turn distort the perception of data.
- **Complexity:** Creating accurate and effective maps can be more technically complex than creating simple charts.
- **Over-familiarity:** Sometimes a map is used by default for geographic data, when a simple sorted bar chart would make the comparison of values much easier.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Big Empty State" Problem:** In a choropleth map of the US, large western states dominate visually, even if their data values (e.g., population) are small.
- **Comparison is difficult:** It's hard for viewers to accurately compare the colors of two non-adjacent regions on a map.
- **The "Where is that?" problem:** The map lacks sufficient labels or context, leaving viewers unsure of what regions they are looking at.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** If using a choropleth map, also provide a simple bar chart of the same data, sorted by value. This allows for both geographic context and accurate comparison.

- **Moderate approach:** Use a tile grid map or a cartogram, which equalizes the area of each region, to avoid distortions where geographic size doesn't match data importance.

- **Comprehensive approach:** Create a well-designed interactive map. Use tooltips to provide exact values on hover. Offer different baselayers and allow users to zoom and pan. If using a choropleth, ensure the color scale is perceptually uniform and has a clear, easy-to-read legend.
