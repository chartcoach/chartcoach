## Guidance

Ensure visual elements appear simple and straightforward, without overwhelming the viewer with excessive density or technicality.

## Why

Lay audiences can feel intimidated or overwhelmed by visualizations that look overly technical or are packed with information. When a chart is too complex, viewers may disengage entirely or become confused and draw incorrect conclusions. Simplifying the visual density and ensuring each element is easy to interpret is crucial for maintaining viewer engagement and ensuring accurate comprehension.

### Core Principle

Clarity invites engagement; complexity creates barriers. A viewer who feels intimidated by a chart is a viewer you have already lost.

## When it applies

- When designing for a general audience, public communication, or news media.
- When dealing with multi-dimensional or large datasets.
- When choosing between showing all the data in one complex chart versus breaking it into several simpler charts.

## Exceptions

- For expert audiences who require high data density to perform specific analytical tasks (e.g., in scientific research or financial analysis), a complex, information-rich display may be appropriate and even necessary.

## Trade-offs

- **Oversimplification:** In reducing complexity, you risk removing important nuance or context (see "Balance simplification with sufficient context").
- **Space:** Breaking one complex chart into multiple simpler charts requires more screen or page real estate.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Wall of Data":** The chart looks like a dense, impenetrable thicket of lines, dots, or bars.
- **Viewer disengagement:** Users say things like "This is too complicated for me" or "I don't know where to start looking."
- **Long interpretation time:** It takes viewers a very long time to figure out what the chart is showing.
- **Incorrect takeaways:** Viewers consistently misinterpret what they are seeing because they are overwhelmed.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** Remove non-essential visual elements. Can you eliminate gridlines, borders, or background shading? Can you reduce the number of colors?

- **Moderate approach:** Instead of showing all data categories, use color to highlight one or two key categories and de-emphasize the rest by showing them in a muted gray.

- **Comprehensive approach:** Reconsider the entire structure. Instead of a single, complex chart, tell the story through a series of small, simple charts (small multiples). Each chart can focus on one dimension or category, making the information easier to digest while still allowing for comparison.
