## Guidance

Strategically emphasize key elements in your visualization to guide the viewer's attention to the most important insight.

## Why

When all visual elements carry the same weight, viewers don't know where to look first, and the main message can be lost in the noise. By using strategic emphasis—such as making a key line bold, highlighting a specific bar with a bright color while showing others in gray, or using annotations—you create a clear visual hierarchy. This acts as a guide, directing the viewer's focus to the most important part of the story.

### Core Principle

Direct the spotlight. Don't make your audience guess what's important; show them.

## When it applies

- When a visualization contains a lot of data, but only one or two series are part of the main story.
- In line charts with many overlapping lines (a "spaghetti plot").
- In any static visualization where you want to tell a specific story without relying on animation or interaction.
- When you have a clear, message-driven title and want the visual to directly support it.

## Exceptions

- In purely exploratory tools, where the user is meant to find their own insights, pre-applying emphasis would be biasing.
- In reference charts (like a table or index) where all data points are considered equally important.

## Trade-offs

- **Discovery vs. Direction:** Emphasizing one part of the data inherently de-emphasizes others. This makes the primary story clearer but may discourage viewers from discovering other, secondary patterns on their own.
- This technique is inherently rhetorical and moves the visualization away from a purely "objective" presentation of data.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Spaghetti Plot":** A line chart with many lines of equal color and weight, making it impossible to trace any single one.
- **Visual Democracy:** All bars, dots, or lines in the chart have the same visual prominence, leaving the viewer to figure out which are important.
- **Conflicting Signals:** The title talks about one category, but the color scheme gives equal weight to all categories.
- **"Where do I look?":** Viewers express confusion about where to focus their attention.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** Pick the single most important category or data point. Make it a bold, saturated color and change all other data elements to a light gray.

- **Moderate approach:** Combine color with annotations. Highlight the key data series in color and add a text label directly next to it that explains its significance. This removes the need for the viewer to consult a separate legend.

- **Comprehensive approach:** Design the entire visualization as a narrative that guides the user. Use a combination of layout, color, size, and annotations to create a clear path for the eye to follow, leading from the main message (often in the title) to the visual evidence in the chart.
