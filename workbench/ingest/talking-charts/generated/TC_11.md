## Guidance

Avoid purely decorative embellishments that may distract or confuse rather than clarify the data.

## Why

Every element in a visualization should serve a purpose. While aesthetic choices and meaningful visual cues (like icons tied to the data's topic) can support engagement and understanding, purely decorative elements—often called "chartjunk"—can distract viewers from the data, add cognitive load, and potentially be misinterpreted as representing data themselves.

### Core Principle

Form should enhance function, not compete with it. A beautiful chart is one that is, first and foremost, clear.

## When it applies

- When choosing icons, illustrations, or background images to accompany a chart.
- When considering using 3D effects, gradients, or heavy textures on chart elements like bars or areas.
- When deciding on a visual style for a project.

## Exceptions

- In infographics or marketing materials, some level of illustration or stylistic embellishment is often expected and can be effective for attracting attention, as long as it doesn't distort the underlying data.
- As noted by Scientific American designers, aesthetics can be intentionally used to create emotional resonance and make data more relatable, which is a valid communicative goal beyond pure clarity.

## Trade-offs

- **Engagement vs. Clarity:** A visually spartan chart may be very clear but could be perceived as dry or unengaging. A more illustrative chart might attract more attention but risks being less clear.
- **Brand vs. Best Practice:** An organization's branding guidelines might call for visual elements (like heavy gradients or specific icons) that conflict with data visualization best practices.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Duck":** The visualization is designed in a decorative way that is unrelated to the data itself (a concept from Edward Tufte).
- **Ambiguous elements:** A viewer asks, "What does this little picture mean? Is it data?"
- **Distraction:** The background image or decorative icons are more eye-catching than the data itself.
- **Distortion:** 3D effects on bars or pie slices make it impossible to accurately compare their sizes.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** Remove any visual element that doesn't represent data or directly aid in its interpretation (e.g., background images, unnecessary icons). Opt for a clean, minimalist style.

- **Moderate approach:** If using icons or illustrations, ensure they have a clear semantic purpose. For example, use country flags to label countries in a bar chart, or use a small, universally understood icon in the title to establish the topic.

- **Comprehensive approach:** Develop a design system where aesthetic choices are purposefully tied to communicative goals. Use aesthetics to create mood, foster emotional connection, or make abstract data feel more tangible, but test these designs to ensure they don't undermine the chart's readability or perceived credibility.
