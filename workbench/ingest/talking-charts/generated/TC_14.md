## Guidance

When designing for a general audience, use familiar and simple chart types like bar charts, line charts, or maps.

## Why

The choice of chart type fundamentally shapes how an audience interprets data. For lay viewers, familiarity is a key factor in comprehension and trust. Simple, conventional formats like bar and line charts require less cognitive effort to decode, allowing the audience to focus on the data's message rather than on deciphering the chart's mechanics. Novel or complex chart types can be intimidating and lead to misinterpretation.

### Core Principle

Don't make your audience learn a new visual language to understand your message. Use the conventions they already know.

## When it applies

- When communicating to a broad, general, or non-expert audience.
- When the primary goal is clear and rapid communication of a key finding.
- When you have a choice between one complex, multi-dimensional chart and several simpler ones.

## Exceptions

- For expert audiences, more complex chart types (e.g., parallel coordinates, complex network diagrams) may be appropriate and efficient for their specific analytical tasks.
- When a novel chart type is exceptionally good at revealing a pattern that would be invisible in a standard chart, it may be worth the cost of explaining how to read it.

## Trade-offs

- **Novelty vs. Clarity:** Sticking to simple charts may feel less creative or visually innovative, but it prioritizes clarity and broad accessibility.
- **Information Density:** A single complex chart can sometimes show relationships between multiple dimensions more efficiently than several simple charts, but at the cost of being harder to read.

## Signs of Trouble

- **"How do I read this?":** The viewer's first question is about the chart's mechanics, not its message.
- **The "Cool" Factor:** The chart type was chosen because it looks interesting or is trendy, not because it's the best fit for the data and task.
- **High Error Rate:** When you test the chart, users frequently make errors when trying to read values or compare data points.
- **Request for Simplicity:** Your audience explicitly says, "Can't you just show this in a bar chart?"

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** If you're using a complex chart, ask yourself: "Can I communicate the main message just as well with a bar, line, or scatter plot?" If yes, switch to the simpler form.

- **Moderate approach:** If a complex chart is necessary, accompany it with a simple "companion" chart. For example, show the main finding in a simple bar chart, and then provide the more complex multi-dimensional view for further exploration.

- **Comprehensive approach:** If you must use a novel chart type, explicitly teach the audience how to read it. Use extensive annotations, a step-by-step guide, or an animated introduction to explain the visual encoding before they engage with the full dataset.
