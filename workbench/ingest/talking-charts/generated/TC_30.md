## Guidance

Whenever possible, test your visualization with members of your target audience to check if it is clear, understandable, and conveys the intended message.

## Why

As the creator of a visualization, you are cursed with knowledge. You know what the data means, what the message is supposed to be, and how the chart is meant to be read. Your audience has none of this context. This gap often leads designers to overestimate the clarity of their work. Testing with real users is the only reliable way to know if your design is actually effective. It helps you identify points of confusion, check if your intended message is being received, and ensure your color and layout choices are not causing problems.

### Core Principle

You can't grade your own homework. The only people who can tell you if your visualization is clear are the people you designed it for.

## When it applies

- Before publishing any high-stakes visualization.
- When you are unsure if a design is too complex.
- When you want to confirm that your main message is being understood.
- When choosing between two alternative designs.

## Exceptions

- **Time and Resources:** The most common reason for skipping testing. While formal testing is ideal, even informal feedback is valuable.
- **Low-stakes visuals:** For quick, internal charts with a short lifespan, formal testing is likely not necessary.

## Trade-offs

- **Time and Cost:** User testing requires time to recruit participants, run the sessions, and analyze the results.
- **"Design by committee":** A risk of misinterpreting user feedback is to simply implement every suggestion, which can lead to a weaker design. The goal is to diagnose problems, not to crowdsource solutions.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "echo chamber":** The only people who have seen the visualization are the people who made it.
- **"It's obvious":** The design team believes the chart is perfectly clear and doesn't need testing.
- **Post-publication surprises:** After publishing, you receive a flood of confused questions or see your chart being widely misinterpreted.
- **Reliance on metrics alone:** Judging a chart's success solely by clicks or likes, which don't measure comprehension.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach (Informal):** The "coffee shop test." Show your chart to a friend, family member, or colleague from another department. Ask them one question: "What is the main thing this chart is telling you?" If their answer is wildly different from your intended message, you have a problem.

- **Moderate approach (Unmoderated):** Use an online testing service (like UserTesting.com or Maze) to get feedback from a small number of users from your target demographic. Give them a few simple tasks to complete with the chart and ask for their interpretation.

- **Comprehensive approach (Moderated):** Conduct formal, one-on-one usability sessions. Use a "think-aloud" protocol where you ask users to narrate their thought process as they interpret the visualization. This provides deep insights into specific points of confusion and misunderstanding.
