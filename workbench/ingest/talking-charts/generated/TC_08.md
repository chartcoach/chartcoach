## Guidance

To reduce misinterpretation when visualizations are shared outside their original context, build key explanations directly into the chart image.

## Why

Visualizations are often screenshotted and shared on social media or in presentations, stripped of their original article and surrounding text. If a chart relies on that external context for correct interpretation, it can be easily misunderstood or misused. By embedding essential elements like a descriptive title, annotations, and source information into the visual itself, you create a self-contained unit of information that remains clear wherever it travels.

### Core Principle

A good visualization should be a self-sufficient artifact. It should carry its own context with it.

## When it applies

- When creating static visualizations (e.g., PNG, JPG) for the web, especially for news or social media.
- When dealing with complex or easily misinterpreted data.
- When breaking down a complex topic across multiple visuals; each visual should still make sense on its own.

## Exceptions

- In a closed, controlled environment like a PDF report or a book, where the surrounding text is guaranteed to be present, this is less critical.
- For purely exploratory, interactive tools where the context is generated by the user's interaction.

## Trade-offs

- Embedding text can make the visualization more cluttered and requires careful layout to maintain readability.
- It may require more design effort than simply writing a caption in a CMS.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Naked Chart":** The image file contains only the chart's graphical elements, with no title, source, or annotations.
- **Misuse in the wild:** You see your chart shared online with an interpretation that is completely wrong.
- **Reliance on captions:** The chart is incomprehensible without reading the paragraph of text below it on the original webpage.
- **Essential info in HTML:** The title, source, or creator's credit is in the webpage's HTML, not burned into the image itself.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** Ensure that your final exported image includes the chart's title, subtitle, and source credit.

- **Moderate approach:** Add direct annotations to the chart to label key events, highlight important data points, or explain what the axes represent in plain language.

- **Comprehensive approach:** Create a template for all your shareable graphics that includes dedicated space for a descriptive title, a succinct explanation of the main finding, data source, and your organization's logo or name. Follow an internal style guide for consistent placement of these elements.
