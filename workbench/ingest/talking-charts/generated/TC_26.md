## Guidance

While inviting a personal connection can boost engagement, this should be balanced against the need for overall clarity and accessibility for all viewers.

## Why

Personalization—such as highlighting a viewer's local area on a map or allowing them to input their own data—is a powerful tool for boosting engagement. It answers the viewer's implicit question: "How does this affect me?" However, a hyper-focus on personal relevance can sometimes cause viewers to ignore the broader context or message. A well-balanced design uses personalization as a hook to draw the viewer in, but then guides them to understand the larger story.

### Core Principle

Use personalization as a doorway, not a destination. It should be an entry point to the data, not a distraction from it.

## When it applies

- When designing interactive visualizations that allow for personalization (e.g., "Enter your zip code to see local data").
- When using geographic data where viewers are likely to look for their own location first.
- In data-driven calculators or tools that provide a personalized result.

## Exceptions

- For tools that are purely personal utilities (e.g., a personal finance dashboard), the focus is entirely on the user's own data, and broader context is less relevant.
- In static graphics intended for a very broad audience, deep personalization is often not technically feasible or desirable.

## Trade-offs

- **Engagement vs. Big Picture:** A highly personalized view is engaging for the individual but may make it harder for them to see national or global trends.
- **Relevance vs. Alienation:** Content that is highly relevant to one user (e.g., their local area) can make other users (from different areas) feel that the visualization is not for them.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Navel-Gazing" Effect:** In an interactive map, users find their own location, get the information they want, and then leave without ever exploring other areas or understanding the overall pattern.
- **Loss of context:** The personalized view is presented without any comparison or benchmark, making it hard for the user to know if their result is high, low, or average.
- **Frustration:** Users who cannot find their specific situation or location in a personalized tool feel excluded and disengage.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** In a static graphic, highlight a few diverse and representative examples rather than just one. This shows the data is relevant to multiple groups.

- **Moderate approach:** In an interactive visualization, when a user selects a personalized view (e.g., their city), always present it alongside a relevant benchmark (e.g., the national average). This provides immediate context.

- **Comprehensive approach:** Design a guided interactive experience. Start with the big picture (e.g., a national map). Then, invite the user to find their location. After showing the local data, zoom back out to show how their location fits into the larger pattern. This creates a "zoom in, zoom out" narrative structure.
