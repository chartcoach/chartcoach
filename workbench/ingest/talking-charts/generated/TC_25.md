## Guidance

Consider using familiar visuals, such as icons, simple illustrations, or meaningful shapes, to make abstract data feel more tangible and relatable.

## Why

Standard charts like bar charts are efficient but abstract. For lay audiences, connecting data to familiar concepts through visual metaphors can significantly improve comprehension, recall, and engagement. Visual anchors like icons or object shapes act as intuitive shortcuts, helping viewers quickly grasp the topic and ground the data in a real-world context. They can function as implicit narratives, making the chart feel less like a dry analysis and more like a story.

### Core Principle

Connect the abstract to the concrete. Use what people already know and recognize to help them understand what they don't.

## When it applies

- When designing for a general audience or those with lower data literacy.
- When the topic of the data is a physical object or a universally understood concept (e.g., people, houses, trees).
- In infographics or explanatory journalism where engagement and memorability are key goals.

## Exceptions

- The chosen visual metaphor must be universally understood by the target audience; otherwise, it can create more confusion.
- The visuals must not distort the data. For example, in an isotype chart (pictogram), all icons must be the same size. Scaling the icon size to represent value (a common mistake) distorts perception.

## Trade-offs

- **Clarity vs. Clutter:** Poorly chosen or overly complex illustrations can become "chartjunk" that clutters the visualization.
- **Precision vs. Appeal:** While appealing, an illustrative style might not be perceived as sufficiently serious or precise for some scientific or financial contexts.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Stretched Icon" problem:** An icon's height or width is distorted to match a value, making it a "decorated bar chart" and leading to perceptual distortion. The icon's area does not scale linearly with the value.
- **Ambiguous Metaphor:** The icon or illustration is unclear, and viewers don't know what it's supposed to represent.
- **Decoration, not data:** The illustration is purely decorative and has no connection to the data's meaning or scale.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** Add a simple, relevant icon to the title or legend of your chart to immediately establish the topic.

- **Moderate approach:** Create an isotype chart (pictogram), where each icon represents a certain quantity (e.g., one figure icon = 1 million people). This makes large numbers more tangible.

- **Comprehensive approach:** Design a "semantic context chart" where the data is displayed within a larger, meaningful illustration. For example, a chart about sea-level rise could be integrated into a cross-section of a coastline. This requires careful design to ensure the illustration enhances, rather than distorts, the data.
