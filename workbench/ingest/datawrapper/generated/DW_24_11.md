## Guidance

Use color in tables to serve a specific purpose, such as highlighting key information, showing categories, or guiding the eye. Adjust the color's intensity based on the area it fills.

## Why

Color is a powerful pre-attentive attribute that instantly draws the eye. When used without purpose, it creates distracting visual noise. When used strategically, it can significantly improve a table's readability by making important information stand out or by helping users navigate different data categories. The perceived intensity of a color changes with its size, requiring different approaches for text versus backgrounds.

### Core Principle

Every use of color should have a job. Use bright colors for small areas (like text) and muted, pastel colors for large areas (like cell backgrounds) to ensure emphasis without compromising legibility.

## When it applies

- When you want to draw attention to a specific row, column, or cell.
- When you want to group rows or columns into categories (e.g., by political party, region).
- When you want to highlight the highest or lowest values in a column.

## Exceptions

- In a purely exploratory, expert-facing table, extensive color-coding might be used for complex status indicators, but this is a specialized use case. For general audiences, less is more.

## Trade-offs

- Overusing color can make a table look chaotic and diminish the impact of any single highlight.
- Poor color choices can create accessibility issues for users with color vision deficiencies or low vision.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Rainbow Effect:** The table uses many different bright colors, and it's not clear what they signify. It looks like a bag of Skittles.
- **Vibrating Colors:** A bright, saturated background color makes the black text on top appear to vibrate and is difficult to read.
- **Invisible Highlights:** A colored piece of text is too dark or has too little contrast with the background to be readable.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Highlight with Purpose.** Pick one or two key data points you want the reader to see and use a single, simple highlight color (e.g., a yellow background) for those cells only.
- **Moderate Approach: Categorize with Color.** If you have categorical data (e.g., political parties), assign a distinct, accessible color to each category. Use this color consistently, for instance, as a colored dot next to the category name or as a subtle background.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Modulate Color by Area.**
    - **For backgrounds (cells, rows):** Use light, unsaturated pastel colors. These provide a tint without overwhelming the text.
    - **For text:** Use brighter, more saturated colors. Since text characters are small, they need a stronger color to be noticeable. Always ensure the colored text has at least a 4.5:1 contrast ratio against its background to be accessible.
