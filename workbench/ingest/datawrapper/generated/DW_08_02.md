## Guidance

When visualizing concepts that have strong, pre-existing color associations (like political parties, brands, or abstract concepts like "good" vs. "bad"), use the conventional colors.

## Why

Your audience does not come to your chart as a blank slate. They bring a lifetime of cultural and learned associations with them. Leveraging these conventions acts as a cognitive shortcut, making your chart instantly more intuitive. For example, in the US, coloring Democrats blue and Republicans red is an immediate, universally understood code. Using these colors means your audience can understand the chart without even looking at the legend. Conversely, violating a strong convention (e.g., coloring Democrats red) creates cognitive dissonance, forcing the viewer to unlearn their expectation and actively work to decode your chart, which can lead to misinterpretation or distrust.

### Core Principle

Don't make your audience think more than they have to. Meet them where they are by using the visual language they already understand.

## When it applies

- Visualizing political data (e.g., election maps, polling results).
- Charting data related to specific brands (e.g., comparing market share of Coca-Cola, Pepsi, and Sprite).
- Representing concepts with strong symbolic color meanings (e.g., green for positive/growth, red for negative/loss; gold/silver/bronze for 1st/2nd/3rd place).
- Visualizing data for countries or teams with well-known flag or jersey colors.

## Exceptions

- When the conventional colors have poor perceptual properties (e.g., they are not colorblind-safe or have poor contrast). In this case, your primary responsibility is to make the chart readable, but you should acknowledge that you are deviating from the norm.
- When you are intentionally subverting the convention to make a specific rhetorical point. This is an advanced technique and should be used with extreme care, as it can easily backfire.
- When visualizing for an international audience where conventions may differ or not exist. For example, the red/blue political convention is specific to the US; in other countries, party colors are different (e.g., red for Labour in the UK, orange for the NDP in Canada).

## Trade-offs

- **Brand Guidelines vs. Convention:** A company's brand palette may conflict with a common convention. For example, if your brand color is red and you are charting financial growth, using red for positive numbers would be confusing. In these cases, the semantic convention should usually take precedence over brand consistency within the chart itself.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Cognitive Dissonance:** A viewer says, "Wait, I thought [X] was supposed to be the blue one?"
- **Slower Interpretation:** Viewers pause for a long time before they can answer a simple question about the chart, as they have to consciously override their initial assumption.
- **Accusations of Bias:** If you swap political colors, viewers may assume you are being intentionally misleading or are ignorant of the topic.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Research First.** Before choosing colors, do a quick search for "[Category] color convention" (e.g., "US political party colors"). Follow the established standard.
- **Moderate Approach: Adapt Conventions for Accessibility.** If a conventional color is not accessible (e.g., a yellow with poor contrast), find the closest accessible alternative. For example, use a darker, more accessible gold instead of a light yellow.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Add Redundant Cues.** If you must deviate from a convention, or if no convention exists, don't rely on color alone. Add direct labels, icons, or other markers so that the chart is understandable even if the color mapping isn't intuitive.
