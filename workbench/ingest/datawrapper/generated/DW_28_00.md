## Guidance

When visualizing categorical data where the categories have no natural order (e.g., countries, products, types of fruit), assign a unique and perceptually distinct hue (e.g., blue, green, orange) to each category. This is known as a **qualitative** color scale.

## Why

Different hues are pre-attentively processed by our brains as indicating separate, distinct items. Using shades of a single color (a sequential scale) would incorrectly imply a quantitative or ordinal relationship (e.g., that 'dark blue' is somehow "more" than 'light blue'), which does not exist in nominal data. This avoids misleading the viewer into seeing a pattern or order that isn't there.

## When it applies

- When your data consists of discrete, non-ordered categories (nominal data).
- When the primary task is to distinguish between different groups and look up their values.

## Exceptions

- **Highlighting**: If you want to draw attention to one specific category, it's effective to use a single distinct hue for that category and a muted gray for all others.
- **Grouping**: If your categories have a hierarchical structure, you can use distinct hues for the main parent categories and then use shades of each hue for their respective subcategories.

## Trade-offs

- Using many distinct hues (more than 7-10) can become visually overwhelming and chaotic. The colors can also become difficult to tell apart, a problem known as high cardinality.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Implied Ranking:** A bar chart of different companies or products uses light blue, medium blue, and dark blue, making viewers wonder if there's a ranking or order they are supposed to see.
- **Legend Overload:** Viewers have to constantly refer back to the legend to remember which color corresponds to which category. This is a sign the colors are not distinct enough or there are too many.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Switch to a Qualitative Palette.** Replace the sequential or diverging palette with a qualitative one where each color is a distinct hue.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Use a Tested Palette.** Instead of picking colors by hand, use a tool like ColorBrewer or Datawrapper's palette generator to find a pre-made, perceptually distinct qualitative palette. These are designed to maximize the difference between colors.
