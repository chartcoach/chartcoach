## Guidance

Reserve area charts for visualizing how values develop over a continuous period, such as time. For comparing values across discrete, non-temporal categories, use a bar or column chart.

## Why

Area charts connect data points, creating a visual flow that implies continuity and progression. This metaphor is appropriate for time-series data but is misleading when applied to distinct categories like countries, products, or departments. Bar charts are designed specifically to facilitate comparisons between discrete items.

### Core Principle

The visual grammar of a chart should match the structure of the data. Use visual elements that suggest continuity (like lines and areas) for continuous data, and use elements that suggest discrete items (like bars) for categorical data.

## When it applies

-   You are choosing a chart type to compare quantities across different categories.
-   Your independent variable is categorical (e.g., products, regions, poll responses) and not temporal.

## Exceptions

-   If your categories have a natural, inherent order (ordinal data) and you want to show a cumulative build-up across them, a stepped area chart could be considered, but a stacked bar chart is often still clearer.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Categorical X-Axis:** The labels on your area chart's x-axis are names (e.g., "North America," "Europe," "Asia") instead of dates or times.
-   **Awkward Connections:** The line connecting your data points feels meaningless. For example, what does the slope of the line between "Apples" and "Oranges" represent?

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix:** Change the chart type from an area chart to a column or bar chart. Most charting software allows this with a single click. This immediately corrects the misleading visual metaphor.
-   **Comprehensive Approach:** Re-evaluate the primary goal. If it's to compare categories, a bar chart is best. If there's a hidden time element, restructure the data to create a proper time-series visualization.

## Guidance

If your primary goal is to compare the trends of individual series over time and their cumulative total is not meaningful, use a line chart instead of a stacked area chart.

## Why

Stacked area charts are designed to show how a total is composed of different parts and how that composition changes over time. The "stacking" visually sums the component values, which draws the reader's attention to the total. This adds unnecessary cognitive load if the total is irrelevant to your message. A line chart focuses purely on the trend of each individual series, making it a cleaner and more direct way to show and compare trends.

## When it applies

-   You are visualizing multiple time-series data sets together.
-   The values of the different series are independent and should not be added together (e.g., stock prices, temperatures in different cities, unemployment rates for different countries).
-   Your primary task for the reader is to compare the trajectory of one series to another.

## Exceptions

-   If the values represent parts of a whole (e.g., population share by continent, market share by company) and both the individual shares and the total are important, a stacked area chart is appropriate.
-   If the series add up to 100%, a 100% stacked area chart is a very effective choice.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Meaningless Total:** The total height of all your stacked areas represents a number that has no real-world meaning (e.g., "total temperature," "total price").
-   **Comparison is the Goal:** Your chart's title or key message is about comparing series (e.g., "X overtakes Y"), but you are using a stacked format that obscures this comparison.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix:** In your charting tool, switch the chart type from "Stacked Area" to "Line." All the series will now share a common baseline, making them instantly comparable.
-   **Moderate Approach:** After converting to a line chart, review your color choices and line weights to ensure each series is distinct and the most important ones stand out.

## Guidance

When visualizing time-series data where the variations between values are small relative to their total magnitude, choose a line chart over an area chart.

## Why

Area charts represent quantity through shaded area, which means they must have a y-axis that starts at zero to avoid misrepresentation. This requirement can severely compress the visual representation of your data, making small but important fluctuations nearly invisible. Line charts, which encode values purely by position, are not bound by the zero-baseline rule for trend analysis. This allows you to adjust the y-axis to "zoom in" on the relevant data range, magnifying subtle variations and making them easy to perceive.

## When it applies

-   You are visualizing a time series where all values are far from zero (e.g., a human body temperature chart).
-   The absolute magnitude is less important than the change, volatility, or pattern of change over time.
-   Your data range is small compared to its distance from zero (e.g., values range from 1,050 to 1,060).

## Exceptions

-   If the absolute magnitude and its relation to zero is the key message (e.g., "profits remained high"), an area chart (or a line chart that starts at zero) might be acceptable. However, you risk the audience missing the smaller trends.

## Trade-offs

-   By using a line chart with a non-zero baseline, you gain clarity on variation but sacrifice the at-a-glance perception of absolute magnitude. This can be misleading if not done carefully. Always clearly label your axes to make the range apparent.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Flat-Liner:** Your area chart looks almost flat, even though you know there are important fluctuations in the data.
-   **High Baseline:** All the action in your area chart is happening in a narrow band at the very top of the chart.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix:** Change the chart type to a line chart.
-   **Moderate Approach:** After changing to a line chart, adjust the minimum and maximum of your y-axis to tightly frame the data's range. For example, if your data ranges from 850 to 875, set your axis to run from 840 to 880 instead of 0 to 900.

## Guidance

If you are showing how the composition of a total changes over a small number of discrete time points (e.g., fewer than ten), use a stacked column chart instead of a stacked area chart.

## Why

Stacked columns create distinct, stable blocks for each time point, which are easier for the eye to process and compare than the angular points of an area chart with sparse data. This format also provides more defined spaces for placing data labels, improving readability. An area chart's continuous nature is best suited for showing trends over many data points; with few points, it can look awkward and imply a misleadingly simplistic trend between points.

-   You have a small number of dates or time periods (e.g., quarterly data for two years, or annual data for five years).

-   **Moderate Approach:** After switching to a stacked column chart, ensure the gaps between columns are appropriately sized (usually smaller than the column width) to reinforce the connection over time. Add direct labels to the most important segments.

## Guidance

When the key insight is to compare the trends of different series—especially to show a "crossover" where one overtakes another in rank—use a line chart instead of a stacked area chart.

## Why

In a stacked area chart, every series except the one at the very bottom is plotted on a fluctuating baseline created by the series below it. This distorts the perceived shape of its trend, making it extremely difficult to judge its true trajectory or compare it accurately to other non-baselined series. A line chart, by contrast, places all series on the same common y-axis, enabling direct, accurate comparisons and making intersections (crossovers) unambiguous.

### Core Principle

Humans make the most accurate visual comparisons when objects are positioned along a common scale. Line charts excel at this, while stacked area charts (for all but the bottom series) inherently prevent it.

## When it applies

-   Your primary message involves comparing two or more individual series within a whole.
-   A key event in your data is a change in the rank ordering of the series (e.g., "Product B sales surpass Product A").
-   You want to draw attention to the relationship between specific series, not all of them.

## Trade-offs

-   Switching to a line chart de-emphasizes the part-to-whole relationship. The viewer will no longer see how the series sum to a total. If showing the total is also critical, consider showing both charts or using annotations.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Wobbly Trends:** A series in the middle of your stack appears to go down, but its actual value may have been increasing (just slower than the series below it grew).
-   **Hidden Crossover:** You have to verbally explain that one series overtook another because it's not visually obvious from the chart.
-   **Narrative Focus on a Slice:** Your story is about the blue slice and the green slice, but they are separated by a red slice in the middle of the stack.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix:** Change the chart type to a line chart.
-   **Moderate Approach:** After changing to a line chart, remove any series that are not part of the core comparison to reduce clutter. Use color and line thickness to highlight the key series you want the audience to compare.
-   **Comprehensive Approach:** Create a line chart focused on the crossover story. Then, if the part-to-whole context is still important, consider adding a simple secondary chart (like a pie chart or stacked bar for a single point in time) to establish the composition.

## Guidance

When creating a stacked area chart, strategically order the layers so the most important category for your message, or the least volatile category, is at the bottom.

## Why

Humans can only accurately judge and compare values that share a common, straight baseline. In a stacked area chart, only the bottom-most series benefits from the stable, flat baseline of the x-axis. All other series are plotted on top of the fluctuating series below them, distorting their shape and making their true values and trends difficult to read. -   There is one particular series whose trend is more important than the others.

-   **Baseline Instability:** The bottom layer of your chart is a very small, volatile series, creating a "noisy" foundation for all the other categories.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix:** In your charting tool, reorder the data series so that the most important one is listed first (as this usually places it at the bottom).
-   **Moderate Approach:** After reordering, use color to further emphasize the key series at the bottom. Make it a strong, saturated color while using more muted or analogous colors for the other series above it.

## Guidance

Whenever possible, place labels directly onto their corresponding areas in an area chart, rather than relying on a separate color legend.

## Why

Requiring a viewer to look back and forth between a legend and the chart itself increases cognitive load. This phenomenon, known as the split-attention effect, forces the brain to work harder to match the key to the data, slowing down comprehension and increasing the chance of error. Placing labels directly on or next to the data they describe eliminates this extra mental step, allowing for faster and more accurate interpretation.

## When it applies

-   You are creating an area chart (or line chart, pie chart, etc.) with multiple series.
-   There is enough space within or near the areas to place readable text labels.

## Exceptions

-   In highly dense or volatile charts, there may not be enough clear space for direct labels.
-   In interactive charts, a legend can be used as a filtering or highlighting mechanism, which provides a functional benefit that may outweigh the split-attention cost. A common solution is to highlight the corresponding area on hover.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Ping-Pong Eyes:** You find yourself (or watch others) repeatedly glancing between the chart's colored areas and a legend to understand what's what.
-   **Ambiguous Colors:** Two colors in your legend are similar enough that it's hard to distinguish them in the chart itself.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix:** Turn off the default legend and manually add text labels in your charting software. Place them in a clear, spacious part of each area, usually towards the right side of the chart where the trend concludes.
-   **Moderate Approach:** Style the direct labels to match the color of their respective areas. This creates an even stronger visual link. Ensure there is sufficient contrast between the text color and the area's background color for readability.
-   **Comprehensive Approach (for interactive charts):** Implement interactive labels that appear on hover, or use the legend to trigger highlighting, which connects the legend item to the data without requiring manual matching.

## Guidance

In a stacked area chart with many categories, combine the smallest and least important series into a single group, typically labeled "Other."

## Why

An excess of categories in a stacked area chart leads to visual clutter. The smallest series often appear as thin, unreadable "slivers" that are difficult to distinguish, color-code, and label. While technically accurate, this level of detail overwhelms the viewer and obscures the main trends. Grouping these minor series simplifies the chart visually, reduces the number of colors and labels needed, and allows the reader to focus their attention on the most significant categories.

## When it applies

-   You have a stacked area chart (or stacked bar, or pie chart) with high cardinality (many categories).
-   A number of your categories contribute very little to the total (e.g., each is less than 1-2% of the total).
-   The individual trends of these small categories are not the main point of the story.

## Trade-offs

-   You lose the ability to see the individual trends of the series that have been grouped into "Other." If the behavior of a specific small series is important, it should be kept separate.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Rainbow Slivers:** The top of your chart is a thin, messy band of many different colors.
-   **Unlabelable Layers:** It is physically impossible to place a readable label on several of the areas.
-   **Cognitive Overload:** The chart looks intimidatingly complex at first glance due to the sheer number of series.

## How to Improve

-   **Data Preparation:** Before creating the chart, identify the categories that consistently fall below a certain threshold (e.g., 2% of the total). Create a new category called "Other" and sum their values into it.
-   **Visual Styling:** After grouping, place the "Other" category at the top of the stack. Assign it a neutral color, like grey, to signal that it's a miscellaneous collection and to keep the visual focus on the more important, individually colored categories below.
