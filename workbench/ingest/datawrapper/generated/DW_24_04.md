## Guidance

Use a table when you want to allow readers to compare values both across rows (e.g., different metrics for the same item) and down columns (e.g., the same metric for different items).

## Why

Most chart types are optimized for a single direction of comparison. For example, a grouped bar chart makes it easy to compare categories within a group, but harder to compare the same category across different groups. A table's grid structure naturally supports both horizontal and vertical comparisons, offering more flexibility to the reader.

## When it applies

- When you have a matrix of data where both the rows and columns represent distinct, comparable categories.
- When you cannot easily decide which comparison direction is more important for your audience.
- When you want to provide a flexible data exploration tool for the reader.

## Exceptions

- If there is one primary comparison that is far more important than any other, a chart designed specifically for that comparison will be more effective.

## Trade-offs

- **Increased Cognitive Load:** While offering flexibility, tables force the reader to do the comparative work (mental math, scanning back and forth) themselves.
- **Missed Insights:** Important trends, outliers, or patterns that would be immediately obvious in a chart are likely to be missed in a table.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Chart indecision:** You are struggling to decide whether to group your bar chart by region or by year, because both comparisons seem equally important.
- **Complex needs:** Your audience wants to compare both a company's performance against its competitors this year, and that same company's performance against its own results from last year.

## How to Improve

- **Action:** Structure your data in a simple table with clear row and column headers. This empowers the reader to make their own comparisons as they see fit. For added clarity, consider using a heatmap within the table to guide the eye to high and low values.
