## Guidance

Limit the primary comparison goal of a stacked column chart to the overall totals and the single segment that rests on the shared baseline (the bottom-most segment).

## Why

Humans are very good at comparing the lengths of objects that share a common starting point (a baseline). In a stacked column chart, only the bottom segment has this property. It is perceptually difficult and inaccurate to compare the sizes of the other, "floating" segments because they all start at different heights. Focusing the chart's message on the totals and the bottom series aligns the visualization with what viewers can accurately perceive.

### Core Principle
Make the most important comparisons the easiest to perform. Aligning data to a common baseline is the most effective way to enable accurate visual comparison of magnitude.

## When it applies
- When creating a stacked column chart.
- When the primary task for the viewer is to compare the size of different components across several parent categories.

## Exceptions
- If the goal is to give a general, qualitative sense of the composition of totals without needing precise comparisons between the inner segments. For example, showing that a certain component is consistently the largest or smallest part of the total.

## Trade-offs
- This limits the utility of a stacked column chart. If you need to enable comparison for more than one or two segments, this chart type is not the right choice and another should be used.

## Signs of Trouble
- **Floating Comparisons:** The chart's title or annotations ask the reader to compare segments that are not on the bottom baseline (e.g., "Notice the growth in segment C").
- **Reader Confusion:** Viewers express difficulty in determining which of the inner segments is larger or how they change across the columns.
- **Over-reliance on Labels:** The chart is unreadable without data labels on every single segment, indicating that the visual encoding itself is failing.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Reorder and Highlight.** If you must use a stacked column chart, move the most important segment for comparison to the bottom and use a highlight color to draw attention to it.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to Grouped Bars.** Change the chart type to a grouped (or "split") bar chart. This gives every segment its own bar and a common baseline, making all comparisons easy and accurate.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Small Multiples or a Dot Plot.** For comparing multiple parts across multiple totals, a series of small multiple bar charts (one for each segment) or a dot plot can be even clearer, especially with many categories or segments.

## Guidance

When visualizing how parts of a whole change over time, prefer a line chart if the primary goal is to show trends in the individual parts. Only use a stacked column chart if the cumulative total at each time point is also a critical part of the story.

## Why

Line charts are specifically designed to show trends and changes over a continuous interval like time. Each line provides a clear, uninterrupted path, making it easy to see patterns of growth, decline, and volatility. A stacked column chart, while showing composition, forces the reader to mentally calculate the trends for "floating" segments and adds visual complexity (the part-to-whole relationship) that may be irrelevant and distracting if the total value isn't important.

### Core Principle
Choose a chart type that directly encodes the primary task. For showing trends, a line chart is more direct and requires less cognitive effort than a stacked column chart.

## When it applies
- When you have time-series data for multiple categories that make up a whole.
- When the main story is about the individual trajectory of each category over time.

## Exceptions
- When the absolute size of the total at each time point is a key message. For example, showing how total revenue (and its composition) has grown each year.
- When there are very few time points (e.g., 2 or 3), making a "trend" less apparent and the part-to-whole comparison at each point more important.

## Trade-offs
- A line chart does not explicitly show the total sum of the parts at each time point. This information is lost in favor of clearer individual trends.
- A line chart no longer communicates that the categories are parts of a whole, unless explicitly stated.

## Signs of Trouble
- **Hidden Trends:** The primary message is about a trend in an inner (non-baseline) segment, but this is difficult to see in the stacked column chart.
- **Irrelevant Totals:** The total height of the columns varies, but this variation has no meaning or importance to the story being told.
- **Cognitive Strain:** Viewers find it hard to describe the shape of the trend for any segment other than the one at the bottom.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Add Trendlines.** Overlay trendlines on your stacked column chart, though this can become very cluttered.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Line Chart.** Change the chart type to a line chart to make all the trends visible and easy to compare.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use a Panel Chart.** Create small-multiple line charts, one for each category. This provides maximum clarity for each trend while still allowing comparison across the panels.

## Guidance

When using a stacked column chart to display data over time, ensure the time points are at regular, consistent intervals (e.g., every day, month, or year). If your time intervals are irregular, use a line or area chart instead.

## Why

Column charts use a categorical axis, where each column occupies a slot of equal width. This visual treatment strongly implies that the "distance" between each category is uniform. When used for time, this means the viewer will assume the time between each column is the same. Plotting irregular time intervals on this type of axis distorts the perception of time and can make changes appear more or less dramatic than they actually are, which is misleading. Line and area charts use a continuous scale axis, which can accurately represent the true spacing of irregular time points.

### Core Principle
The visual representation of data should accurately reflect its underlying properties. Time is continuous and should be scaled accordingly.

## When it applies
- When visualizing time-series data using a chart with a categorical axis, like a column or bar chart.
- When your data points are not evenly spaced in time (e.g., data for 2010, 2011, 2015, 2020).

## Exceptions
- If the time points are being treated as discrete, unrelated events rather than a continuous series. For example, comparing sales during "Holiday Season 2019" vs. "Product Launch 2021". Here, the specific dates are less important than the named events.

## Trade-offs
- You may not be able to use a column chart format if your data has irregular intervals, even if you prefer it for other reasons (e.g., emphasizing discrete totals).

## Signs of Trouble
- **Uneven Gaps:** The labels on the x-axis show time points that are not equally spaced (e.g., 'Jan', 'Feb', 'Jun').
- **Distorted Slopes:** A mental line drawn between the tops of the columns would have a misleading slope because the x-axis is not to scale.
- **Misleading Narrative:** The chart might suggest steady growth, when in fact there was a large time gap where no data was collected or is being shown.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Add Annotations.** Explicitly call out the irregular time gaps with text annotations on the chart to warn the reader. This is a patch, not a fix.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Line/Area Chart.** Change the chart type to a line or area chart and ensure the x-axis is set to a continuous time scale. This will correctly position the data points in time.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Impute or Aggregate Data.** If appropriate for the analysis, process the data to create regular intervals. This could involve aggregating data into larger, regular bins (e.g., annual data from monthly data) or imputing missing values (with clear documentation).

## Guidance

To emphasize when one category's value surpasses another's over time (a change in rank or an "overtaking" event), a line chart is a more effective choice than a stacked column or area chart.

## Why

Line charts represent each data series as a distinct, continuous path. When two paths cross, it creates a highly salient and unambiguous visual event that directly corresponds to one value overtaking another. In a stacked chart, all but the bottom series "float" on top of others. A change in rank is represented only by the changing thickness of the colored bands, an effect that is much less direct and harder to spot than an explicit crossover.

- When a key part of the story is that one category grew faster than another and eventually became larger in value.

## Exceptions
- If showing the part-to-whole relationship and the total value is more important than highlighting the exact point of the crossover. You might then use a stacked chart and add an annotation to point out the rank change.

## Trade-offs
- Using a line chart means you lose the ability to see the total sum of the categories at each time point.
- The chart no longer inherently shows that the categories are parts of a single whole.

## Signs of Trouble
- **A Buried Story:** The main takeaway is a rank change, but you have to explain it in the caption because it's not obvious from the stacked chart itself.
- **Subtle Thickness Change:** The crossover is visible only as a subtle shift in the relative thickness of two adjacent color bands.
- **Ambiguous Ordering:** It is difficult for a viewer to quickly tell the rank order of the inner segments at any given point in time.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Annotate the Stacked Chart.** Add a text label and an arrow pointing to the spot on the stacked chart where the "overtaking" happens.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Animate the Transition.** In an interactive medium, you could start with the stacked chart to show composition, then animate a transition to a line chart to tell the story of the rank change.

If you are visualizing the components of only a single total, use a standard bar or column chart where each component gets its own bar. Avoid using a single stacked column.

## Why

A standard bar chart aligns all components to a common baseline, making comparison between them simple and accurate. It also typically makes better use of space. A single stacked column is essentially a divided bar, but presented vertically it can look awkward and be space-inefficient. - If the single stacked bar is part of a larger dashboard or system where this format is used consistently for a specific purpose (e.g., as a custom KPI indicator).

- **Difficult Comparisons:** To compare two segments, you have to read the data labels; you can't do it easily just by looking.

## Guidance

When your entire message can be summarized by one or two numbers, present them directly as text. Avoid creating a chart to show a single data point.

## Why

Charts are for showing patterns, relationships, and comparisons that are difficult to see in a table of numbers. A single number has no pattern or relationship to show. - When the single number is part of a larger, standardized dashboard format (e.g., a "gauge" chart or a single-bar KPI) where the visual consistency is important for quick scanning across many such indicators.
- When the visual itself is used for branding, decoration, or to create an emotional impact that goes beyond simply conveying the number.

- You lose the potential "graphical appeal" of a chart, but gain clarity and speed of comprehension.

## Guidance

In a stacked column or bar chart, arrange the segments so that the most important category for comparison is placed on the baseline (the bottom of a column or the start of a bar).

## Why

The segment resting on the baseline is the only one whose size can be accurately judged and compared across the different stacks. The other segments are "floating," making it perceptually difficult to compare their lengths. By strategically placing the most important segment on the baseline, you are making the most critical comparison for your story the easiest and most accurate one for your audience to perform. Using a highlight color on this segment further directs viewer attention.

### Core Principle
Design the chart to answer the most important question most easily. Guide the viewer's eye and leverage perceptual strengths.

## When it applies
- When creating any stacked chart (column, bar, area).
- When one specific component's trend or comparison is the primary message of the chart.

## Exceptions
- If there is a natural or conventional ordering of the segments that would be confusing to change (e.g., ordering by age group, or a 'strongly agree' to 'strongly disagree' Likert scale). In this case, the conventional order may take precedence over placing the most important segment at the bottom.

## Trade-offs
- You can only give one segment the "prime real estate" on the baseline. Other segments will remain difficult to compare.

## Signs of Trouble
- **The Floating Story:** The chart's title or main insight is about a segment that isn't on the baseline.
- **Re-ordering Confusion:** A viewer might be confused if the legend order doesn't match the stacking order, so ensure they are consistent.
- **Mismatched Focus:** The colors or annotations highlight a middle segment, while the easiest-to-read segment at the bottom is unrelated to the main point.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Use a Stronger Color.** If you can't change the order, use a bold, saturated highlight color on the most important segment to help it stand out, and de-saturate the other colors.
- **Moderate Redesign: Reorder the Segments.** Change the stacking order of your data series to place the most important one at the bottom (for columns) or beginning (for bars). Update the legend to match the new stacking order.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use a Different Chart.** If you need to compare two or more segments accurately, a stacked chart is the wrong choice. Switch to a grouped bar chart or a line chart.

## Guidance

Use a 100% stacked chart when comparing the relative proportions of components is more important than comparing their absolute values. This technique provides a second baseline at the top of the chart, allowing for accurate comparison of both the top and bottom segments.

## Why

By normalizing the total of each column or bar to 100%, you eliminate variations in the total size, focusing the viewer's attention purely on the changing composition. A key benefit of this approach is that it creates a flat top edge, which acts as a second baseline. This allows you to place your second-most-important category at the top of the stack, enabling viewers to accurately compare its proportion across the chart, in addition to the bottom-most category.

### Core Principle
Transform the data to better suit the specific task. Normalizing to percentages changes the task from comparing absolute values to comparing relative proportions.

## When it applies
- When the absolute totals of each stack are irrelevant or misleading (e.g., comparing survey results from samples of different sizes).
- When the primary goal is to show how the percentage breakdown of a whole changes across categories.
- When you have two key segments you want viewers to be able to compare accurately.

## Exceptions
- When the absolute size of the totals is an important part of the story. A 100% stacked chart completely hides this information. For example, showing revenue composition where total revenue growth is also important.

## Trade-offs
- You lose all information about the absolute size of the totals. A category could have a large percentage share of a very small total, which would be hidden.
- The middle segments remain difficult to compare, just as in a regular stacked chart.

## Signs of Trouble
- **Hidden Totals:** The chart shows stable percentages, but the underlying absolute totals are changing dramatically (or vice-versa), and this is a key part of the story that is being missed.
- **Misleading Interpretation:** A reader concludes that the value of the top segment is growing, when in reality it's just its percentage of a shrinking total that is growing.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Add an Annotation.** In the title or a caption, explicitly state that the chart shows relative proportions and that totals cannot be compared. Mention the absolute totals if they are important.
- **Moderate Redesign: Provide a Companion Chart.** Show two charts: the 100% stacked chart to show composition, and a simple bar or line chart next to it to show how the totals change.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Choose a Different Chart Type.** If both absolute totals and composition are important, a standard stacked chart may be better, or perhaps a different form like a treemap where both area (for total) and sub-area (for parts) can be perceived.

## Guidance

To reduce clutter and improve clarity in charts showing composition, group numerous small or insignificant categories into a single, combined category labeled "Other" or "Miscellaneous."

## Why

Presenting too many categories at once overwhelms the viewer, creating "chart junk" and high cognitive load. Many tiny segments create a visually noisy chart, require an excessive number of colors (making them hard to distinguish), and demand a large, complex legend. By grouping the long tail of small, less important items, you simplify the visualization, declutter the legend, and focus the reader's attention on the data that matters most.

### Core Principle
Maximize the signal-to-noise ratio. Remove or consolidate trivial details to make the main message more prominent.

## When it applies
- In any part-to-whole chart (pie, donut, stacked bar/column, treemap).
- When you have high-cardinality categorical data where many categories represent a very small percentage of the total.

## Exceptions
- If the "long tail" itself is the story. In that case, you might want to specifically show the diversity of the small categories rather than grouping them.
- In an interactive visualization where a user might want to explore every single category, no matter how small. In this case, they could be grouped by default but expandable on click.

## Trade-offs
- You lose the detail of the individual small categories. The composition of the "Other" category is hidden.

## Signs of Trouble
- **Rainbow Chart:** Your chart uses more than 7-10 different colors and looks like a rainbow.
- **Sliver Segments:** Some segments are so small they are barely visible as thin lines.
- **Massive Legend:** The chart's legend is longer than the chart itself.
- **"Where's Waldo?"**: The viewer has to hunt for a specific category in a sea of tiny parts.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Group and Color.** Identify categories below a certain threshold (e.g., <2% of the total), sum them up into an "Other" category, and assign it a neutral color like gray.
- **Moderate Redesign: Order and Position.** Place the "Other" category at the end of the legend and at the top of a stacked column (or the end of a stacked bar). This makes it clear that it's a residual group and keeps it from interfering with the main segments.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Add Interactivity.** In a digital format, allow users to click on the "Other" segment to see a breakdown of its components in a tooltip or a separate view. This provides a clean default view with the option to drill down for more detail.

## Guidance

When constructing a stacked chart, ensure you are only plotting the component parts that make up the whole. Do not include a data series for the total itself in the stack.

## Why

The entire purpose of a stacked chart is that the visual sum of the parts (the total height of the column or length of the bar) represents the total. If you include the total as one of the segments to be stacked, you are double-counting it. This creates a chart where the total height is a meaningless, inflated number (often double the true total) and fundamentally misrepresents the data. It is a common but serious data preparation error.

### Core Principle
A visualization must be logically and mathematically sound. The visual representation of the sum should equal the sum of the parts shown.

## When it applies
- When preparing data for any type of stacked chart (column, bar, or area).
- This is often a pre-processing check in your spreadsheet or data preparation tool.

## Exceptions
- There are no exceptions. Including the total as a segment in a stack is always an error.

## Trade-offs
- There are no trade-offs. This is a rule of correctness.

## Signs of Trouble
- **A Dominant Segment:** One segment of your stack is exactly as large as all the other segments combined.
- **Nonsensical Totals:** The y-axis scale goes up to a number that is far higher than what you know the total should be.
- **Redundant Legend:** The legend contains entries for the parts and for the total (e.g., "Men," "Women," and "All Respondents").

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Remove the Total Series.** Go back to your source data and remove the row or column that contains the pre-calculated total before creating the chart. The charting software will calculate and display the total for you by stacking the parts.
