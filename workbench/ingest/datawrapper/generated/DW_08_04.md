## Guidance

To draw attention to the most important part of your data, use a single, strong, saturated color for that element and display all other contextual data in a muted, neutral color like gray.

## Why

Not all data points are equally important. Often, a chart's purpose is to tell a specific story or highlight a key insight. Using a full, multi-color palette gives equal visual weight to every data point, forcing the viewer to figure out what's important. By strategically using color for your main point and gray for everything else, you are doing that work for them. This use of a "spot color" leverages pre-attentive processing; the unique color "pops out" and directs the eye exactly where you want it to go, making your message land in seconds.

### Core Principle

Guide your audience's attention. Use visual hierarchy to signal what is most important, and de-emphasize what is merely context.

## When it applies

- When your chart is intended to communicate a specific story or finding (e.g., "Our sales have doubled," "This country is an outlier").
- In any chart with many data series (lines, bars, dots) where you want to focus on just one or two.
- In dashboards, to draw attention to a key metric or an alert.

## Exceptions

- **Exploratory analysis:** When the goal is to explore the data without a preconceived story and allow the user to find their own insights, giving every category a distinct color may be more appropriate.
- **When all categories are equally important:** If you are presenting a simple comparison of a few categories (e.g., sales for 3 main competitors) and there is no single "hero," then giving each its own color from a balanced palette is the right choice.

## Trade-offs

- **Loss of detail in context:** The de-emphasized gray elements become harder to distinguish from each other. This is a deliberate choice: you are trading the ability to easily compare the contextual elements for the ability to instantly see the main point.

## Signs of Trouble

- **A Sea of Color:** Your chart uses many bright colors, and your eye doesn't know where to look first. The main insight is lost in the noise.
- **The "So what?" question:** A viewer looks at your colorful chart and asks, "Okay, but what am I supposed to be looking at?"
- **Weak Highlight:** You've tried to highlight something, but the highlight color is not distinct enough from the other colors in the palette to truly stand out.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: "Gray out" the competition.** Take your existing chart. Keep the color for the one or two categories you want to talk about, and change every other category's color to a light or medium gray.
- **Moderate Approach: Add an interactive highlight.** If it's important for users to be able to inspect all categories, start with one highlighted by default but allow the user to click or hover on other categories in the legend to highlight them in turn.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Re-structure with small multiples.** If you need to tell a story about several different categories, it's often better to give each one its own small chart (a "small multiple") where it is highlighted, rather than trying to cram all the highlights into a single, confusing chart.
