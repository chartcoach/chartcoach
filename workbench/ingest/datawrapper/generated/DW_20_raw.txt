## Guidance

Instead of a purely descriptive title that only states what the data is (e.g., "Population of European Countries, 2023"), use the title to pose the central question the chart answers or to state the primary finding.

## Why

A narrative title immediately tells your audience *why* they should care about the chart. It frames the data as a story or the answer to a question, which is more engaging and memorable than a neutral description. This reduces the cognitive load on the reader, as they don't have to study the chart to figure out its main purpose.

### Core Principle
Make the chart's primary message or purpose unmissable by stating it at the top.

## When it applies

-   This is especially effective for explanatory and journalistic visualizations intended for a general audience.
-   Use it when your visualization is designed to communicate a specific finding, story, or answer to a question.

## Exceptions

-   In exploratory data analysis tools or academic contexts where objectivity is paramount, a neutral, descriptive title may be more appropriate.
-   For dashboards with multiple charts, a simple descriptive title for each component might be clearer than multiple narrative titles.

## Trade-offs

-   A narrative title asserts a specific interpretation, which may subtly discourage the audience from exploring the data for other possible insights.
-   It can feel less objective, which might be a drawback depending on the context and audience expectations.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The "What is this?" Title:** The title is a simple list of the variables on the axes (e.g., "Temperature vs. Time").
-   **The Encyclopedia Title:** The title is a long, dry sentence containing methodological details, data sources, or definitions that belong in a subtitle or footnote.
-   **Audience Confusion:** Readers have to spend a long time looking at the chart before they understand what they are supposed to get out of it.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Add a Subtitle.** Keep your descriptive title, but add a narrative subtitle below it that states the main insight or question.
-   **Moderate Approach: Turn it into a Question.** Rephrase your descriptive title as the question your chart answers. For example, change "Projected Hot Days in 2050" to "Where will be cool enough to live in 2050?".
-   **Comprehensive Approach: State the Punchline.** Formulate a complete sentence that summarizes the single most important finding from your chart and use that as the title. For example, "In 2050, northern states will offer the most relief from extreme summer heat."

## Guidance

The legend (or key) should create a direct, simple mapping between the visual properties (like color, size, or shape) and the data values they represent. Avoid adding intermediate, abstract categories or scoring systems that readers must learn first.

## Why

Every layer of abstraction is an extra mental step for your audience. A direct legend reduces cognitive load, making the chart faster and easier to understand. If a reader has to learn a "star rating" or "index score" and then remember what that score means in terms of the actual data, you create a barrier to understanding and increase the chance of misinterpretation.

### Core Principle
Reduce the cognitive work required to decode your visualization; every unnecessary step is a chance for misinterpretation.

## When it applies

-   When visualizing quantitative data that has been binned into categories.
-   When using a color scale (sequential, diverging, or categorical) to represent data.
-   Anytime a chart includes a legend to explain what the visual marks mean.

## Exceptions

-   If the abstract layer is a widely understood standard that is more meaningful to the audience than the raw data (e.g., using "Category 5 Hurricane" instead of wind speeds).
-   When creating a composite index where the index score itself is the primary message, though its components should still be clearly explained elsewhere.

## Trade-offs

-   Using raw data values in the legend can sometimes be more complex or less "clean" than a simplified abstract score, potentially making the legend appear more cluttered.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Two-Step Decoding:** A reader has to look at the legend, learn a system (e.g., stars, grades, levels), and then find a separate explanation for what that system means.
-   **Arbitrary Scores:** The legend uses a custom-made index or score that has no pre-existing meaning for the audience.
-   **Questions about Meaning:** Viewers ask "What does a '4-star' county mean, exactly?"

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** If you must keep the abstract system, add the underlying data values in parentheses within the legend (e.g., "★★★★ (fewer than 30 days)").
-   **Comprehensive Approach: Redesign the Legend.** Remove the abstract layer entirely. Rewrite the legend labels to describe the data values directly. Instead of "5 Stars," use "< 30 hot days." This creates a direct and unambiguous connection between the color and its meaning.

## Guidance

Add text labels directly onto your chart—known as annotations—to point out and explain important data points, trends, or regions. Use them to provide context, explain a specific feature, or state a key conclusion.

## Why

Annotations guide your reader's attention to the most important parts of the visualization. They act as a narrative layer, helping you tell a story and answer the "So what?" question that the data raises. By placing explanations directly on the chart, you reduce the need for the reader to hunt for information in the legend or accompanying text, making your message more immediate and impactful.

### Core Principle
Guide your reader's eye and mind to the most important parts of the story.

## When it applies

-   When a specific data point, outlier, or trend is central to your message.
-   In explanatory charts where you are communicating a specific story to a general audience.
-   When you want to add a personal or contextual layer to the data.

## Exceptions

-   In purely exploratory visualizations or dashboards, where the user is meant to discover insights for themselves, pre-set annotations may be counterproductive.
-   Over-annotating can lead to a cluttered and confusing chart. Focus on the 1-3 most critical points.

## Trade-offs

-   Annotations add visual complexity. Poorly placed annotations can obscure data or make the chart feel messy.
-   They editorialize the chart, which may be inappropriate for contexts demanding strict neutrality.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The "Now, find the..." Problem:** You find yourself explaining the chart by telling people to "look for the peak in 2015" or "notice that one outlier in the corner." If it needs that much verbal explanation, it needs an annotation.
-   **Lost in the Data:** Readers look at the chart but miss the main point or focus on trivial details.
-   **Buried Insight:** The key takeaway is explained in a long paragraph of body text far away from the chart itself.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Bold a Label.** If you already have labels, make the most important one bold or a different color to draw attention.
-   **Moderate Approach: Add a Label with a Leader Line.** Identify the most important data point. Add a text label explaining its significance and use a simple, thin line to connect the text to the data point.
-   **Comprehensive Approach: Integrate Narrative Annotations.** Weave 2-3 brief annotations into the chart's empty spaces to tell a progressive story. For example, one annotation can set the scene, another can point out the main event, and a third can explain the outcome.

## Guidance

Structure your footnotes to be brief and scannable. Prioritize the most essential information: the data source and any critical limitations or assumptions that affect how the data should be interpreted.

## Why

Footnotes build trust and provide crucial context, but a dense wall of text is likely to be skipped entirely. By keeping footnotes concise, you increase the likelihood that your audience will actually read them. This achieves the goal of transparency while respecting the reader's time and attention. A good footnote provides just enough information to establish credibility and prevent misinterpretation.

### Core Principle
Provide transparency without overwhelming your audience. Essential context should be easy to find and digest.

## When it applies

-   Every visualization that uses data from an external source should have a source credit.
-   When the data involves predictions, significant transformations, or has limitations that the audience must know to interpret it correctly.

## Exceptions

-   In academic papers, legal documents, or scientific reports, detailed and lengthy footnotes with full methodological descriptions are often required and expected by the expert audience.
-   If a chart is purely illustrative and not based on specific data (e.g., a conceptual diagram), a footnote may not be necessary.

## Trade-offs

-   A concise footnote sacrifices completeness for readability. You may have to omit secondary sources or minor methodological details. This can be mitigated by linking to a more detailed methodology page.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The Footnote-as-Paragraph:** The footnote is a multi-sentence block of text that looks like a regular paragraph.
-   **The Kitchen Sink:** The footnote contains everything from the source URL, to definitions of terms, to author credits, to contact information.
-   **No one Reads It:** During user testing or feedback sessions, you notice that no one refers to or seems to have read the information in the footnote.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Use a "Source:" Prefix.** The simplest and most crucial step is to clearly label the data source. Start the line with "Source:" (e.g., "Source: U.S. Census Bureau, 2020").
-   **Moderate Approach: The One-Sentence Rule.** Try to distill all essential information into a single sentence. For example: "Source: NOAA summer forecasts for 2050; does not account for humidity."
-   **Comprehensive Approach: Layer the Information.** Provide the most critical information directly in the footnote. For users who want more, include a hyperlink with text like "See full methodology" that leads to a separate page with the exhaustive details.

## Guidance

On maps, add text labels for specific cities, regions, landmarks, or other points of interest that are relevant to your story. These labels should help orient the reader and connect the data patterns to real-world places.

## Why

A map without labels can be an abstract pattern of shapes and colors. Labels ground that data in reality. They help your audience orient themselves, recognize familiar places, and understand the practical implications of the data. If your map is meant to answer a question like "Where should I go?", labels provide the direct, actionable answers.

### Core Principle
Connect abstract data patterns to concrete, recognizable places to make information more meaningful and useful.

## When it applies

-   On choropleth, symbol, or other types of maps showing spatial data.
-   When the story involves specific locations that exemplify a trend or serve as important reference points.
-   When the map is intended to help users make decisions or understand the impact on specific communities.

## Exceptions

-   On very high-level or global maps where individual city labels would create overwhelming clutter. In these cases, labeling continents or countries may be more appropriate.
-   In highly abstract or schematic maps where the geographic precision is intentionally removed to focus solely on relationships (like some transit maps).

## Trade-offs

-   Adding labels increases the visual density of a map. You must be selective to avoid clutter that can obscure the underlying data.
-   Placing labels automatically can be algorithmically complex, and manual placement requires design effort.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The "Where is that?" Question:** Viewers can see an interesting pattern on the map but cannot identify the location without consulting another map.
-   **Abstract Shapes:** The map feels like a collection of abstract colored polygons rather than a representation of real places.
-   **Lack of Actionability:** The map shows a pattern but doesn't give the viewer enough information to act on it (e.g., it shows areas with low crime but doesn't name the neighborhoods).

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Label Extremes.** Identify the locations with the highest and lowest values in your dataset and add labels for them. This immediately provides context and highlights the range of the data.
-   **Moderate Approach: Label Reference Points.** Add labels for a few well-known cities or landmarks (e.g., New York, Los Angeles, Chicago) even if their data is not extreme. This helps the audience orient themselves on the map.
-   **Comprehensive Approach: Label for the Narrative.** Re-read your chart's title and central question. Identify 3-5 locations that are key characters in that story (e.g., the author's hometown, potential destinations, surprising outliers) and add labels and/or annotations for them.
