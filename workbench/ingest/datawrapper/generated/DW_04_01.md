## Guidance

Use a choropleth map when your geographic data exhibits clear regional patterns, such as clusters, gradients, or sharp contrasts between areas (e.g., urban vs. rural). If no spatial pattern exists, use a different chart type.

## Why

The main strength of a choropleth map is its ability to reveal the "where" of a story. It helps viewers quickly spot geographic trends that would be invisible in a table or a simple bar chart. If the values are randomly distributed across the map, there is no geographic story to tell, and the map format adds cognitive load without providing spatial insight.

## When it applies

-   You have data for defined geographic regions (countries, states, counties).
-   You suspect or want to investigate if there is a geographic component to your data's distribution.
-   Your analysis reveals clusters of high or low values, or a clear pattern like a north-south divide.

## Exceptions

-   **Reader Engagement:** Even without strong regional patterns, a map of a local area can be highly engaging for readers because they enjoy finding their own location ("the 'I am here' effect"). If your audience lives in the area being mapped, a choropleth can be justified for this reason alone.
-   **Reference Tool:** A map can serve as a simple geographic lookup tool, even without patterns, especially in an interactive format where users can explore data for their specific area.

## Trade-offs

-   **Pro (using map):** Excellent for showing spatial distributions and engaging readers with familiar geography.
-   **Con (using map without patterns):** Can be an inefficient and potentially confusing way to present data. It makes precise comparisons between regions difficult, a task for which a bar chart is far superior.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **"Salt and Pepper" Effect:** The map shows no clear clusters. High and low values are interspersed randomly, making it look like a noisy, chaotic image.
-   **The Main Takeaway Isn't Geographic:** If the key insight from your data is "Region X has the highest value," and there's no broader spatial story, a sorted bar chart will communicate that point more effectively.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Test for Patterns.** Before committing to a map, quickly scan your data. Is there a geographic logic to where the high and low values are? If not, proceed with caution.
-   **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Bar Chart.** If the main task is to compare or rank regions, a simple sorted bar chart is almost always better. It allows for easy and accurate comparison of values.
-   **Comprehensive Approach: Pair a Map with a Bar Chart.** Use a map to provide the geographic overview and engage the reader, but pair it with a bar chart or table that allows for easy ranking and comparison of the individual regions.
