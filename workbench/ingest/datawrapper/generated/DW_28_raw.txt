## Guidance

When visualizing categorical data where the categories have no natural order (e.g., countries, products, types of fruit), assign a unique and perceptually distinct hue (e.g., blue, green, orange) to each category. This is known as a **qualitative** color scale.

## Why

Different hues are pre-attentively processed by our brains as indicating separate, distinct items. Using shades of a single color (a sequential scale) would incorrectly imply a quantitative or ordinal relationship (e.g., that 'dark blue' is somehow "more" than 'light blue'), which does not exist in nominal data. This avoids misleading the viewer into seeing a pattern or order that isn't there.

## When it applies

- When your data consists of discrete, non-ordered categories (nominal data).
- When the primary task is to distinguish between different groups and look up their values.

## Exceptions

- **Highlighting**: If you want to draw attention to one specific category, it's effective to use a single distinct hue for that category and a muted gray for all others.
- **Grouping**: If your categories have a hierarchical structure, you can use distinct hues for the main parent categories and then use shades of each hue for their respective subcategories.

## Trade-offs

- Using many distinct hues (more than 7-10) can become visually overwhelming and chaotic. The colors can also become difficult to tell apart, a problem known as high cardinality.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Implied Ranking:** A bar chart of different companies or products uses light blue, medium blue, and dark blue, making viewers wonder if there's a ranking or order they are supposed to see.
- **Legend Overload:** Viewers have to constantly refer back to the legend to remember which color corresponds to which category. This is a sign the colors are not distinct enough or there are too many.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Switch to a Qualitative Palette.** Replace the sequential or diverging palette with a qualitative one where each color is a distinct hue.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Use a Tested Palette.** Instead of picking colors by hand, use a tool like ColorBrewer or Datawrapper's palette generator to find a pre-made, perceptually distinct qualitative palette. These are designed to maximize the difference between colors.

## Guidance

To represent data that has a natural order (e.g., low to high, worst to best, agree to disagree), use a **sequential** color scale. This typically involves varying the lightness of a single hue, from light to dark.

## Why

The human visual system naturally associates darkness with greater quantity or importance. A light-to-dark progression creates an intuitive and immediate visual mapping to the data's order, allowing for quick comparison and ranking without requiring the viewer to memorize a legend of arbitrary colors.

### Core Principle

Visual encodings should match the structure of the data. Ordered data deserves an ordered visual encoding, and the lightness of color is a strong ordered encoding.

## When it applies

- When visualizing ordinal data (e.g., survey responses from 'Strongly Disagree' to 'Strongly Agree').
- When visualizing binned or continuous quantitative data in choropleth maps, heatmaps, or colored bar charts.

## Exceptions

- **Diverging Data:** For data with a meaningful midpoint (e.g., values above/below a baseline, temperature change), a **diverging scale** (two hues moving from a neutral center) is more appropriate than a simple sequential scale.
- **Tracking Across Charts:** For tracking specific items across small multiples, using stable, distinct hues may be more effective than re-applying a sequential scale in each chart.

## Trade-offs

- A single-hue sequential scale can sometimes lack visual impact compared to a more colorful palette. Multi-hue sequential palettes (e.g., Viridis) can be a good compromise, but must be designed carefully to be perceptually uniform.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Rainbow Confusion:** Your chart uses a rainbow palette (red, orange, yellow, green, blue) to show magnitude. This is a classic mistake, as the perceived order of hues does not match the spectrum and the luminance values are uneven.
- **Qualitative for Quantitative:** A map of population density uses unrelated colors like pink, brown, and cyan, forcing the user to work hard to understand which areas are more or less dense.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Adopt a Single-Hue Sequence.** Change your color scheme to a simple light-to-dark progression of one color (e.g., light gray to dark gray, or light blue to dark blue).
- **Comprehensive Approach: Use a Perceptually-Uniform Palette.** Switch to a scientifically designed sequential palette like Viridis, Plasma, or the palettes from ColorBrewer. These are engineered to ensure that the perceived change in color matches the change in the data, and they perform well for colorblind users and in grayscale.

## Guidance

When your data has a two-level hierarchy (e.g., main categories and subcategories), assign a distinct hue to each main category. Then, use different shades or tints of that hue for its respective subcategories.

## Why

This technique leverages the Gestalt principle of similarity. The shared hue visually connects all subcategories to their parent, creating clear groups that are processed pre-attentively. The variation in shade then allows for distinction *within* the group. This creates an intuitive visual hierarchy that is easier to decode than a flat palette of many different colors.

## When it applies

- When you need to show both membership in a larger group and distinction within that group.
- For stacked bar charts, grouped bar charts, or treemaps with a clear categorical hierarchy.

## Exceptions

- **Too Many Subcategories:** If you have more than 3-4 subcategories per main category, the shades may become too difficult to tell apart. In this case, other encoding methods like faceting (small multiples) might be better.
- **Deep Hierarchies:** This technique works best for a simple two-level hierarchy. For deeper trees, other visualization types (like a treemap or sunburst) might be necessary.

## Trade-offs

- This approach reduces the total number of unique hues you can use, as you are "reserving" shades of one hue for a single group.
- It requires careful color selection to ensure that the shades are distinct enough from each other, and that a dark shade of one hue isn't confused with a light shade of another.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Flat Palette for Hierarchy:** Your chart uses a disparate collection of colors for items that belong to logical groups, forcing the viewer to mentally reconstruct the hierarchy.
- **Ambiguous Shades:** The dark shade of your "blue" category looks very similar to the light shade of your "purple" category, causing confusion.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Manually Group Your Colors.** Identify your main groups. Assign a distinct hue to each. Then, for each group's members, manually select lighter and darker versions of the parent hue.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Use a Hierarchical Color Tool.** Use a tool or script that can generate hierarchical palettes. This involves defining a parent color and then programmatically generating perceptually distinct steps away from it for the child elements.

## Guidance

To draw attention to a specific data point, category, or region, render it in a distinct, often saturated, color. Render all other 'contextual' data in a single, muted color, such as gray.

## Why

This approach minimizes cognitive load by telling the viewer exactly where to look. The highlighted element stands out immediately due to its unique color (a "preattentive attribute"), while the grayed-out elements provide necessary context for comparison without competing for attention. It's a powerful way to guide a narrative and make a clear point.

## When it applies

- When your primary goal is to tell a story about one specific part of the data in relation to the whole.
- In presentations or reports where you want to control the narrative and focus the audience's attention.
- When annotating a specific point or trend in a chart.

## Exceptions

- **Exploratory Tools:** If the goal is to encourage free exploration and comparison between all categories equally (e.g., in an interactive dashboard for analysts), this technique would be counterproductive as it imposes a specific narrative.
- **Multiple Highlights:** Highlighting more than 2-3 categories with different colors dilutes the effect and can become as confusing as using no highlighting at all.

## Trade-offs

- You sacrifice the ability to easily distinguish between the non-highlighted categories. They blend into the background, which is the point, but means detailed comparisons among them become impossible.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Competing Colors:** Your chart tries to make a point about one region's performance, but all regions are colored with different, bright hues, forcing the reader to search for the one you're talking about.
- **Everything Is Important:** The chart uses multiple bright, "urgent" colors, creating a visually loud and stressful experience where nothing stands out because everything is trying to.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Pick a Focus, Mute the Rest.** Decide what the single most important story in your chart is. Give that data series a strong color. Change every other data series to a light or medium gray.
- **Moderate Approach: Add an Annotation.** After highlighting a series in color, add a text annotation directly on the chart that explains *why* it's highlighted. This connects the visual emphasis to the narrative insight.

## Guidance

When visualizing binary data (e.g., yes/no, present/absent) or a small number of simple categories, consider using shades of gray (e.g., black, gray, and white) instead of distinct hues.

## Why

A grayscale palette is universally perceivable and eliminates any risk of misinterpretation due to color vision deficiency. It can also create a more sophisticated and less "busy" aesthetic, allowing other visual elements, like annotations or a single highlighted color, to stand out more effectively. It forces you to rely on brightness differences, which is a robust encoding channel.

## When it applies

- For binary data (e.g., yes/no, private/public).
- When you have only 2-3 categories that don't have strong, conventional color associations.
- As the "context" color in a chart that uses a single bright hue for highlighting.

## Exceptions

- **Branding:** If you must adhere to a brand style guide that requires specific colors.
- **Convention:** If the categories have strong, conventional color associations that aid interpretation (e.g., red/blue for political parties in the US), using grayscale might confuse the audience or strip away important context.

## Trade-offs

- You lose the ability to use color for emotional or symbolic association. A grayscale palette is neutral and "data-forward," which may not be appropriate for all communication goals.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Unnecessary Color:** Your chart uses two bright, saturated colors for a simple "before/after" comparison, making the graphic feel visually loud and distracting from the main message.
- **Accessibility Fail:** A chart uses red and green for a "pass/fail" dashboard, which is unreadable for the most common form of colorblindness. A simple light/dark gray scheme would have been instantly accessible.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Switch to Grayscale.** Change the category colors to two distinct shades of gray (e.g., dark gray and light gray, or black and light gray). Ensure they have a strong contrast ratio.
- **Moderate Approach: Use Grayscale Plus a Highlight.** If you have three categories, consider making two of them shades of gray and one of them a color to draw attention to it. This combines the clarity of grayscale with the attention-grabbing power of a highlight.

## Guidance

When using small multiples to show how items change or rank across different conditions or time periods, assign a stable, distinct hue to each item. Maintain that color-to-item mapping across all charts in the series.

## Why

This technique turns color into a persistent visual label for each entity (e.g., "France is always green"). It allows the viewer to visually track an item's trajectory across the charts without having to re-read labels in each panel. This offloads the cognitive work of identification, freeing up the viewer's mental resources to focus on interpreting the pattern of change. This is a deliberate and powerful exception to the rule of using sequential colors for ranked data.

## When it applies

- Specifically for small multiples (also called facets or trellis plots) where the primary task is to compare an entity to itself across different contexts or to track its relative position over time.
- Common in line charts showing trends for multiple categories, or grouped bar charts across time.

## Exceptions

- If the primary task is to analyze the distribution *within* each small multiple, and tracking a single entity is secondary, then using a consistent sequential scale in each chart (e.g., dark blue is always #1 rank) would be better. The choice depends on what comparison you want to make easiest.

## Trade-offs

- You lose the ability to quickly see the rank of an item *within* a single chart based on its color. A green bar could be rank #1 in the first chart and rank #5 in the second. The viewer must use position (the y-axis) to judge rank within a chart. This approach prioritizes cross-chart comparison over within-chart comparison.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Recoding Hell:** Your viewer complains, "I keep losing track of which line is which" when looking at a series of charts. They have to repeatedly look back and forth between the legend and the data across multiple panels.
- **Color Meaning Changes:** The color red means "Highest" in Chart 1, but "Third Highest" in Chart 2, because the item that was highest has changed. This inconsistent meaning for the color encoding is a sign of high cognitive load.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Assign Stable Hues.** Create a consistent mapping: one category, one color. Apply this palette consistently across all your small multiples.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Combine Stable Hues with Highlighting.** Assign stable hues to all items, but make the key item(s) of interest more saturated while desaturating the others. This gives you both stable tracking and a clear focal point.

## Guidance

Whether your color palette is qualitative (hues) or sequential (shades), ensure that each color step is clearly distinguishable from its neighbors. Crucially, the colors must also have different levels of brightness (luminance) so that the differences are maintained when viewed in grayscale or by someone with a color vision deficiency.

## Why

If colors are too similar, they fail at their primary job: to distinguish data. If they don't vary in brightness, their distinctions disappear for many colorblind users and when printed in black and white. A palette with good perceptual steps and brightness variation is robust, accessible, and effective.

### Core Principle

Visual encodings must be discriminable to be useful. For color, this means creating sufficient difference in both hue and brightness to ensure the encoding works for all users in all contexts.

## When it applies

- Always, when using color to encode categories or quantities. This is a foundational rule for creating effective and accessible visualizations.

## Exceptions

- There are no known exceptions. This is a fundamental requirement for reliable visual encoding with color.

## Trade-offs

- Achieving perfect perceptual steps and brightness variation can sometimes limit aesthetic choices. You may not be able to use your exact brand colors if they don't meet these criteria. Functionality and accessibility should take priority over pure aesthetics.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Grayscale Collapse:** You convert your chart to grayscale (or print it in black and white), and two different categories (e.g., a medium green and a medium red) become the exact same shade of gray, making them indistinguishable.
- **Subtle Neighbors:** Your legend or chart contains two colors that are very similar (e.g., a light teal next to a light green), and you have to look closely to tell them apart even in full color.
- **The Squint Test:** If you squint your eyes while looking at the chart, do different colored categories blend into one? If so, their brightness values are too similar.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Validate Your Palette.** Use a tool like Viz Palette or a colorblindness simulator to check your current palette. If it fails, make adjustments to the brightness of the failing colors until they are distinct.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Use a Perceptually-Uniform Palette.** Don't invent your own palettes. Use professionally designed, tested palettes from sources like ColorBrewer, or perceptually-uniform palettes like Viridis, Magma, or Plasma. These are engineered to solve this exact problem.
