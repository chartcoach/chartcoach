## Guidance

Place labels directly on or next to the chart elements they describe, such as lines, bars, or pie slices, instead of relying on a separate color key or legend.

## Why

This reduces cognitive load by eliminating the need for viewers to constantly shift their eyes back and forth between the data and the legend. Direct labeling makes the connection between a label and its data point immediate and effortless, which speeds up comprehension.

### Core Principle

Reduce the cognitive work required to decode your visualization. By placing explanatory text exactly where it's needed, you remove an unnecessary step of mapping a legend to the data.

## When it applies

- When a chart uses color to distinguish between multiple categories or series, such as a multi-line chart, a stacked bar chart, or a pie/donut chart.
- In explanatory charts where rapid, clear communication is the primary goal.

## Exceptions

- **Small Screens:** On very small screens (e.g., mobile devices) where space is extremely limited, a legend at the top might be a necessary compromise to avoid labels overlapping and becoming unreadable.
- **Dense Plots:** In highly dense charts, like a scatter plot with many overlapping categories or a line chart with dozens of tangled series, a legend may be less cluttered than attempting to label every element directly. In this case, interactive highlighting is a better solution.

## Trade-offs

- Direct labels can sometimes clutter the chart, especially with many series or in dense data regions.
- They may require manual placement and adjustment to avoid overlapping with data marks or other labels, which can take more time than an automatic legend.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Back-and-Forth:** Viewers have to repeatedly look between the legend and the chart to decode which line is which.
- **Label Hunting:** It is difficult to match a color in a dense chart to its corresponding entry in a long legend.
- **The Squint Test:** Squinting at the chart makes it impossible to connect the colored marks to their meaning without consulting the legend.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Reorder the Legend.** If you must use a legend, order the items in it to match the order of the data. For example, in a line chart, list the series in the same top-to-bottom order as the lines appear at the end of the chart.
- **Moderate Redesign: Use Direct Labels.** Use your tool's features to add labels directly. For line charts, place labels at the end of the lines. For pie charts, place labels inside or next to their slices.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Redesign to Avoid a Legend.** Use "small multiples" to give each category its own chart with a clear title, eliminating the need for colored series. Alternatively, for a narrative chart, highlight and directly label only the most important series and de-emphasize the rest in gray.

## Guidance

Include the unit of measurement (e.g., `_`%`_`, `_`$`_`, `_`kg`_`, `_`m`_`) directly within axis labels, tooltips, and annotations, not just in a single title or description.

## Why

Repeating the unit keeps the context directly with the data, preventing misinterpretation and reducing the viewer's memory load. It ensures that even when focusing on a specific part of the chart—like a tooltip or a single axis—the meaning of the numbers remains clear and unambiguous.

### Core Principle

Don't make your audience remember context. Present information as a complete, self-contained package wherever it appears.

## When it applies

- Any visualization displaying quantitative data with a specific unit of measurement.
- Interactive charts with tooltips.

## Exceptions

- In an extremely simple chart where the unit is universally understood and stated clearly in the title (e.g., "Unemployment Rate (%)"), it might be omitted from axis labels to reduce minor clutter, but it should still always appear in tooltips.

## Trade-offs

- This may add very slight visual clutter, but the benefit of clarity far outweighs this minimal cost.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Naked Numbers:** Tooltips or labels show just a number (e.g., "15.3") without the unit, leaving the interpretation to the viewer.
- **Unit Hunting:** Viewers have to scan the title or description to remember if the axis shows millions, thousands, or percentages.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Edit Formats.** In your charting tool, edit the number formatting for axes and tooltips. Append the unit symbol or prefix (e.g., `%`, `_`$`_`, `_`k`_`, `_`m`_`) to the number format string.
- **Moderate Approach: Rephrase Annotations.** When writing annotations, phrase the text to naturally include the unit. For example, write "peaked at $45M in sales" instead of "peaked here at 45."
- **Comprehensive Approach: Systematize It.** Create a style guide or template for your charts that specifies the inclusion of units in all relevant text elements as a default rule.

Design tooltips to show not just the numerical value, but also the category and unit it represents. Phrase them as a complete piece of information.

## Why

Informative tooltips serve as a "just-in-time" reminder of what the data point represents, reinforcing the chart's message and reducing cognitive load. Instead of just showing a number, a well-designed tooltip answers the question, "What am I looking at?" directly at the point of interaction.

- Requires a small amount of extra effort to configure the tooltip format, but has no significant downsides.

Add textual annotations directly onto the chart to point out outliers, explain trends, define key thresholds, or provide crucial context for specific data points.

## Why

Annotations transform a chart from a passive display of data into an active piece of communication. They guide the reader's attention to what's important, answer potential questions before they are asked, and provide narrative context that the raw data alone cannot. This makes the chart more insightful, engaging, and memorable.

Don't just show data; tell a story. Guide your reader to the insights you want them to see.

## Guidance

Use deliberate variations in font size, weight (boldness), and color contrast to guide the reader's eye. Make the most important text (like the title or a key finding) the most prominent, and less important text (like the source line or footnotes) the least prominent.

## Why

A clear visual hierarchy tells the reader what to look at first, second, and third. It structures the information, reduces cognitive load, and makes the visualization scannable and easier to navigate. Without it, all text competes for attention, creating a flat, confusing layout.

### Core Principle

Design is about making choices. Decide what's most important and use visual contrast to signal that importance to your audience.

- In any visualization that contains more than one type of text element (e.g., title, description, labels, source). This applies to virtually all charts.

## Exceptions

- None. A clear hierarchy is always beneficial for communication.

- This is a fundamental design practice with no significant trade-offs. The only "cost" is the time it takes to be intentional about typographic choices.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Wall of Same:** All the text on the chart has the same size, weight, and color, giving no clue as to what's most important or where to start reading.
- **Competing Elements:** A faint, gray source line is given the same visual weight as the main title, or an annotation is more visually demanding than the title.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Emphasize the Title, De-emphasize the Source.** Make your chart title bold. Make the source line and any footnotes smaller and gray. This immediately creates two levels of hierarchy.
- **Moderate Approach: Establish Three Levels.** Define a simple, clear hierarchy and apply it consistently. For example:
    1.  **Title:** Large, bold, black.
    2.  **Labels & Annotations:** Medium size, regular weight, dark gray.
    3.  **Description & Source:** Small size, regular weight, light gray.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Design from the Bottom Up.** As suggested in the article, set all text to be small, light, and gray. Then, go back and intentionally add emphasis one level at a time, asking yourself: "What is the single most important piece of text? What's next?" This forces you to make conscious, motivated decisions about the hierarchy.

## Guidance

Use a limited and clearly distinct set of font sizes to create a clean and organized typographic hierarchy. For most charts, two to four distinct sizes are sufficient for all text elements.

## Why

Using too many different font sizes creates a cluttered, chaotic appearance that undermines the visual hierarchy and looks unprofessional. A restrained, consistent typographic scale makes the design feel more intentional and organized, which in turn makes the hierarchy clearer and easier for the reader to process.

### Core Principle

Create clarity and harmony through constraint. Use fewer elements to make the remaining ones more powerful.

## When it applies

- When designing any visualization with multiple text elements like a title, subtitle, axis labels, annotations, and a source line.

## Exceptions

- None known. Simplicity and consistency in typography are universally beneficial.

## Trade-offs

- None. This is a best practice that improves both aesthetics and clarity without sacrificing information.

- **Typographic Ransom Note:** Your chart uses 5, 6, or more different font sizes, making it look chaotic and unplanned.
- **Indistinguishable Sizes:** You are using very similar font sizes (e.g., 11px and 12px) that don't create a clear, perceptible step in the visual hierarchy.
- **Relying on Size Alone:** You are using a new font size for every new level of emphasis, instead of using other variables like weight (bold) or color.

- **Moderate Approach: Establish a Type Scale.** Define a simple typographic scale with 3-4 distinct steps and stick to it. For example: Title (24px), Subtitle/Key Annotations (16px), Body/Labels (12px), and Source/Footnotes (10px).

## Guidance

For multi-line blocks of text such as titles, descriptions, and annotations, use left-alignment. Avoid center-alignment for anything more than a single, short line of text.

## Why

Left-aligned text provides a consistent vertical starting point for each line, allowing the reader's eye to easily find the beginning of the next line. In contrast, centered text has a ragged left edge, forcing the reader to work harder on every line break. Furthermore, a strong vertical edge created by left-aligned text creates a cleaner, more organized layout that can align neatly with other chart elements.

## When it applies

- For any text block with more than one line.
- For single-line titles or labels where alignment with other chart elements creates a more structured layout.

## Exceptions

- A short, single-line title can be centered over a chart without harming readability, though left-alignment often looks more modern and structured.
- For very formal, symmetrical presentations like an invitation or a title page, centering is a common convention.

## Trade-offs

- The only trade-off is giving up a symmetrical, formal aesthetic, which is often less important than readability and layout structure in a data visualization.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Ragged Edges:** Your multi-line title or annotation has uneven, "ragged" edges on both the left and right, making it look messy and unprofessional.
- **Slower Scan:** You find your eyes have to work slightly harder to track from the end of one line to the beginning of the next in a block of text.
- **Awkward Gaps:** Centered text creates awkward empty spaces that disrupt the clean lines of the chart grid.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Switch to Left-Align.** In your chart editor, find the text alignment setting for your title, description, and annotations, and change it from "center" to "left."
- **Moderate Approach: Align with the Chart.** Position your left-aligned title block so its left edge aligns with the left edge of the chart's plot area or y-axis. This creates a strong, clean vertical line in the overall composition and reinforces the structure of the visualization.

When text is placed over other visual elements like gridlines, data lines, or colored map areas, add a subtle outline (or "halo") to the text that matches the chart's background color.

## Why

The outline creates a small, clean buffer around the letters, effectively "carving out" space and preventing background elements from visually interfering with the letterforms. ## Guidance

Write titles, descriptions, and annotations in simple, clear language. Avoid jargon, acronyms, and overly formal or academic phrasing. Actively rephrase official dataset descriptions to be more direct and understandable.

## Why

Clear communication is the primary goal of most visualizations. Complex, jargon-filled language creates a cognitive barrier, making your audience work harder or simply give up. Simple phrasing ensures your message is accessible to the widest possible audience, including non-experts, non-native speakers, and readers with different literacy levels.

### Core Principle

To write clearly, imagine you are explaining the chart to an intelligent friend who is not an expert in the subject. Use the words you would use in that casual, direct conversation.

## When it applies

- Always, but it is especially critical when visualizing for a general or non-expert audience.

## Exceptions

- When creating a chart for a highly specialized, expert audience (e.g., in a scientific paper or a technical brief), using precise, shared jargon can be more efficient and appropriate. Even then, unnecessary complexity should be avoided.

## Trade-offs

- Simplifying language can sometimes feel like "dumbing down" the data, but it is actually a mark of respect for the reader's time and attention. The trade-off is between sounding "academic" or "official" and being genuinely understood.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Academic Tone:** Your title sounds like it was copied from a research paper abstract (e.g., "A visualization denoting the central estimate of the average extent to which citizens support...").
- **Jargon Alert:** You are using insider acronyms or technical terms that a general reader would not know without a glossary.
- **Copypasta:** You have copied and pasted the description directly from a metadata file or an API response.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: The 'Friend Test'.** Read your title and description out loud. Does it sound like something a human would actually say to another human? If not, rephrase it until it does.
- **Moderate Approach: From 'What it is' to 'What it shows'.** Instead of a descriptive title that states what the chart *is* (e.g., "A Chart of Sales Over Time"), write an assertive title that states what the chart *shows* (e.g., "Sales Have More Than Doubled Since 2020").
- **Comprehensive Approach: Edit for Plain Language.** Review all text on your chart and actively replace complex words with simpler ones (e.g., use "use" instead of "utilize"), eliminate prepositional phrases, and break long sentences into shorter ones.

## Guidance

Use a clear, conversational, and impactful statement for the main title that summarizes the chart's key finding. Relegate the precise, technical details about the data (e.g., specific date ranges, population definitions, methodology caveats) to the description or notes, which should be styled to be less prominent.

## Why

This strategy hooks the reader with a clear, memorable takeaway first. The necessary but complex details are still available for those who need them, but they don't obscure the primary message. People remember simple statements and stories, not methodological footnotes. By delivering the main point upfront, you respect the reader's time and increase the chance your message will stick.

## When it applies

- When presenting data to a general audience, especially in a storytelling context like a news article, report, or presentation.

## Exceptions

- In formal scientific or academic contexts, precision and methodology may need to be stated upfront, as the validity of the finding is paramount for an expert audience.
- In legal or regulatory documents where the precise definition is the most important piece of information.

## Trade-offs

- There is a fine line between simplifying for clarity and oversimplifying to the point of being misleading. The main message in the title must still be a truthful and defensible representation of the data. The details in the notes are essential for maintaining intellectual honesty and credibility.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Footnote in the Title:** Your title is cluttered with parenthetical clarifications, date ranges, and asterisks (e.g., "Change in Rent (Q1 2020-Q1 2022)*").
- **Buried Lede:** The most interesting finding is hidden in a small annotation or the surrounding article text, while the chart's title is a dry, descriptive label like "Rent Prices by County."
- **Reader Fatigue:** The title is so long and dense that readers might skip it entirely.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Swap Title and Subtitle.** Find the most interesting conclusion in your chart (it might already be in your subtitle or an annotation) and make it the main title. Move the old, descriptive title to the subtitle/description field.
- **Moderate Approach: Structure a Text Hierarchy.** Intentionally design your chart's text into three layers of information, styled according to the visual hierarchy:
    1.  **Title:** The main, conversational takeaway.
    2.  **Subtitle/Description:** A sentence providing the "what, where, when."
    3.  **Notes/Source:** The fine print: methodology, specific definitions, and data source.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Write the Headline First.** Before you even finalize the chart design, decide on the single most important message you want to convey. Write that message as a short, clear headline. Then, build the chart and all its supporting text elements to prove and explain that one headline.

## Guidance

Format numbers to be as simple as is appropriate for the context. Avoid showing excessive decimal places and use standard suffixes to abbreviate large numbers (e.g., "12.8k" for 12,831, "12.8m" for 12,831,283).

## Why

Unnecessary precision adds visual clutter and cognitive load without improving understanding. Readers rarely need, or remember, numbers with many decimal places or a long string of digits. Simplified, rounded numbers are easier to scan, compare, and recall, making your visualization more effective and approachable.

## When it applies

- In any text element that displays numbers: axis labels, data point labels, annotations, and tooltips.

## Exceptions

- **High-Stakes Precision:** When high precision is scientifically, financially, or engineeringly critical and the audience expects it (e.g., pharmaceutical dosages, financial statements to the cent).
- **Small Ranges:** When the key differences in your data only appear after the decimal point, more precision is required to show the variation.
- Even in these cases, consider if the full precision is needed everywhere, or if it can be reserved for tooltips while the main labels are simplified.

## Trade-offs

- Rounding and abbreviation inherently remove some information. You must ensure that the rounding does not distort the data's message, create false equivalences, or violate the expectations of an expert audience.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Decimal Overload:** Your labels show numbers like `22.428%` or `12,831,783.92`.
- **Spurious Precision:** The data displays a level of precision that is not meaningful or real in the underlying measurements (e.g., survey results reported to three decimal places).
- **Jagged Alignment:** In a table or a list of labels, numbers with varying numbers of decimal places do not align correctly on the decimal point, looking messy.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Change the Number Format.** In your charting tool's settings, change the default number format to show zero or one decimal place. Enable a format that abbreviates large numbers (e.g., using `k` for thousands, `m` for millions).
- **Moderate Approach: Use Smart Formatting and Tooltips.** Use a "smart" number format that removes unnecessary trailing zeroes (e.g., `27.0%` becomes `27%` while `22.4%` stays as is). Move the full, precise numbers into interactive tooltips, so they are available on demand without cluttering the main view.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Define Precision by Context.** Establish rules for number formatting based on the data type and magnitude. For example: percentages get one decimal place, dollar amounts over $1M are abbreviated to one decimal place (e.g., $3.4m), and population counts are rounded to the nearest thousand.
