## Guidance

Instead of just letting the viewer interpret a line's slope, add annotations that explicitly state the scale and significance of the change. Use clear, impactful language (e.g., "doubled," "fell by half," "7x more likely") to quantify the difference between key points.

## Why

Trend lines can be visually deceptive depending on the aspect ratio and axis scales. A direct annotation removes ambiguity and delivers the key takeaway with greater impact. Stating that a population "quadrupled" is more powerful and memorable—it has more "bonkersness vibes"—than just showing a line going up.

### Core Principle

Make the most important conclusion as easy as possible to grasp. If the magnitude of change is the story, state it directly on the chart.

## When it applies

- When the primary message of a chart is the scale or magnitude of a change over time or the difference between two important values.
- When you want to add emotional impact or emphasize the significance of a finding.

## Exceptions

- When the exact magnitude is less important than the overall pattern, volatility, or shape of the trend.
- In highly dense charts with many lines, where annotations for each one would cause too much clutter. In this case, a summary table or text might be better.

## Trade-offs

- Annotations can add visual clutter if overused.
- They focus attention on specific points (e.g., start and end), potentially drawing attention away from the journey or variability in between.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Understated Impact:** A dramatic, real-world change (e.g., a 400% increase) looks like a gentle, unimpressive slope on the chart.
- **Reader Math:** Viewers have to hover over points or trace gridlines to figure out the start and end values and then calculate the difference themselves.
- **Lack of "Wow":** The chart presents impressive numbers, but fails to elicit a surprised or emotional response from the audience because the scale of the change isn't apparent.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Label Start and End Points.** Add data labels with the exact values to the first and last points of a time series. This is better than nothing, as it provides the raw numbers for calculation.
- **Moderate approach: Annotate the Calculated Change.** Add a single annotation that does the math for the viewer. E.g., "+300%" or "An increase of 1.0B people". Place it near the trend line for context.
- **Comprehensive approach: Use a Narrative Annotation.** Craft a short sentence that frames the magnitude in a more relatable way, as shown in the article's example. Instead of just "+35 years," write "...and can expect to live 35 years longer." This integrates the number into a more human-centered story.
