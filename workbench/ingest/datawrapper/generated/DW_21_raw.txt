## Guidance

Instead of relying solely on a numerical zero for the baseline of an index or relative-change chart, add a clear text label directly on the axis to explain what that baseline represents.

## Why

A number like "0" is abstract and requires interpretation. A descriptive label (e.g., "Rate in Q3 2023" or "2019 Average") provides immediate, unambiguous context. This anchors the reader's understanding of all other values on the chart, which are relative to this baseline. It reduces cognitive load and helps prevent misinterpretation.

### Core Principle

Make the reference point of a comparison explicit and easy to understand.

## When it applies

-   When using an index, a percentage change, or any other relative measure where one point in time or a specific value serves as the reference (the "zero" point).
-   This is especially crucial for general audiences or anyone who may be unfamiliar with the specific indexed data being presented.

## Exceptions

For highly specialized audiences who are extremely familiar with the specific index being used (e.g., economists looking at the Consumer Price Index), an explicit label might be considered redundant, but it rarely hurts clarity.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Baseline Ambiguity:** Viewers ask, "What does the zero line mean?"
-   **Context Hunting:** Readers must hunt through the title, subtitle, or notes to decipher the reference point.
-   **Misinterpretation:** Viewers mistake relative values for absolute ones (e.g., thinking a value of 0 on an employment index chart means "zero employment").

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix:** Add an annotation pointing to the baseline with an explanatory text.
-   **Comprehensive Approach:** In your charting tool, directly replace the '0' tick mark label with a descriptive text label. Many tools, like Datawrapper, allow for custom axis labels to achieve this.

## Guidance

On an axis with complex or relative units (like an index or percentage difference), add an explanatory annotation next to a representative tick mark to show readers how to interpret the values.

## Why

Complex units like "indexed difference" are not intuitive. A small annotation, such as labeling the "-5" tick as "-5% from 2023," provides a concrete example that teaches readers how to decode the entire axis. This makes the chart more self-contained and significantly lowers the barrier to understanding.

### Core Principle

Provide a key or legend for your encoding, even for axes. Don't assume readers understand your units.

## When it applies

-   When your axis doesn't use simple, universally understood units like dollars, temperature, or absolute counts.
-   This is vital for indices, standardized scores, log scales, or any custom-calculated metric being shown to a general audience.

## Exceptions

This is unnecessary for standard, easily understood units where the meaning is clear from the axis title (e.g., "Temperature (°C)", "Revenue ($M)", "Population").

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Unit Confusion:** Viewers are unsure what a value of "-10" actually means in the real world.
-   **Decoding Effort:** Viewers have to stop and mentally perform calculations to understand the scale.
-   **Over-reliance on Text:** The chart's main description needs a long paragraph just to explain the y-axis.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix:** Add a single text annotation near a prominent gridline. Combine the numerical value with a plain-language explanation (e.g., "-10 means 10% lower than today").
-   **Comprehensive Approach:** Integrate the explanation seamlessly into the axis design, as shown in the Datawrapper example where "-5% from 2023" is placed clearly and elegantly next to the -5% tick.

## Guidance

If your data represents a directional change (positive/negative) or a specific unit (like a percentage), consistently include the relevant symbol (e.g., "+", "-", "%") in all numerical displays, including axis ticks, data labels, and interactive tooltips.

## Why

Repetition reinforces meaning. Consistently showing a value as "-11%" instead of just "11" prevents ambiguity and repeatedly reminds the reader that they are looking at a value that is *less than* the baseline and is a *percentage*. This reduces the cognitive load of having to remember the scale's context.

### Core Principle

Use visual redundancy to improve clarity and reduce the chance of misinterpretation.

## When it applies

-   When visualizing data that represents a deviation from a baseline (e.g., profit/loss, surplus/deficit, change from average).
-   When data is in a specific unit like percentages that might otherwise be forgotten.

## Exceptions

If all values are of the same type (e.g., all negative), and the axis title already makes this abundantly clear (e.g., "Decrease from Peak"), symbols may be omitted for visual simplicity, but this is a risky trade-off that increases cognitive load.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Ambiguous Numbers:** A value of "10" on an axis could be interpreted as +10, -10, or 10%.
-   **Forgotten Context:** A viewer looks at a tooltip value of "11" and momentarily forgets the context, mistaking it for an absolute value.
-   **Inconsistent Formatting:** The axis has a "%" sign in the title, but the labels and tooltips are just plain numbers.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix:** Update the number formatting for your tooltips to include the necessary symbols. This is especially important for interactive charts.
-   **Comprehensive Approach:** Systematically apply a custom number format (e.g., `+0%;-0%` or `#,##0%`) to all numerical representations of the data on the chart: axis labels, direct data labels, and tooltips.

## Guidance

Before publishing, show your visualization to a few people who represent your intended audience. Ask them what the chart is telling them to identify confusing elements that you, the creator, have become blind to.

## Why

As the creator, you are too close to the project and the data; this is often called the "curse of knowledge." You already know what the chart is supposed to show. Fresh eyes, especially from your target audience, are the best way to discover if your message is unclear, your visual encoding is confusing, or if your chart can be misinterpreted.

### Core Principle

You are not your user. The only way to know if a communication is effective is to test it with the intended receivers.

## When it applies

This is a universal principle for any visualization intended for an audience other than yourself. It's especially critical when using complex data, abstract chart types, or when communicating with a non-expert audience.

## Exceptions

This is not necessary for purely personal data exploration or analysis where you are the only audience.

## Trade-offs

Testing takes time and effort, which may be in short supply on a deadline. However, the time saved by avoiding confusion and corrections after publication—and the benefit of communicating more effectively—often outweighs the initial investment.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Creator Bias:** You are the only person who has seen the chart before it goes live.
-   **Long Explanations:** You find yourself writing a very long caption or feeling the need to verbally explain the chart to everyone who sees it.
-   **Post-Publication Confusion:** After publishing, you receive questions from readers who misunderstood the chart's main point, or your work is cited incorrectly.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix ("The Coworker Test"):** Show your chart to a colleague from a different team. Ask them one question: "What is the main story this chart is telling you?" Do not lead them or explain it first.
-   **Moderate Approach ("The 3-User Test"):** Find 2-3 people who fit your audience profile. Show them the chart and ask them to "think aloud" as they look at it. Note where they hesitate, what questions they ask, and what conclusions they draw.
-   **Comprehensive Approach (Formal Usability Testing):** Conduct structured testing sessions with a script and specific tasks (e.g., "Using this chart, can you tell me which country had the biggest drop in 2020?"). This provides more rigorous, structured feedback.
