## Guidance

Every time color is used to represent data values or categories, explain what those colors mean. Use a color key, direct labels, or an annotated legend integrated into the chart's title or text.

## Why

Unexplained visual marks are meaningless. A legend is essential for decoding the visualization. Without it, the reader cannot understand what the colors represent, making the chart useless and frustrating.

### Core Principle

Make visualizations self-contained and decodable. Don't assume the reader can guess the meaning of your visual encodings.

## When it applies

- Any chart that uses color to encode categorical or quantitative data. This includes choropleth maps, line charts with multiple series, stacked bar charts, scatter plots with colored categories, etc.

## Exceptions

- When there is only one color used in the chart (e.g., a simple blue bar chart), and the meaning of the bars is obvious from the axes and title.
- When colors are purely decorative and do not encode any data (this practice itself is generally discouraged).

## Trade-offs

- A legend takes up space. Integrating the legend into the chart (e.g., direct labels) can sometimes create clutter if not done carefully.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Mystery Chart:** A chart uses multiple colors, but there is no key to explain them.
- **Guesswork Required:** The reader is forced to guess what the colors mean based on the title or surrounding text.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add a Standard Legend.** Most charting tools can automatically generate a color legend. Place it in a clear, unobtrusive position close to the chart.
- **Moderate Redesign: Use Direct Labels.** Instead of a separate legend, place labels directly on or next to the chart elements (e.g., at the end of each line in a line chart). This reduces the back-and-forth eye movement between the chart and the legend.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Integrate the Legend into Text.** Weave the color key into the title, subtitle, or annotations. For example: "The unemployment rate fell in Germany (orange) but rose in France (blue)." This method is highly effective for storytelling.
