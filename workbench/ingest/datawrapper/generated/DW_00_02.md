## Guidance

In an explanatory visualization, use a single bright or saturated color to highlight the most important category or data points for your message, and color all other elements in a neutral, muted color like gray.

## Why

This technique acts as a visual guide, immediately drawing the viewer's eye to the most important part of the data story. By de-emphasizing non-essential information, you reduce cognitive load and prevent the viewer from getting lost. This makes your main point more prominent, memorable, and easier to understand.

### Core Principle
Make the most important information the easiest to see. Use preattentive attributes like color to create a strong visual hierarchy that mirrors the hierarchy of importance in your message.

## When it applies

- In explanatory visualizations for articles, reports, or presentations where you have a specific insight to communicate.
- When you have many categories but only one or a few are central to your narrative (e.g., "your country," "your company," "the biggest change").
- In "spaghetti graphs" (line charts with many overlapping lines) to trace a single, important story.

## Exceptions

- In exploratory visualizations where the goal is to allow the user to freely browse and compare all categories equally. Highlighting would bias this exploration.
- When all categories are of equal importance to the message.

## Trade-offs

- **Gained:** A clear focal point; a stronger, more guided narrative; reduced cognitive load for the viewer.
- **Sacrificed:** The ability for viewers to easily examine and compare the de-emphasized "grayed-out" data. This approach is intentionally opinionated.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Where's Waldo?" Chart:** A complex chart where the main takeaway is buried among a sea of equally-weighted visual information.
- **Audience Confusion:** Viewers are unsure where to look first or what the main point of the chart is.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Find Your Story, Then Highlight It.**
  1.  Write down the single most important sentence you want your viewer to take away from the chart.
  2.  Identify which data category is the primary evidence for that sentence.
  3.  In your chart, change that category's color to something bold and attention-grabbing.
  4.  Change all other data categories to a light, neutral gray.
