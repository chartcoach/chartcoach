## Guidance

Whether your color palette is qualitative (hues) or sequential (shades), ensure that each color step is clearly distinguishable from its neighbors. Crucially, the colors must also have different levels of brightness (luminance) so that the differences are maintained when viewed in grayscale or by someone with a color vision deficiency.

## Why

If colors are too similar, they fail at their primary job: to distinguish data. If they don't vary in brightness, their distinctions disappear for many colorblind users and when printed in black and white. A palette with good perceptual steps and brightness variation is robust, accessible, and effective.

### Core Principle

Visual encodings must be discriminable to be useful. For color, this means creating sufficient difference in both hue and brightness to ensure the encoding works for all users in all contexts.

## When it applies

- Always, when using color to encode categories or quantities. This is a foundational rule for creating effective and accessible visualizations.

## Exceptions

- There are no known exceptions. This is a fundamental requirement for reliable visual encoding with color.

## Trade-offs

- Achieving perfect perceptual steps and brightness variation can sometimes limit aesthetic choices. You may not be able to use your exact brand colors if they don't meet these criteria. Functionality and accessibility should take priority over pure aesthetics.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Grayscale Collapse:** You convert your chart to grayscale (or print it in black and white), and two different categories (e.g., a medium green and a medium red) become the exact same shade of gray, making them indistinguishable.
- **Subtle Neighbors:** Your legend or chart contains two colors that are very similar (e.g., a light teal next to a light green), and you have to look closely to tell them apart even in full color.
- **The Squint Test:** If you squint your eyes while looking at the chart, do different colored categories blend into one? If so, their brightness values are too similar.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Validate Your Palette.** Use a tool like Viz Palette or a colorblindness simulator to check your current palette. If it fails, make adjustments to the brightness of the failing colors until they are distinct.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Use a Perceptually-Uniform Palette.** Don't invent your own palettes. Use professionally designed, tested palettes from sources like ColorBrewer, or perceptually-uniform palettes like Viridis, Magma, or Plasma. These are engineered to solve this exact problem.
