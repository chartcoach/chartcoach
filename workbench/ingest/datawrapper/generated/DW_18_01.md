## Guidance

In a multi-part or multi-variable visualization, use color to encode the function or contribution of each element within the story. For example, use one color for factors that increase a value and another for factors that decrease it.

## Why

This adds a semantic layer to your color palette, moving beyond simple differentiation. It allows viewers to instantly recognize the relationship between different variables (e.g., competing forces), making the underlying story more intuitive and easier to grasp.

### Core Principle

Color should support the story. Use it to encode meaning, not just to decorate or distinguish.

## When it applies

- When visualizing multiple variables that have opposing or complementary effects on a final outcome.
- Useful for stories about financial performance (revenue vs. costs), demographic change (births vs. deaths), or environmental impact (emissions vs. mitigation).

## Exceptions

- When colors are already bound to a specific, widely-understood convention (e.g., political party colors in a given country) or strict brand guidelines that you cannot override.
- When variables do not have a clear positive or negative function in the narrative.

## Trade-offs

- This approach requires a clear, simple narrative with well-defined roles (e.g., "increase" vs. "decrease"). It may not work for more complex situations with ambiguous relationships.
- You are limited by the number of distinct functional roles you can represent clearly with color.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Rainbow of Confusion:** Multiple variables are shown with distinct but semantically meaningless colors that don't help explain their relationship.
- **Decoding Effort:** The viewer has to constantly refer to the legend and think hard to understand if a trend is "good" or "bad" in the context of the story.
- **Contradictory Colors:** Using colors with strong existing connotations (like red for "stop/danger" or green for "go/good") in a way that contradicts their narrative role.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Use a Diverging Palette.** If you have positive and negative contributors, use a standard diverging palette (e.g., blue-white-red) centered on a neutral point to separate the two groups.
- **Moderate approach: Group by Function.** Identify the narrative role of each variable (e.g., 'contributes to growth', 'reduces growth', 'outcome'). Assign a single, meaningful color to each functional group.
- **Comprehensive approach: Explain the Color Logic.** In addition to assigning functional colors, add a small legend or annotation that explicitly states what the colors mean (e.g., "Green = increases population"). Ensure the chosen colors are accessible for colorblind users.
