## Guidance

Refrain from using a palette of browns, olives, beiges, or other colors that resemble human skin tones when representing any racial or ethnic group.

## Why

Using colors that are literal or figurative representations of skin tone is reductive and can be dehumanizing. It boils complex, multifaceted identities down to a single physical attribute, which can make people feel uncomfortable and misrepresented, regardless of their own race.

### Core Principle

Represent people as people, not as their physical characteristics. Visualizations should respect the complexity of human identity.

## When it applies

- When choosing colors for charts that categorize people by race, ethnicity, or any other demographic characteristic.

## Exceptions

- An explicit artistic or narrative project about skin color itself (like Angelica Dass's "Humanae" project, which photographs people against a Pantone color matched to their skin) is a clear exception, as the color is the subject, not just a label. For general data visualization, there are no common exceptions.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Flesh-Tone Palette:** The color palette for your racial or ethnic groups consists of shades of brown, tan, beige, or pink that evoke skin.
- **Audience Discomfort:** Viewers express that the colors make them feel "weird" or "uncomfortable."

## How to Improve

- **Comprehensive approach:** Completely replace the skin-tone palette with an abstract categorical color palette. Choose hues like blues, purples, greens, and oranges that have no common association with skin color. Ensure the chosen colors are distinct and accessible.
