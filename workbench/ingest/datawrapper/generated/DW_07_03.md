## Guidance

Restrict the number of distinct colors used to encode categories, ideally to four or fewer. If you have more categories, find ways to group them or use a different chart type.

## Why

The more colors you introduce, the harder it becomes to find a set where every color is easily distinguishable from all others, especially for colorblind viewers. Limiting colors also reduces the cognitive load for all readers, as they don't have to memorize as many color-to-category associations or constantly scan back and forth to a legend.

### Core Principle

Simplify the visual task to reduce cognitive load. Every color added is another piece of information to be processed and remembered.

## When it applies

-   When visualizing categorical data with many levels (e.g., more than 5-7 categories).
-   When a chart appears cluttered or its legend is long and difficult to parse.

## Exceptions

-   When visualizing a large, fixed set of categories is the explicit goal and alternatives are not feasible (e.g., a map showing results for all 27 EU member states). Even in these cases, other techniques like interactivity should be used to aid interpretation.

## Trade-offs

-   To reduce colors, you may need to group smaller categories into an "Other" bucket, which involves a loss of detail.
-   Switching to a chart type that handles more categories better (like a bar chart or small multiples) might take up more space.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Legend Overload:** The color legend for your chart has more than five items and looks like a list.
-   **Rainbow Effect:** The chart uses a multitude of colors that look like a rainbow, making it hard to focus and distinguish individual categories.
-   **Subtle Shades:** The palette relies on multiple similar shades (e.g., teal, light blue, dark blue) to represent different categories.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick approach:** Group smaller or less important categories into a single "Other" category and color it with a neutral gray.
-   **Moderate approach:** Instead of coloring every category, highlight only the one or two most important categories with a distinct color and set all others to gray. This focuses the reader's attention on the main story.
-   **Comprehensive approach:** Reconsider your chart choice. Instead of a single, multi-colored chart (like a pie or stacked bar), use a chart type that handles many categories more gracefully, such as a simple bar chart with labels, a table, or small multiples.
