## Guidance

To represent data that has a natural order (e.g., low to high, worst to best, agree to disagree), use a **sequential** color scale. This typically involves varying the lightness of a single hue, from light to dark.

## Why

The human visual system naturally associates darkness with greater quantity or importance. A light-to-dark progression creates an intuitive and immediate visual mapping to the data's order, allowing for quick comparison and ranking without requiring the viewer to memorize a legend of arbitrary colors.

### Core Principle

Visual encodings should match the structure of the data. Ordered data deserves an ordered visual encoding, and the lightness of color is a strong ordered encoding.

## When it applies

- When visualizing ordinal data (e.g., survey responses from 'Strongly Disagree' to 'Strongly Agree').
- When visualizing binned or continuous quantitative data in choropleth maps, heatmaps, or colored bar charts.

## Exceptions

- **Diverging Data:** For data with a meaningful midpoint (e.g., values above/below a baseline, temperature change), a **diverging scale** (two hues moving from a neutral center) is more appropriate than a simple sequential scale.
- **Tracking Across Charts:** For tracking specific items across small multiples, using stable, distinct hues may be more effective than re-applying a sequential scale in each chart.

## Trade-offs

- A single-hue sequential scale can sometimes lack visual impact compared to a more colorful palette. Multi-hue sequential palettes (e.g., Viridis) can be a good compromise, but must be designed carefully to be perceptually uniform.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Rainbow Confusion:** Your chart uses a rainbow palette (red, orange, yellow, green, blue) to show magnitude. This is a classic mistake, as the perceived order of hues does not match the spectrum and the luminance values are uneven.
- **Qualitative for Quantitative:** A map of population density uses unrelated colors like pink, brown, and cyan, forcing the user to work hard to understand which areas are more or less dense.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Adopt a Single-Hue Sequence.** Change your color scheme to a simple light-to-dark progression of one color (e.g., light gray to dark gray, or light blue to dark blue).
- **Comprehensive Approach: Use a Perceptually-Uniform Palette.** Switch to a scientifically designed sequential palette like Viridis, Plasma, or the palettes from ColorBrewer. These are engineered to ensure that the perceived change in color matches the change in the data, and they perform well for colorblind users and in grayscale.
