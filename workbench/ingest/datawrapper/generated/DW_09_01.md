## Guidance

Avoid using the color white or a very light gray to represent the "White" category in a visualization. Assign it a distinct color with a visual weight comparable to other categories.

## Why

Using white can make this group appear as a neutral default or a background, rather than one group among many. This visually diminishes the presence and importance of other racial groups, unintentionally centering the "White" category as the norm against which others are measured.

### Core Principle

All categories representing groups of people should be given equal visual importance to ensure they are perceived as equally valid subjects of the data story.

## When it applies

- When visualizing data broken down by race, and one of the categories is "White."

## Exceptions

- The Datawrapper article notes a rare, high-risk exception: if the narrative focus is explicitly on how other groups' data compares to or is "catching up" with the white population, using white as a baseline *might* be defensible. However, this is a very advanced and risky choice that should be made with extreme care and clear justification.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Invisible Bar:** The bar, line, or area for the "White" category is colored white and seems to disappear into the chart's background.
- **Default vs. Deviation:** The visualization creates an impression of colored "other" groups being compared against a neutral, un-colored "default" group.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** Change the color from pure white to a light but distinct hue (e.g., a light orange, light blue, or off-white with a colored tint).
- **Comprehensive approach:** Choose a balanced, categorical palette where every color has a similar level of saturation and brightness. Assign one of these colors to the "White" category, ensuring it stands on equal footing with all others.
