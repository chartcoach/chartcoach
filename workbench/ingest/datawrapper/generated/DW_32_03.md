## Guidance

Add textual annotations directly onto the chart to point out outliers, explain trends, define key thresholds, or provide crucial context for specific data points.

## Why

Annotations transform a chart from a passive display of data into an active piece of communication. They guide the reader's attention to what's important, answer potential questions before they are asked, and provide narrative context that the raw data alone cannot. This makes the chart more insightful, engaging, and memorable.

### Core Principle

Don't just show data; tell a story. Guide your reader to the insights you want them to see.

## When it applies

- In any explanatory visualization where you want to tell a story or guide the reader to a specific conclusion.
- Especially useful for highlighting outliers, trend changes, or the impact of external events (e.g., "Recession began").

## Exceptions

- **Exploratory Tools:** In purely exploratory visualizations or dashboards where the goal is for the user to discover their own insights, excessive annotations can be leading and counterproductive.
- **Sparklines:** In dashboards with very small charts (sparklines), there is typically not enough space for annotations.

## Trade-offs

- Adding thoughtful annotations takes more time and critical thinking than just producing a default chart.
- Poorly placed or wordy annotations can create clutter and obscure the data they are meant to explain.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Silent Chart:** The chart presents data without any interpretation, leaving the viewer to wonder "So what?"
- **The Mysterious Spike:** There is a dramatic peak or dip in the data with no explanation offered.
- **Post-Chart Paragraph:** You find yourself writing a long paragraph of text below the chart to explain what it shows; this text should be on the chart as annotations.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Label the 'What'.** Add a single annotation to label the most important data point (e.g., "2022 Peak: $3.4M") or to explain an external event ("New policy introduced").
- **Moderate Approach: Explain the 'Why'.** Add 2-3 annotations to build a small narrative. Explain a key trend, highlight a significant comparison, and define a technical element like a threshold line. Use arrows or connector lines to clearly link text to data.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Guide the Reader.** Structure the entire chart around a narrative told through a series of annotations that guide the reader from one insight to the next, as seen in high-quality data journalism from outlets like The New York Times or The Pudding.
