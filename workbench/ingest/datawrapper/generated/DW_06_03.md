## Guidance

Treat your color key as a mini-infographic. Use a descriptive title, annotations, and short explanations to clarify what the colors mean, highlight important thresholds, and provide narrative context.

## Why

A simple color gradient is just a tool for decoding; an annotated key is a tool for understanding. By adding explanatory text, you can guide the reader's attention to what's important, explain the "so what" behind the data, and make the entire visualization more engaging and trustworthy. It transforms the key from a passive reference into an active part of the story.

## When it applies

- When the scale includes a critical threshold or a value of special interest (e.g., a policy target, a scientific benchmark, the average).
- When the units or the concept being measured are complex and require a brief explanation.
- When you want to add a narrative frame to the visualization.
- When you want to make the visualization more engaging and memorable.

## Exceptions

- **Standard Conventions:** For very common and widely understood color scales (like a standard weather map's temperature scale), extensive annotation may be unnecessary.
- **Exploratory Visualizations:** In an internal, exploratory context where the goal is just to see patterns, a simple key may suffice.
- **Extreme Space Constraints:** On very small charts (sparks), there may not be enough room for an annotated key.

## Trade-offs

- **Space:** Annotated keys require more space than simple ones.
- **Potential for Bias:** The annotations you choose will guide the reader's interpretation. This is a powerful tool, but it must be used ethically to avoid misrepresenting the data.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "So What?" Question:** A user understands what the colors represent but doesn't know why it matters or what to conclude.
- **Unit Confusion:** The key shows a range from 0 to 100, but the reader has no idea if the unit is "percent," "dollars," or "parts per million."
- **Invisible Threshold:** The data story hinges on which areas are above a certain value (e.g., passing a safety limit), but this value is not marked on the key, forcing the reader to guess.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add a Title and Units.** At a minimum, give your color key a descriptive title that explains what is being measured and include the units (e.g., "Population Density (people per km²)")
- **Moderate Approach: Highlight a Key Value.** Add a line, arrow, or text label to your key that points out a single, crucial value—like the national average, a target goal, or a critical threshold.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Build a Narrative Key.** Craft a key with a clear title, annotations for multiple key points, and a short sentence or two explaining the context. Think of it as a small, self-contained story that provides the setup for the main chart.
