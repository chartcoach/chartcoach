## Guidance

In a pie chart, use a single, saturated color to highlight the most important slice and use muted colors or shades of a single neutral color (like gray) for all other slices. Avoid using a "rainbow" of different, vibrant hues.

## Why

Color is a powerful pre-attentive attribute, meaning our brains process it before we even consciously pay attention. By using a unique, strong color for one slice, you instantly guide the reader's focus to the part of the data you want to emphasize. Conversely, using multiple bright colors creates visual noise, forcing all slices to compete for attention and distracting the reader from making meaningful comparisons or identifying the key message.

### Core Principle

Use color strategically to create visual hierarchy and guide attention, rather than just to differentiate categories.

## When it applies

- You are creating a pie chart (or similar part-to-whole chart).
- There is one specific category or share that is the focus of your story.
- The other categories are secondary or provide context.

## Exceptions

- When all slices are of equal importance and the goal is simply to differentiate them. Even in this case, a carefully chosen categorical palette is better than a random rainbow of colors.

## Trade-offs

- **Prioritizes:** Guiding attention and emphasizing a key finding.
- **Sacrifices:** Giving equal visual weight to all categories.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Rainbow Pie:** The pie chart uses many different, highly saturated colors, making it look like a color wheel or a Trivial Pursuit piece. It's not clear which slice, if any, is the most important.
- **Visual Competition:** All slices are colored with equal intensity, making it hard for the reader to know where to look first.

## How to Improve

- **Create a Focal Point:** First, identify the single most important slice for your message. Color this slice with a distinct, saturated color. Then, assign all other slices different shades of a single, neutral color like gray. This ensures the contextual slices are visible and distinct from each other but do not compete with the main point of interest.
