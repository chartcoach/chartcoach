## Guidance

When visualizing data from samples, such as polls or surveys, include visual representations of uncertainty like confidence intervals or margins of error.

## Why

Polls and survey results are estimates derived from a sample, not certainties from the whole population. They inherently have a margin of error. Failing to show this uncertainty can lead the audience to believe the numbers are precise and final, causing them to draw false conclusions about small differences between categories (e.g., that one party is "ahead" when the difference is within the margin of error). Visualizing uncertainty is an act of intellectual honesty.

## When it applies

- Any time you are charting statistical estimates rather than exact counts. This is especially critical for polls, survey data, or scientific measurements.

## Exceptions

- When the uncertainty is extremely small and adding error bars would just add visual clutter without changing the interpretation.
- For audiences with very low graph literacy, the extra marks might cause confusion. In these rare cases, a clear textual note ("Margin of error is ±3%") might be a better, though less effective, alternative.

## Trade-offs

- **Cognitive Load:** Adds visual complexity to the chart, which can slightly increase the effort required to read it.
- **Explanation Needed:** May require a brief explanation for a general audience unfamiliar with the concept of statistical error.

## Signs of Trouble

- **False Precision:** A chart presents poll numbers with decimal-point precision but gives no indication of the inherent error, implying a level of accuracy that doesn't exist.
- **Overstating Differences:** An article or presenter makes a big deal about one category being "ahead" of another by a tiny amount that is well within the margin of error.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** Add a note below the chart stating the margin of error for the data (e.g., "Margin of error: ±3 percentage points").
- **Moderate Approach:** Add error bars to your bar chart or dot plot, or a confidence band to your line chart, to visually represent the interval of uncertainty.
- **Comprehensive Approach:** Use a gradient or transparency for the error bars/bands to emphasize that values near the center of the interval are more probable than those at the edges. Accompany the chart with a simple, plain-language explanation of what the margin of error means.
