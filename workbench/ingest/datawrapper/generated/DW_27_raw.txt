## Guidance

When a key data point in your part-to-whole story is a simple fraction like a quarter (25%), a half (50%), or three-quarters (75%), a pie chart can be an effective choice.

## Why

Humans are particularly good at perceiving and decoding simple angles that correspond to familiar fractions. A slice that is a right angle (90°) or a straight line (180°) is instantly recognizable as 25% or 50% of the whole, respectively. This makes pie charts highly intuitive for communicating these specific proportions, often more so than a stacked bar or column chart where the reader must consult the axis.

### Core Principle

Match the visual encoding to human perceptual strengths. For simple, common fractions, the angle encoding of a pie chart leverages our intuitive understanding of quarters and halves.

## When it applies

- You are visualizing a part-to-whole relationship (composition).
- One of the key shares you want to emphasize is very close to 25%, 50%, or 75%.
- The total number of shares is small (typically five or fewer).

## Exceptions

If precise comparison between multiple, non-simple fractions is more important (e.g., comparing 47% to 53%), a bar chart is a better choice, as judging small differences in angles is difficult.

## Trade-offs

- **Prioritizes:** Quick, intuitive recognition of simple fractions.
- **Sacrifices:** Precision in comparing shares that are not simple fractions. While a pie chart makes 50% obvious, it makes distinguishing between 47% and 49% difficult.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Ambiguous Stacked Bar:** You are using a stacked bar chart to show a 25% share, but it's not immediately obvious to the viewer that the segment represents exactly one-quarter of the total without carefully reading the axis.

## How to Improve

- **Switch to a Pie Chart:** If your primary message is "about half of the total," convert the stacked bar chart into a pie chart. The half-circle shape will communicate this message more directly and intuitively than a bar filled to the 50% mark.

## Guidance

When the primary task for the reader is to accurately compare or rank the sizes of different shares of a total, choose a bar chart or column chart instead of a pie chart.

## Why

Humans are notoriously bad at accurately judging and comparing angles and areas, which are the primary visual encodings in a pie chart. This perceptual limitation makes it difficult to tell which slice is larger when values are close. In contrast, bar and column charts encode values using length along a common baseline, a task humans perform with much higher accuracy.

### Core Principle

Use visual encodings that people perceive most accurately for the intended task. For quantitative comparison, position along a common scale (length) is superior to angle and area.

## When it applies

- The main goal is for users to compare the sizes of different categories (e.g., "Which region had the highest sales?").
- You need to show the ranking of different shares.
- The differences between share values are small and require precise judgment.
- You have more than a few categories to compare.

## Exceptions

- When showing a single share relative to the whole, and that share is a simple fraction (e.g., 25%, 50%). In this case, the task is less about comparison between shares and more about seeing one part of a whole.

## Trade-offs

- **Prioritizes:** Accuracy in comparison and ranking.
- **Sacrifices:** The immediate "part-of-a-whole" visual metaphor that a pie chart provides. While a stacked bar chart can also show parts of a whole, it's a less iconic representation.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Indecipherable Slices:** Your pie chart has multiple slices that are very close in size. Viewers cannot confidently tell which is larger without reading data labels. This forces them to read and compare numbers, defeating the purpose of the chart.

## How to Improve

This allows for easy and accurate comparison of their lengths, making it immediately clear which categories are larger and by how much. Sorting the bars by value further aids comparison and ranking.

## Guidance

Avoid using a pie chart when you have more than five categories (slices). If you need to display a part-to-whole relationship with many shares, use a stacked bar or stacked column chart instead.

## Why

As the number of slices in a pie chart increases, the chart quickly becomes cluttered and difficult to interpret. Each slice becomes smaller, making their angles harder to judge and compare. Furthermore, finding space for legible labels becomes a significant challenge, often resulting in a messy web of leader lines or an unreadable legend. ## When it applies

- You are visualizing a composition (part-to-whole relationship).
- Your categorical data has high cardinality (more than 5 categories).

If you can group the smaller slices into a single "Other" category to reduce the total slice count to five or fewer, a pie chart may still be viable. This works if the individual small slices are not important to the story.

- **Sacrifices:** The ability to show every single category as a distinct slice in a circular format.

- **Labeling Nightmare:** Labels are overlapping, are too small to read, or require a complex set of lines pointing from the label to the slice.

If you are visualizing a composition with only two shares (e.g., "Yes" vs. "No"), consider forgoing a chart altogether and simply stating the key percentage directly in your text.

## Why

When you need to compare the composition of two or more different groups or time periods, use a chart type like a stacked bar chart, not multiple pie charts.

## Guidance

In a pie chart, use a single, saturated color to highlight the most important slice and use muted colors or shades of a single neutral color (like gray) for all other slices. Avoid using a "rainbow" of different, vibrant hues.

## Why

Color is a powerful pre-attentive attribute, meaning our brains process it before we even consciously pay attention. By using a unique, strong color for one slice, you instantly guide the reader's focus to the part of the data you want to emphasize. Conversely, using multiple bright colors creates visual noise, forcing all slices to compete for attention and distracting the reader from making meaningful comparisons or identifying the key message.

### Core Principle

Use color strategically to create visual hierarchy and guide attention, rather than just to differentiate categories.

## When it applies

- You are creating a pie chart (or similar part-to-whole chart).
- There is one specific category or share that is the focus of your story.
- The other categories are secondary or provide context.

## Exceptions

- When all slices are of equal importance and the goal is simply to differentiate them. Even in this case, a carefully chosen categorical palette is better than a random rainbow of colors.

## Trade-offs

- **Prioritizes:** Guiding attention and emphasizing a key finding.
- **Sacrifices:** Giving equal visual weight to all categories.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Rainbow Pie:** The pie chart uses many different, highly saturated colors, making it look like a color wheel or a Trivial Pursuit piece. It's not clear which slice, if any, is the most important.
- **Visual Competition:** All slices are colored with equal intensity, making it hard for the reader to know where to look first.

## How to Improve

- **Create a Focal Point:** First, identify the single most important slice for your message. Color this slice with a distinct, saturated color. Then, assign all other slices different shades of a single, neutral color like gray. This ensures the contextual slices are visible and distinct from each other but do not compete with the main point of interest.

If your pie chart contains multiple small, individually unimportant slices, group them into a single, larger slice labeled "Other" or "Miscellaneous".

## Why

A proliferation of small slices makes a pie chart cluttered, difficult to label, and hard to read. By consolidating these minor categories, you simplify the visualization, reducing the number of slices and colors. - The individual identity of these small categories is not critical to your story.

To create a balanced and efficient layout, place pie charts within narrow containers, such as sidebars or margin columns, rather than in full-width content areas.
