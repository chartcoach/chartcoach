## Guidance

Write a headline that summarizes the core insight or story your chart is meant to convey, rather than just describing its topic.

## Why

A good headline acts as a hypothesis, telling readers what they should pay attention to before they even look at the details. This saves them time, helps them understand the chart's purpose immediately, and makes the central message more memorable. It transforms the chart from a passive display of data into an active piece of communication that makes a clear point.

### Core Principle

Frame the insight for your audience. Don't just show data; tell the story the data reveals.

## When it applies

-   This applies to virtually all charts intended for communication, especially when a chart is part of a larger article, report, or presentation and needs to convey a specific point quickly.

## Exceptions

-   In a purely exploratory context where the goal is for the user to discover their own insights (like in a dashboard for analysts), a more neutral, descriptive title might be more appropriate.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The "So What?" Test:** If someone can look at your chart's title and ask "So what?", it's not descriptive enough.
-   **Topic Title:** The title is just a noun phrase naming the variables (e.g., "Sales by Year," "Population of Cities").
-   **No Conclusion:** The title describes the chart's components but doesn't state a finding.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix:** Look at your chart and ask, "What is the single most important thing I want someone to learn from this?" Turn the answer to that question into a full sentence and use it as your headline.
-   **Moderate Redesign:** After writing a descriptive headline, review the chart's design (colors, annotations) to ensure all visual elements actively support and prove the headline's claim. If your headline is "Product A is outperforming all others," make sure Product A is visually highlighted.

## Guidance

To make a data point or trend meaningful, include other relevant data series that provide context and a basis for comparison.

## Why

A single number or data series is rarely meaningful on its own. Showing a value in isolation leaves the reader asking, "Is that high or low? Good or bad?" Comparing your primary data to other categories (e.g., other products), time periods (e.g., the previous year), or benchmarks (e.g., a competitor, an industry average) makes it relatable and helps the reader understand its significance. Context is what turns data into insight.

### Core Principle

Humans understand value through comparison. To show that something is big, small, high, or low, you must show it relative to something else.

## When it applies

-   When you are trying to make a point about the magnitude, rank, or trend of a particular data series.
-   When presenting a key performance indicator (KPI) that needs to be evaluated against a target or a historical precedent.

## Exceptions

-   If the chart's purpose is a simple lookup of a single value and no interpretation is needed (e.g., a price list). However, this is rare for data visualizations.

## Trade-offs

-   Adding more data can increase chart complexity. This is why it's important to use other techniques (like color) to visually distinguish between the primary data and the contextual data.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The Lonely Line:** The chart contains only one data series, with no benchmarks, targets, or other series to compare it against.
-   **Unanswered Questions:** The chart makes the viewer wonder, "Is that number good?" or "How does that compare to last year?"

## How to Improve

-   **Moderate Approach:** Identify what comparison would make your point stronger. Is it against a competitor? A previous product? A national average? A company target? Find that data and add it to your chart as one or more new series. In the source article's example, sales of other Apple products (iPod, iPad) were added to contextualize iPhone sales.

  - type: good
    description: "The same chart, but the iPhone line is a prominent red, while the iPad and iPod lines are both a neutral grey. The viewer's eye is immediately drawn to the iPhone line, which is the focus of the story."
---

## Guidance

Use a single, saturated color to draw attention to the most important data in your chart. Render all other contextual or comparison data in a neutral color like grey.

## Why

Color is a powerful pre-attentive attribute that guides a viewer's eye before they even consciously think about it. By highlighting the data that supports your main point and pushing everything else into the background, you create a strong visual hierarchy. This makes the chart easier and faster to read, reduces cognitive load, and ensures the viewer focuses on the intended message.

### Core Principle

Use visual salience to match thematic importance. The most important information should be the most visually prominent.

## When it applies

-   When your chart contains multiple data series but you want to tell a story about one of them in particular.
-   When you have added contextual data (see 'provide-comparison-context') and need to visually separate it from the primary data.

## Exceptions

-   When all data series are of equal importance and the task is pure exploration rather than communication of a specific insight.
-   In a categorical comparison where the task is to distinguish between all categories equally.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The Rainbow Effect:** Every bar or line has a different, bright color, creating visual noise and suggesting all are equally important.
-   **Lost in the Crowd:** The most important data series uses a color that is no more prominent than any other, forcing the reader to search for the story.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix:** In your chart tool, change the color of all contextual lines or bars to a light or medium grey. Change the color of your main data series to a more saturated, distinct color.
-   **Moderate Approach:** Ensure your chosen highlight color is not only distinct from the grey but also accessible for users with color vision deficiencies. It should have sufficient contrast and not rely on a red/green distinction alone.

## Guidance

Add text labels and visual highlights (like arrows or ranges) directly onto the chart to explain important points, outliers, or external events that influenced the data.

## Why

Charts show *what* happened, but they often can't explain *why*. Annotations provide crucial context, answer anticipated reader questions, and weave a narrative into the visualization. They connect the abstract points and lines on the chart to tangible, real-world events, making the information more insightful, credible, and memorable.

## When it applies

-   When a specific point on the chart (a peak, a dip, a crossover point) has a known cause that is relevant to the story.
-   When you want to draw attention to a specific area of the chart that supports your main message.
-   When showing a trend over time where external events (e.g., policy changes, product launches, economic shocks) have influenced the data.

## Trade-offs

-   Too many annotations can clutter the chart. Focus on the 1-3 most important points that are essential to understanding the story.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Mysterious Spikes:** The chart has dramatic movements in the data with no explanation.
-   **Required Body Text:** The viewer must read a separate paragraph of text to understand a key feature of the chart that could have been labeled directly.

## How to Improve

-   **Moderate Approach:** Identify the most important moment in your chart that needs explanation. Use your tool to add a text box with a line or arrow pointing to that data point. In a short sentence, explain what happened at that time or why that point is significant.
-   **Comprehensive Approach:** Consider combining annotations with other visual cues. For example, the source article uses a highlighted range in addition to a text annotation to emphasize the period when the iPhone overtook the iPod.

  - type: good
    description: "The chart includes a clear, concise subtitle: 'Worldwide sales of selected Apple products in million, by fiscal quarter, 2000 to 2014.' This single sentence answers multiple potential questions and ensures accurate interpretation."
---

## Guidance

Include a descriptive subtitle or text block that clearly explains what the data shows, including units, geographic scope, and any important definitions or limitations.

## Why

Your readers have not been part of your data exploration process and lack your intimate knowledge of the dataset. A precise description or subtitle orients them, preventing misinterpretation. It clarifies what is being measured (e.g., sales in millions), the scope (e.g., worldwide), and any caveats (e.g., "selected products," "fiscal quarter"), which builds trust and ensures accurate understanding. This text is also critical for accessibility, as it provides context for screen reader users.

## When it applies

-   Always. Every chart needs to explain what its axes and visual marks represent in unambiguous terms.

## Trade-offs

-   Adding text can increase the visual density of the chart. However, the gain in clarity and accuracy almost always outweighs the cost of a slightly busier visual.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Ambiguous Axes:** Axis labels are single words like "Sales" or "Amount" without units ($, £, millions, etc.).
-   **Unclear Scope:** The reader cannot tell if the data is local, national, or global, or over what precise time period it was collected.
-   **Assumed Knowledge:** The chart relies on the reader knowing that "Q1" means "fiscal quarter" or that the data only includes certain products.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix:** Below your headline, add a sentence that concisely answers the key "W" questions: **What** is being measured? What are the **units**? **Where** is the scope (geography)? **When** is the scope (timeframe)? **Which** items are included (if it's a selection)?

## Guidance

Whenever feasible, place labels directly next to the lines, bars, or areas they describe, instead of using a separate color legend.

## Why

Forcing a reader to look back and forth between a legend and the data itself increases cognitive load. This visual search task, however brief, slows down interpretation and consumes mental energy that could be spent understanding the data's message. Direct labels make the chart self-contained and immediately decodable.

### Core Principle

Minimize the cognitive work required to decode the visualization by co-locating labels with the data they describe.

## When it applies

-   For charts with a small number of categories or series (typically fewer than 5-6), especially line charts, pie charts, and some bar charts.

## Exceptions

-   May not be feasible in very dense charts with many overlapping series (e.g., a "spaghetti" line chart). In such cases, a separate legend, ideally an interactive one that highlights a series on hover, might be a better choice to avoid clutter.
-   When labels are too long to fit neatly within the chart area.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The Back-and-Forth:** You find yourself (or watch others) repeatedly glancing between the legend and the main chart area.
-   **Color Matching:** The primary task becomes "match the blue in the legend to the blue line on the chart."

## How to Improve

-   **Moderate Approach:** Remove the chart's default legend. Add text labels at the end of each line, or inside/next to each major bar or pie slice. If possible, match the label color to the data series color for an even stronger visual connection. Many modern charting tools have a "direct labeling" option.

## Guidance

Always include a clearly visible source for your data, typically in the footer of the visualization.

## Why

Citing your source is crucial for establishing credibility and trust with your audience. It demonstrates transparency about where your information comes from and allows interested readers to verify your data or explore it further for their own purposes. It is a fundamental and non-negotiable practice of ethical data communication. A source acts as proof and shows respect for the work of the original data collectors.

## When it applies

-   Always. Every chart based on data not originally generated by the author for that specific purpose should cite its source.

## Exceptions

-   None for public-facing or professional work. The only plausible exception is for purely internal, informal sketches where the source is implicitly and universally understood by the immediate audience (e.g., a quick chart of team data shared within that team).

## Signs of Trouble

-   **"Where did this come from?":** The first question a skeptical viewer asks is about the data's origin.
-   **Missing Footer:** The chart has a title, axes, and a main body, but no information in the lower-left or lower-right corner.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix:** Add a line of text at the bottom of your chart that says "Source:" followed by the name of the organization, person, or publication that provided the data (e.g., "Source: U.S. Census Bureau, 2020 American Community Survey"). If possible, make the source name a hyperlink to the original data table or report.
