## Guidance

Before publishing, show your visualization to a few people who represent your intended audience. Ask them what the chart is telling them to identify confusing elements that you, the creator, have become blind to.

## Why

As the creator, you are too close to the project and the data; this is often called the "curse of knowledge." You already know what the chart is supposed to show. Fresh eyes, especially from your target audience, are the best way to discover if your message is unclear, your visual encoding is confusing, or if your chart can be misinterpreted.

### Core Principle

You are not your user. The only way to know if a communication is effective is to test it with the intended receivers.

## When it applies

This is a universal principle for any visualization intended for an audience other than yourself. It's especially critical when using complex data, abstract chart types, or when communicating with a non-expert audience.

## Exceptions

This is not necessary for purely personal data exploration or analysis where you are the only audience.

## Trade-offs

Testing takes time and effort, which may be in short supply on a deadline. However, the time saved by avoiding confusion and corrections after publication—and the benefit of communicating more effectively—often outweighs the initial investment.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Creator Bias:** You are the only person who has seen the chart before it goes live.
-   **Long Explanations:** You find yourself writing a very long caption or feeling the need to verbally explain the chart to everyone who sees it.
-   **Post-Publication Confusion:** After publishing, you receive questions from readers who misunderstood the chart's main point, or your work is cited incorrectly.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix ("The Coworker Test"):** Show your chart to a colleague from a different team. Ask them one question: "What is the main story this chart is telling you?" Do not lead them or explain it first.
-   **Moderate Approach ("The 3-User Test"):** Find 2-3 people who fit your audience profile. Show them the chart and ask them to "think aloud" as they look at it. Note where they hesitate, what questions they ask, and what conclusions they draw.
-   **Comprehensive Approach (Formal Usability Testing):** Conduct structured testing sessions with a script and specific tasks (e.g., "Using this chart, can you tell me which country had the biggest drop in 2020?"). This provides more rigorous, structured feedback.
