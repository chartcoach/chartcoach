## Guidance

To represent nominal (unordered) categories, use a palette of different hues (e.g., blue, orange, green). Do not use shades of a single hue (e.g., a sequential gradient from light blue to dark blue).

## Why

A single-hue gradient (a sequential palette) implies order, rank, and magnitude through its change in lightness. Using it for categories that have no inherent order (like countries, products, or departments) is misleading, as it will make viewers incorrectly assume that the "darker" categories are more important or have a higher value. Different hues are perceived as qualitatively different, which correctly matches the nature of categorical data.

### Core Principle

The visual encoding should match the structure of the data. Use a categorical (qualitative) color scheme for categorical data, and an ordered (sequential/diverging) scheme for quantitative/ordered data.

## When it applies

- When assigning colors to categories in a pie chart, donut chart, stacked bar chart, line chart, or scatter plot.
- Any time the data being colored is nominal/categorical.

## Exceptions

- If you are intentionally highlighting one category, you can color it with a distinct hue and use a greyscale gradient for all other (de-emphasized) categories.
- If categories are ordered (ordinal data, e.g., 'low', 'medium', 'high'), then a sequential scheme is appropriate.

## Trade-offs

- A multi-hue palette can look "too colorful" or less aesthetically cohesive than a monochrome palette. However, clarity and honesty should almost always take precedence over this type of aesthetic preference.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Implied Ranking:** A bar chart of "Sales by Department" uses shades of blue, leading viewers to wonder why the "dark blue" department is ranked higher than the "light blue" one, even when the bars are sorted alphabetically.
- **Identity Confusion:** It's much harder to talk about "the third-darkest-blue line" than "the orange line." Distinct hues give categories a clearer visual identity.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Switch to a Qualitative Palette.** In your tool, change the color palette setting from "Sequential" to "Qualitative" or "Categorical." Tools like ColorBrewer provide excellent pre-built options.
- **Moderate Redesign: Re-evaluate the Chart.** If the chart still feels too colorful with a proper categorical palette, it might be a sign that you have too many categories and should consider grouping them or switching to a different chart type (see `limit-categorical-colors`).
