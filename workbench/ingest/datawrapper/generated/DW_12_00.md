## Guidance

Use a line chart to visualize how quantitative values change over a continuous period, such as poll numbers leading up to an election.

## Why

Line charts connect individual data points, making it easy for the brain to perceive trends, patterns, rates of change, and volatility. By conventionally plotting time on the horizontal axis, they provide an intuitive representation of change that is easy to interpret.

### Core Principle
Make the most important comparisons the easiest to see. For trends, the most important comparison is a value's position over time, which a line chart makes explicit.

## When it applies

- When you have a series of data points for one or more categories measured at regular or irregular intervals over time (i.e., time-series data).
- When the primary task is to understand trends, see patterns of change, or compare the evolution of multiple series.

## Exceptions

- If you only have a few discrete time points (e.g., two or three years), a bar chart or dot plot might be clearer and less presumptive about the trend between points.
- If you want to emphasize the individual values at each time point rather than the continuous trend, a dot plot could be a better choice.

## Trade-offs

- Can become cluttered and unreadable if too many lines are shown, a situation often called a "spaghetti chart."
- The perceived steepness of a trend can be dramatically influenced by the chart's aspect ratio and the scale of the Y-axis.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Spaghetti Chart:** The chart has so many overlapping and crossing lines that it is impossible to follow any single one.
- **Disconnected Dots:** The data is a time series, but it's shown as a scatter plot, forcing the reader to mentally connect the dots to see the trend.
- **Non-Sequential Axis:** The time axis is not sorted chronologically, making the line's path nonsensical.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** Use distinct, perceptually separated colors for each line and provide a clear, easy-to-read legend.
- **Moderate Approach:** Directly label the lines at the end or at key points. This reduces the cognitive load of looking back and forth between the lines and a legend. You can also highlight the most important line(s) by making them thicker or brighter than the others.
- **Comprehensive Redesign:** If you have many lines, consider breaking the chart into small multiples. This gives each line its own chart while maintaining a common scale, making comparisons possible without the clutter.
