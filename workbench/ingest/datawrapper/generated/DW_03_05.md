## Guidance

Prioritize clarity and ease of interpretation over visual novelty. Use basic, familiar chart types (e.g., bar, line, column) as your default, and deploy more "exciting" or unusual chart types (e.g., stream graph, Marimekko chart) only when they are the uniquely best fit for your data and goals.

## Why

The primary goal of a chart is to communicate data effectively. Familiar chart types have a low cognitive barrier to entry; your audience can immediately focus on understanding the **data's message** instead of first having to learn **how to read the chart**. Novel or "fancy" charts can be engaging, but they require the viewer to learn a new visual grammar on the spot. This extra cognitive load can distract from the message, or worse, lead to misinterpretation.

### Core Principle

Communication efficiency trumps visual novelty. Minimize the cognitive work required to decode your visualization.

## When it applies

- When communicating to a general or non-expert audience.
- When the clarity and accuracy of the message are the top priorities.
- In business or analytical contexts where efficient decision-making is key.

## Exceptions

- When a novel chart type (e.g., a Sankey diagram) is uniquely suited to show a specific structure in the data (e.g., flow) that would be difficult to show otherwise.
- When the primary goal is to grab attention and create an aesthetic experience, and precise data reading is a secondary goal.
- When presenting to an expert audience that is known to be familiar with advanced chart types.

## Trade-offs

- **Sticking to basic charts:** Maximizes clarity and speed of interpretation but might be perceived as less "creative" or "boring."
- **Using a novel chart:** Might increase initial engagement and memorability but risks confusing the audience, slowing comprehension, and obscuring the data's message.

## Signs of Trouble

- **"Looks Cool":** Your primary reason for choosing a chart type is that it "looks cool" or "is different."
- **The Chart "Tutorial":** You have to spend a significant amount of time explaining to your audience how the chart works before you can discuss its insights.
- **The Blank Stare:** You present the chart and your audience looks confused rather than enlightened.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add More Explanation.** If you must use a novel chart, add a clear title, subtitle, and annotations that explicitly state the main insight and briefly explain how to read the chart. Don't make the reader guess.
- **Moderate Approach: Pair It with a Simple Chart.** Show the simple, "boring" chart first to establish the key message. Then, introduce the more "exciting" chart as an alternative view that adds a different dimension.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Re-evaluate Your Goal.** Ask yourself: "What is the single most important message, and what is the absolute clearest, simplest way to show it?" Start with a bar or line chart. Only move to a more complex type if you can clearly articulate why it's a fundamentally better choice for showing the specific pattern in your data.
