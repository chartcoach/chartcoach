## Guidance

Ensure that your chosen colors can be differentiated by people with common forms of color vision deficiency (CVD). Rely on variations in lightness, not just hue, and use simulation tools to check your palettes.

## Why

A significant portion of the population (up to 8% of men and 0.5% of women) has some form of CVD. If your palette relies on hue distinctions that are invisible to them (like red vs. green), your chart will be unreadable or misleading for this large audience. Designing for accessibility is crucial for effective and inclusive communication.

### Core Principle

Design for all users, including those with disabilities. Information must be robustly encoded so it does not fail when a single visual channel (like hue perception) is compromised.

## When it applies

- Always. This is a universal best practice for any visualization that uses color to encode information.

## Exceptions

- None. There is no good reason to knowingly create a visualization that is inaccessible to a large part of your potential audience.

## Trade-offs

- Designing CVD-safe palettes slightly constrains your aesthetic choices, as you must avoid problematic color combinations (like red/green or blue/purple of similar lightness). This is a minor cost for a major gain in accessibility and clarity.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Red/Green Combo:** The palette relies heavily on distinguishing between red and green, the most common problem for CVD.
- **Simulator Failure:** When viewed through a color blindness simulator, two or more distinct categories or gradient steps merge into the same or very similar colors.
- **Hue-Only Palettes:** The chosen colors have very different hues but almost identical lightness levels, making them indistinguishable without full-color vision.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Use a Safe Palette.** Start with a color palette that is explicitly designed to be colorblind-safe. Trusted sources include ColorBrewer (filter for "colorblind safe"), the Okabe-Ito palette, or palettes like Viridis.
- **Moderate Redesign: Test and Adjust Your Palette.** Use an online tool (like Viz Palette or Coblis) to upload your chart and simulate how it looks to users with different forms of CVD. If two colors blend together, adjust one of them, usually by making it significantly lighter or darker.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Use Redundant Encodings.** Do not rely on color alone to convey information. This is a core principle of WCAG. Add other visual cues to distinguish elements, such as different shapes for points in a scatter plot, different dashing patterns for lines in a line chart, or direct text labels. This makes your chart more robust for all users.
