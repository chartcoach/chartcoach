## Guidance

When your chart displays very large or abstract quantities, provide a familiar comparison to make the scale more intuitive and meaningful for the reader.

## Why

Humans are not wired to intuitively grasp the scale of very large numbers (e.g., millions, billions, trillions) or abstract units (e.g., terawatt-hours, square kilometers). Anchoring these numbers to a concrete, familiar analogy (e.g., "the size of Texas," "enough to fill 10,000 Olympic swimming pools," "the cost of two aircraft carriers") translates the data into human terms, making it more understandable, memorable, and impactful.

### Core Principle

Translate data into human experience. Connect abstract quantities to the concrete world your reader understands.

## When it applies

- When your chart displays quantities that are outside the scale of everyday human experience, such as national budgets, geographic areas, astronomical distances, or environmental data.
- When presenting to a general audience that lacks a specialist's frame of reference for the units being used.

## Exceptions

- When presenting to an expert audience that is already familiar with the scale and units of measurement (e.g., climatologists, economists). For this audience, such analogies might seem overly simplistic or patronizing.
- If a relevant and accurate analogy cannot be found. A poor or misleading analogy is worse than no analogy at all.

## Trade-offs

- **Finding the right analogy:** It can be difficult to find a comparison that is both widely understood and accurate.
- **Risk of misrepresentation:** If the analogy is not chosen carefully, it can oversimplify or misrepresent the data.

## Signs of Trouble

- **"Is that a lot?":** The chart presents a large number, but the reader has no frame of reference to judge its magnitude or significance.
- **Numb Numbers:** The quantities are so large (e.g., "trillions of dollars") that they become meaningless and fail to have an emotional or cognitive impact.
- **Abstract Units:** The chart uses units that are technically correct but not meaningful to a general audience (e.g., "gigatons of CO2").

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add a note.** Include a simple comparison in the chart's subtitle or an annotation. E.g., "Total spending reached $1.2 trillion, roughly the entire GDP of Australia."

- **Moderate Fix: Label the axis.** Integrate the comparison directly into the chart by adding a labeled reference line or a note on an axis, as seen in the example chart which labels points on the y-axis with "Area of Canada."

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Change the unit.** Re-frame the entire chart's unit of measurement into something more relatable. For example, instead of showing a total budget in dollars, show it as "dollars per taxpayer." Instead of total water usage in gallons, show it as "number of households supplied for a year."
