## Guidance

Add text directly onto your chart to explain key findings, highlight important data points, and tell the story behind the data. Don't rely on the reader to deduce the main message on their own.

## Why

Readers are often short on time and may not invest the effort to explore a visualization in depth. By explicitly stating the key insights through annotations, you ensure your main message is communicated efficiently and accurately, preventing misinterpretation and guiding the reader's focus to what matters most.

### Core Principle

Guide the reader. Your role as a creator is not just to present data, but to present an insight. Annotations are the most direct way to bridge the gap between data and understanding.

## When it applies

- When you have a clear, specific message or takeaway that you want every reader to understand.
- When presenting a chart to a general audience that may lack the time or expertise for deep analysis.
- In static formats like articles or reports, where interactivity is not available to aid exploration.

## Exceptions

- For purely exploratory visualizations where the primary goal is to allow the audience to freely discover their own insights without guidance. The source article notes this can lead to a "lost learning effect," as we remember things better when we discover them ourselves.

## Trade-offs

- **Clutter vs. Clarity:** Too many annotations can make a chart look busy and cluttered. You must be selective about what to highlight.
- **Discovery vs. Direction:** Annotations reduce the "aha" moment of self-discovery for the reader. This trade-off prioritizes clear communication over unguided exploration.
- **Responsiveness:** Rich annotations can be challenging to implement in responsive designs, as they may overlap or become illegible on smaller screens.

## Signs of Trouble

- **"So what?":** Viewers look at the chart and are unclear about its purpose or what they are supposed to take away from it.
- **Buried Lead:** The main finding is described in a separate paragraph of text, forcing the reader to connect the text to the visual, a step many will skip.
- **Missed Points:** Readers focus on trivial or secondary patterns in the data and miss the primary insight you intended to convey.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Write a better title.** Craft a descriptive title or subtitle that explicitly states the main finding. For example, instead of "Arctic Ice Cover," use "Arctic Ice Cover Shrinks to Yearly Minimum in September."

- **Moderate Fix: Add callouts.** Use text labels with lines or arrows to point directly to key events, peaks, troughs, or anomalies in the data. Explain what these points signify (e.g., "Annual maximum reached").

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Create a narrative path.** Weave annotations together to tell a story. Use placement and typography to guide the reader's eye from one insight to the next, explaining the "what" and the "why" behind the data's patterns.
