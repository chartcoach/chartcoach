## Guidance

To make a data point or trend meaningful, include other relevant data series that provide context and a basis for comparison.

## Why

A single number or data series is rarely meaningful on its own. Showing a value in isolation leaves the reader asking, "Is that high or low? Good or bad?" Comparing your primary data to other categories (e.g., other products), time periods (e.g., the previous year), or benchmarks (e.g., a competitor, an industry average) makes it relatable and helps the reader understand its significance. Context is what turns data into insight.

### Core Principle

Humans understand value through comparison. To show that something is big, small, high, or low, you must show it relative to something else.

## When it applies

-   When you are trying to make a point about the magnitude, rank, or trend of a particular data series.
-   When presenting a key performance indicator (KPI) that needs to be evaluated against a target or a historical precedent.

## Exceptions

-   If the chart's purpose is a simple lookup of a single value and no interpretation is needed (e.g., a price list). However, this is rare for data visualizations.

## Trade-offs

-   Adding more data can increase chart complexity. This is why it's important to use other techniques (like color) to visually distinguish between the primary data and the contextual data.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The Lonely Line:** The chart contains only one data series, with no benchmarks, targets, or other series to compare it against.
-   **Unanswered Questions:** The chart makes the viewer wonder, "Is that number good?" or "How does that compare to last year?"

## How to Improve

-   **Moderate Approach:** Identify what comparison would make your point stronger. Is it against a competitor? A previous product? A national average? A company target? Find that data and add it to your chart as one or more new series. In the source article's example, sales of other Apple products (iPod, iPad) were added to contextualize iPhone sales.
