## Guidance

Add text annotations to your chart to highlight key points and explain them in the context of real-world events.

## Why

Data doesn't exist in a vacuum. Annotations provide crucial context, bridging the gap between *what* the data shows and *why* it might show it. They guide the reader's attention to important features (like peaks, troughs, or inflection points) and offer explanations, transforming the chart from a passive display of numbers into an active, explanatory story.

### Core Principle

Don't just show data; explain it. Guide your audience to the key insights and provide the context needed for understanding.

## When it applies

- On any chart where external events can influence the data, especially time-series charts (line, area) and scatter plots.
- When you want to draw attention to specific outliers, clusters, or points of interest and explain their significance.

## Exceptions

- In purely exploratory visualizations where the goal is for the user to discover their own patterns without being led.
- If the chart is already extremely dense, adding annotations might create too much visual clutter. In this case, the explanation may need to live in the surrounding text.

## Trade-offs

- Can add clutter if overused or poorly placed.
- Requires editorial judgment to decide which events are significant enough to annotate, introducing a potential for author bias.
- Well-placed annotations can take time to design and compose.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "So What?" Chart:** A chart shows a dramatic change, a sharp spike, or a clear outlier, but provides no explanation, leaving the viewer wondering, "So what happened there?"
- **Reader Guesswork:** Viewers are left to speculate about the causes of trends or outliers on their own, potentially arriving at incorrect conclusions. The chart raises more questions than it answers.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Use an Explanatory Title.** Craft a title or subtitle that explains the main event or takeaway shown in the chart (e.g., "Support for Party X surged after the leader's debate").
- **Moderate Redesign: Add Direct Annotations.** Add one or two annotations with lines pointing to the most significant features of the chart. Briefly explain their context (e.g., "New policy announced," "Competitor enters market").
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Create a Narrative Chart.** Build a richly annotated chart that walks the reader through the data's story. Use a combination of pointers, text labels, and shaded time periods to explain multiple key events and their apparent impact on the data, creating a guided analytical experience.
