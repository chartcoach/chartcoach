## Guidance

When a key data point in your part-to-whole story is a simple fraction like a quarter (25%), a half (50%), or three-quarters (75%), a pie chart can be an effective choice.

## Why

Humans are particularly good at perceiving and decoding simple angles that correspond to familiar fractions. A slice that is a right angle (90°) or a straight line (180°) is instantly recognizable as 25% or 50% of the whole, respectively. This makes pie charts highly intuitive for communicating these specific proportions, often more so than a stacked bar or column chart where the reader must consult the axis.

### Core Principle

Match the visual encoding to human perceptual strengths. For simple, common fractions, the angle encoding of a pie chart leverages our intuitive understanding of quarters and halves.

## When it applies

- You are visualizing a part-to-whole relationship (composition).
- One of the key shares you want to emphasize is very close to 25%, 50%, or 75%.
- The total number of shares is small (typically five or fewer).

## Exceptions

If precise comparison between multiple, non-simple fractions is more important (e.g., comparing 47% to 53%), a bar chart is a better choice, as judging small differences in angles is difficult.

## Trade-offs

- **Prioritizes:** Quick, intuitive recognition of simple fractions.
- **Sacrifices:** Precision in comparing shares that are not simple fractions. While a pie chart makes 50% obvious, it makes distinguishing between 47% and 49% difficult.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Ambiguous Stacked Bar:** You are using a stacked bar chart to show a 25% share, but it's not immediately obvious to the viewer that the segment represents exactly one-quarter of the total without carefully reading the axis.

## How to Improve

- **Switch to a Pie Chart:** If your primary message is "about half of the total," convert the stacked bar chart into a pie chart. The half-circle shape will communicate this message more directly and intuitively than a bar filled to the 50% mark.
