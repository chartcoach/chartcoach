## Guidance

Do not rely on color alone to convey information. Use a second, redundant visual variable—such as shape, pattern, line style, or symbols—to reinforce the distinction between categories.

## Why

If a viewer cannot distinguish between the colors you've chosen, a second visual cue ensures they can still interpret the data correctly. This makes your visualization robust not only for colorblind users, but also for situations like black-and-white printing or low-contrast screens. It provides two independent channels for the brain to decode the information.

### Core Principle

Encode important information redundantly. Never rely on a single visual channel (especially color) for critical distinctions.

## When it applies

-   In any chart where different categories are represented by color.
-   **Scatterplots:** Use different shapes (circle, square, triangle) for different categories.
-   **Line charts:** Use different line styles (solid, dashed, dotted) or widths.
-   **Area charts/Maps:** Use different patterns or textures (hatching, stippling).
-   **Tables:** Use symbols or icons (✔️, ▲) in addition to background color.

## Exceptions

-   In charts with a very large number of categories, it can become impractical to find enough distinct shapes or patterns, leading to clutter. In such cases, interactive filtering or small multiples may be better solutions.

## Trade-offs

-   Adding shapes or patterns can increase visual clutter if not done carefully. The article cautions against a "confetti" look in scatterplots and recommends limiting shapes to three or four.
-   Patterns can sometimes create distracting moiré effects or alter the perceived lightness of the color they fill.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Color-Only:** The only difference between two important data series is their color.
-   **Print Fail:** When you print the chart in black and white, it becomes unreadable.
-   **Ambiguous Overlap:** In a line chart, two similarly-colored lines cross, and it's impossible to tell which is which on the other side.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick approach:** In a line chart, change one of the lines from solid to dashed. In a table, add an icon next to the text.
-   **Moderate approach:** In a scatterplot with 3 categories, assign circle, triangle, and square shapes in addition to color. In a map, add a simple diagonal line pattern to one of two adjacent, similar-colored regions.
-   **Comprehensive approach:** Systematically review all chart types your organization produces and establish a standard of using redundant encoding for all categorical data.
