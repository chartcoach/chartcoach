## Guidance

To facilitate comparisons of part-to-whole data across multiple categories, use bar charts, dot plots, or tables with embedded bars instead of a series of pie or donut charts.

## Why

Humans perceive and compare lengths along a common baseline (as in a bar chart) far more accurately and quickly than they compare angles, arcs, or areas (as in a pie/donut chart). A series of pies requires viewers to hold the shape of one slice in memory while comparing it to another, which is a cognitively difficult and error-prone task. Arranging data in bar charts makes the most important comparisons visually easy.

### Core Principle

Humans judge quantities more accurately by comparing positions along a common scale than by comparing angles, areas, or colors. Align your chart type with the most effective perceptual task for your goal.

## When it applies

-   When you need to show and compare part-to-whole relationships for multiple independent groups (e.g., comparing the breakdown of a budget across several departments, or market share across different quarters).
-   When ranking the groups based on one of the component parts is an important task for the user.

## Exceptions

-   If you have only a **single** category to display and the goal is to emphasize a simple part-to-whole relationship with very few, distinct slices (e.g., 75% vs. 25%), a single pie chart can be effective. However, it remains a poor choice if that chart needs to be compared to another one.

## Trade-offs

-   Bar charts may take up more vertical or horizontal space than a grid of compact donut charts.
-   Some audiences may have a strong (though often misguided) preference for the familiar look of pie charts.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **"They all look the same":** Despite different underlying numbers, all the pie or donut charts in your series look very similar, masking important variations.
-   **Label-reading required:** The only way to compare the charts is to ignore the visualization and read the numerical labels on each slice.
-   **Ranking is impossible:** It's difficult for a viewer to confidently rank the categories (e.g., "Which state has the most 'poor' bridges?").

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Add Prominent Labels.** If you must keep the pie charts, add direct, clearly legible percentage labels to each slice. This provides a numerical "escape hatch" but doesn't fix the underlying perceptual flaw of the chart type.

-   **Moderate Redesign: Use Stacked Bars.** "Unroll" each pie chart into a 100% stacked bar chart. This aligns all the compositions into a rectangular format which can be easier to scan, but comparing segments that don't start at the baseline is still difficult.

-   **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Grouped or Tabled Bars.** Convert the data into a series of grouped bar charts or, as the source article demonstrates, a table where each component part gets its own column with an embedded bar chart. This places all corresponding parts (e.g., all 'Poor' condition bars) on the same baseline, enabling highly accurate comparisons.
