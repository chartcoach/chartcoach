## Guidance

To facilitate comparisons of part-to-whole data across multiple categories, use bar charts, dot plots, or tables with embedded bars instead of a series of pie or donut charts.

## Why

Humans perceive and compare lengths along a common baseline (as in a bar chart) far more accurately and quickly than they compare angles, arcs, or areas (as in a pie/donut chart). A series of pies requires viewers to hold the shape of one slice in memory while comparing it to another, which is a cognitively difficult and error-prone task. Arranging data in bar charts makes the most important comparisons visually easy.

### Core Principle

Humans judge quantities more accurately by comparing positions along a common scale than by comparing angles, areas, or colors. Align your chart type with the most effective perceptual task for your goal.

## When it applies

-   When you need to show and compare part-to-whole relationships for multiple independent groups (e.g., comparing the breakdown of a budget across several departments, or market share across different quarters).
-   When ranking the groups based on one of the component parts is an important task for the user.

## Exceptions

-   If you have only a **single** category to display and the goal is to emphasize a simple part-to-whole relationship with very few, distinct slices (e.g., 75% vs. 25%), a single pie chart can be effective. However, it remains a poor choice if that chart needs to be compared to another one.

## Trade-offs

-   Bar charts may take up more vertical or horizontal space than a grid of compact donut charts.
-   Some audiences may have a strong (though often misguided) preference for the familiar look of pie charts.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **"They all look the same":** Despite different underlying numbers, all the pie or donut charts in your series look very similar, masking important variations.
-   **Label-reading required:** The only way to compare the charts is to ignore the visualization and read the numerical labels on each slice.
-   **Ranking is impossible:** It's difficult for a viewer to confidently rank the categories (e.g., "Which state has the most 'poor' bridges?").

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Add Prominent Labels.** If you must keep the pie charts, add direct, clearly legible percentage labels to each slice. This provides a numerical "escape hatch" but doesn't fix the underlying perceptual flaw of the chart type.

-   **Moderate Redesign: Use Stacked Bars.** "Unroll" each pie chart into a 100% stacked bar chart. This aligns all the compositions into a rectangular format which can be easier to scan, but comparing segments that don't start at the baseline is still difficult.

-   **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Grouped or Tabled Bars.** Convert the data into a series of grouped bar charts or, as the source article demonstrates, a table where each component part gets its own column with an embedded bar chart. This places all corresponding parts (e.g., all 'Poor' condition bars) on the same baseline, enabling highly accurate comparisons.

## Guidance

When a dataset has many categories (e.g., 50 states, 100+ products) and no single, obvious "big picture" story, present it in an interactive table that allows users to search, filter, and sort the data.

## Why

Instead of forcing a single, author-chosen narrative on the viewer, this empowers them to find their own story. Users can look up data points relevant to their own lives or interests (e.g., "my state," "my department") and see how they compare to others. This transforms a potentially uninteresting or overwhelming dataset into a personalized, engaging, and useful reference tool.

### Core Principle

If a dataset lacks a single, strong narrative, shift the goal from telling a story to providing an explorable utility. Let the user find what is relevant to them.

## When it applies

-   When you have a dataset with high-cardinality categorical data (e.g., many rows for states, countries, products, people).
-   When the primary goal is to provide a detailed reference or allow for individual lookup and comparison, rather than communicating one specific insight.
-   When the visualization will be viewed on an interactive medium like a website or app.

## Exceptions

-   For static media like print, a PDF report, or a non-interactive presentation slide where interactivity is not possible. In these cases, the author must curate the view by highlighting, annotating, or showing only a subset of the data (e.g., top 10, a specific region).
-   When there *is* a very strong, clear story in the data that should be the focus (e.g., "all values are declining rapidly").

## Trade-offs

-   Requires an interactive medium and a tool capable of creating interactive tables.
-   By giving the user control, you cede some of your narrative control as the author.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Information Overload:** Your chart or table is overwhelmingly large and dense, causing viewers' eyes to glaze over.
-   **"Where am I?":** Readers complain that they can't find the data point that's relevant to them.
-   **Lack of Focus:** There's no clear pattern or trend, so viewers don't know where to direct their attention.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix (for static media):** Pre-sort the table by the most important metric. Use bolding or a different background color to highlight key entries like the top/bottom performers, the average, or a specific entry of interest to the audience.

-   **Comprehensive Redesign (for interactive media):** Implement the data in a tool that supports interactive tables.
    1.  **Add a search bar** that filters rows in real-time as the user types.
    2.  **Make column headers clickable** to allow sorting by that metric (ascending and descending).
    3.  **Set a sensible default sort order** (e.g., ranking by the most important measure, as the article does with "poor condition").

## Guidance

When using a color scheme with strong, intuitive symbolism (like a red-yellow-green "stoplight" palette), use softer, less saturated, or muted versions of those colors rather than their fully saturated counterparts.

## Why

The symbolic association of colors like red for "danger/bad" and green for "safety/good" is so powerful that it does not require high-saturation, "in-your-face" colors to be understood. A more subtle palette is often more aesthetically pleasing, appears more professional, and is less visually fatiguing for the reader. This allows the user to focus on the data without being overwhelmed by the color intensity.

### Core Principle

Use color saturation intentionally. High saturation draws the eye and signals alarm; low saturation creates a calmer, more professional tone. Match the palette's intensity to the desired emotional tone of the visualization.

## When it applies

-   You are using a color palette with inherent symbolic meaning (e.g., red/green for good/bad, a sequential blue scale for low/high).
-   The goal is a professional, calm, and readable chart rather than an alarmist one.

## Exceptions

-   In contexts where high alert and immediate, urgent attention are critical, such as a real-time safety dashboard or an emergency alert system. In these cases, high-saturation, jarring colors may be necessary and appropriate to signal danger.

## Trade-offs

-   In a visually noisy environment, muted colors might not stand out as much as bright, saturated ones.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **"In-your-face":** The colors on your chart are described as "garish," "too bright," or "childish."
-   **Visual Fatigue:** Looking at the chart for more than a few seconds feels visually uncomfortable.
-   **Distraction:** The intensity of the colors distracts from reading the data, rather than supporting it.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Reduce Saturation.** Using an HSL or HSB color picker, take your existing saturated colors (e.g., pure red `hsl(0, 100%, 50%)`) and simply reduce the saturation value (e.g., to `hsl(0, 70%, 50%)`). This will instantly mute the tone without changing the hue.

-   **Moderate Approach: Adjust Lightness and Saturation.** Experiment with both saturation and lightness. Often, a slightly lighter and less saturated color feels more sophisticated than just reducing saturation alone. For example, moving from a pure, dark green to a lighter, softer teal.

-   **Comprehensive Redesign: Use a Curated Palette.** Instead of creating colors yourself, adopt a professionally designed palette from a tool like Datawrapper, ColorBrewer, or a corporate design system. These palettes are designed to be harmonious, legible, and aesthetically pleasing.

## Guidance

Do not use color as the only visual means of conveying information. Always provide a redundant, non-color-based encoding such as direct text labels, distinct positions (e.g., separate columns), or unambiguous patterns/icons.

## Why

A significant portion of the population has some form of color vision deficiency (CVD), most commonly an inability to distinguish between red and green. Relying solely on color to differentiate categories or convey meaning will make a visualization unreadable or misleading for these users. Providing a secondary encoding ensures that your message is accessible to the widest possible audience.

### Core Principle

Design for accessibility by providing multiple ways to understand the information. Redundant encodings (e.g., color + label, color + position) make a visualization more robust and inclusive.

## When it applies

-   Always. This is a universal accessibility principle that applies any time color is used to encode information.
-   It is especially critical when using common color pairs that are problematic for CVD, such as red/green or blue/purple.

## Exceptions

-   There are no exceptions to this rule. While using a "colorblind-safe" palette is a good practice, it is not a substitute for providing a redundant encoding, as different people experience CVD with different severities.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The Grayscale Test:** If you convert your chart to grayscale (or print it in black and white), do key distinctions disappear? If so, you are relying too heavily on color.
-   **Legend-Dependent:** The chart becomes meaningless if the color legend is removed.
-   **Simulator Failure:** A colorblindness simulator tool shows that important categories merge into the same or indistinguishable colors.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** The simplest solution is to add text labels directly onto or next to the colored elements of your chart (e.g., on the slices of a pie chart, at the end of bars, or next to lines).

-   **Moderate Approach: Use Patterns or Shapes.** In addition to color, use distinct patterns (e.g., stripes, dots), line styles (e.g., solid, dashed), or marker shapes (e.g., circles, squares) to differentiate series.

-   **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Positional Encoding.** Redesign the visualization to use position as the primary encoding for categories. The source article's solution is a perfect example: by moving from color-coded donut slices to separate, explicitly labeled columns in a table, the distinction between "poor," "fair," and "good" is made with position and text. Color becomes a secondary, supportive visual cue rather than the primary source of information.
