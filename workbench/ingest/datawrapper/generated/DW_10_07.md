## Guidance

When using a sequential color gradient to encode quantitative data, the intuitive mapping for most readers is that lightness corresponds to magnitude. Your gradient should proceed from a **light color (representing low values)** to a **dark color (representing high values)**.

## Why

People naturally and intuitively associate darker colors with more intensity, density, or quantity. This is often called the "dark is more" heuristic. Violating this strong convention forces the reader to stop and consciously re-learn the scale, increasing cognitive load and the risk of misinterpretation.

### Core Principle

Align visual encoding with common perceptual heuristics to make interpretation faster and more accurate.

## When it applies

- Any chart using a sequential color gradient to show quantitative values.
- Most commonly used in choropleth maps and heatmaps.

## Exceptions

- In some very specific domains, a dark-for-low convention might exist (e.g., depth of water on some nautical charts), but this is rare and should only be used if you are certain it's the standard for your expert audience. For a general audience, always use light-for-low.

## Trade-offs

- None. This is a strong convention that is almost always better to follow for clarity and accuracy.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Inverted Scale:** The legend shows that dark colors represent low numbers and light colors represent high numbers.
- **Reader Confusion:** A viewer glances at a map and incorrectly assumes the dark areas represent the highest values, leading to a complete misreading of the data.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Invert the Gradient.** In your charting tool, find the option to "reverse" or "invert" the color scale. This is usually a single checkbox or button and immediately corrects the problem.
