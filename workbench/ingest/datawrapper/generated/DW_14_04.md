## Guidance
When your data has multiple levels of importance, create a corresponding visual hierarchy using color. Assign the most salient color (brightest, most saturated) to the primary data, a less salient color (darker, desaturated) to secondary data, and a neutral color (light gray) to tertiary or contextual data.

## Why
This technique allows you to tell a more nuanced story than a simple "important vs. unimportant" binary. It guides the reader's eye in a specific order: first to the main point, then to the most relevant comparison, and finally to the overall context. This structures the process of comprehension and reduces cognitive load.

### Core Principle
The perceived importance of a visual element should match its importance in the data's story. Use a gradient of visual salience—not just an on/off switch—to represent a gradient of relevance.

## When it applies
- When you have a story with a primary subject, a key comparison group, and general context (e.g., Your country, neighboring countries, the rest of the world).
- When you need to show 3 or 4 distinct levels of importance.
- In explanatory charts where the reading path is authored and intentional.

## Exceptions
- **Too Many Levels:** Attempting to create a hierarchy with more than 3-4 levels of color salience becomes confusing. The differences between levels become too subtle and the hierarchy breaks down.
- **Exploratory Tools:** In an interactive, exploratory tool, it's better to let the user choose what to highlight rather than imposing a fixed hierarchy.

## Trade-offs
- **Increased Complexity:** Designing and interpreting a multi-level hierarchy is more complex than a simple highlight. It requires careful color choice to ensure the levels are perceived in the correct order.
- **Risk of Ambiguity:** If the color steps are not distinct enough, the viewer may not understand the intended order of importance.

## Signs of Trouble
- **Ambiguous Order:** It's not clear which of two colored elements is meant to be more important.
- **Too Much Subtlety:** The difference between your secondary and tertiary colors is so slight that they appear to be the same category.
- **Cognitive Overload:** You've created too many levels (e.g., five different shades of blue), and the system is now more confusing than helpful.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Exaggerate the Differences.** Make the primary color brighter, the secondary color much darker and/or grayer, and the tertiary color a very light gray. Maximize the perceptual distance between each level.
- **Moderate Approach: Use a Single Hue.** Build the hierarchy from a single color. For example: a saturated, bright blue (primary); a dark, desaturated navy blue (secondary); and a very light, grayish blue (tertiary). This ensures a clear relationship between the elements.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Label the Hierarchy.** Use annotations or a structured legend to explicitly state the role of each color. For example, a legend could read: "Highlighted: Germany", "Comparison: EU Average", "Context: Other Nations". This removes all ambiguity.
