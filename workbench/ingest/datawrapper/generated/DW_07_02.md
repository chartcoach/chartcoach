## Guidance

Design your color palette so that colors differ significantly in lightness (brightness), not just in hue. A reliable test is to see if your visualization remains readable when converted to grayscale.

## Why

People with color vision deficiency, and indeed all people, are very good at perceiving differences in lightness. Even if two colors have hues that are confusing (like red and green), a difference in lightness will keep them distinguishable. This "get it right in black and white" principle also makes your charts robust for monochrome printing and readable in poor lighting conditions.

### Core Principle

Redundantly encode information in both color (hue) and lightness (luminance). The visual system is more sensitive to luminance contrast than to chrominance (hue) contrast.

## When it applies

-   Whenever you use multiple colors to distinguish categories in a chart.
-   When designing a palette that must work for both screen and print.
-   When using adjacent colors that could be confusing, such as red and green.

## Exceptions

-   In sequential color schemes, where a gradient of lightness is already the primary visual variable used to encode quantitative data. Here, the goal is smooth transition, not sharp distinction.

## Trade-offs

-   Finding a set of colors that are both aesthetically pleasing and have strong, distinct lightness values can be challenging. For example, it's hard to find a "light red" that still reads as red and contrasts well with a "dark blue".

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The Squint Test:** If you squint at your chart, do different colored categories blur into a single, muddy patch?
-   **The Grayscale Test:** When you print the chart in black and white or use a grayscale filter, do two or more categories become nearly identical shades of gray?
-   **Subtle Neighbors:** The palette includes colors like a medium-lightness green and a medium-lightness orange, which can easily be confused.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick approach:** Take one of the conflicting colors and use your software's color picker to make it significantly lighter or darker, then check again in grayscale.
-   **Moderate approach:** Manually adjust each color in your palette to ensure a clear "staircase" of lightness values. You can check the lightness value (e.g., the 'L' in HSL) for each color.
-   **Comprehensive approach:** Build your color palette from the ground up using a perceptually uniform color space (like L*C*h) to have precise control over lightness, chroma, and hue. Select colors with deliberately different lightness values.
