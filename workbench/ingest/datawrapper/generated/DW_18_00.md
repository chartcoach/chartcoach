## Guidance

When presenting multiple related charts (small multiples), arrange them in a single row or column. This creates a clear reading path, guiding the viewer through a narrative step-by-step, like panels in a comic strip.

## Why

A linear layout establishes a deliberate sequence, which helps in building a story that reveals cause-and-effect relationships or connections between different facets of the data. This transforms a static comparison into a more engaging narrative and reduces the cognitive load of having to connect disparate charts on your own.

### Core Principle

Guide the viewer's attention to construct a narrative, rather than requiring them to synthesize all information at once.

## When it applies

- When you have several related charts showing different components of a larger phenomenon, and you want to explain the connection between them. This is especially effective for interconnected time-series trends.
- When telling a story with a clear beginning, middle, and end (e.g., cause 1 → cause 2 → effect).

## Exceptions

- A grid layout (e.g., 2x2) can be more effective if the goal is direct comparison across two different categorical variables (e.g., comparing four regions across two years) and a linear narrative is not the primary goal. Grids are also more space-efficient.

## Trade-offs

- A linear layout typically uses more horizontal or vertical space than a compact grid layout.
- It imposes a single, author-driven narrative, which may be less suitable for purely exploratory visualizations where the goal is for the user to freely discover their own insights.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Scattered Story:** The viewer has to jump around a grid of charts to piece together the story you're trying to tell.
- **Missed Connections:** Viewers see individual trends but fail to grasp how they relate to one another.
- **"So What?" Reaction:** The visualization presents data without a clear point or takeaway, leaving the audience wondering about its significance.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Numbering.** If you must keep a grid layout for space reasons, add numbers (1, 2, 3, 4) to the corners of each panel to suggest a reading order.
- **Moderate approach: Linearize the Layout.** Re-arrange your small multiples from a grid into a single row or column. Order them logically to build your narrative (e.g., factor 1 → factor 2 → outcome).
- **Comprehensive approach: Integrate a Narrative Flow.** Combine the linear layout with a summary title and annotations that explicitly connect one panel to the next, creating a cohesive visual essay.
