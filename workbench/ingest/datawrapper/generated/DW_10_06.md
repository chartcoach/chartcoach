## Guidance

Whenever possible, choose colors that have a pre-existing, intuitive meaning for your target audience. This includes cultural conventions (e.g., party colors), natural associations (e.g., water is blue), and learned associations (e.g., red means danger/bad).

## Why

Using intuitive colors leverages the audience's existing mental models, reducing the cognitive load required to understand the chart. It makes the visualization feel more natural and easier to interpret. Conversely, violating these conventions can cause confusion or even lead to misinterpretation.

### Core Principle

Reduce cognitive load by aligning visual encodings with the audience's existing knowledge and expectations.

## When it applies

- Visualizing political data (use established party colors).
- Visualizing environmental or geographic data (blue for water, green for forests, brown for land).
- Showing good/bad or positive/negative metrics (green for good, red for bad in many Western cultures).

## Exceptions

- When a key convention is not universal and the audience is global. In such cases, it may be better to use neutral colors and rely on explicit labels.
- When brand guidelines conflict and must take precedence. Acknowledge the potential for confusion.

## Trade-offs

- Stereotypical colors (e.g., pink/blue for gender) can reinforce harmful biases. The article suggests a compromise: use a warm/cool color distinction (e.g., orange/purple) instead of the direct stereotype to avoid it while still providing a subtle associative hint. An even better approach may be to use two neutral, equally-weighted colors.
- The meaning of colors can vary dramatically between cultures (e.g., red means luck/prosperity in China, danger in the US). Know your audience.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Counter-intuitive Mapping:** A chart shows Democrats in red and Republicans in blue, causing confusion for a US audience.
- **Biased Representation:** A chart about gender uses a stereotypical pink and blue palette, which can be seen as exclusionary or simplistic.
- **Semantic Mismatch:** A chart shows profit growth in red and losses in green, forcing the reader to fight their learned association of red with negative outcomes.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Research and Align.** Do a quick search for the conventional colors related to your topic (e.g., "political party colors UK"). Align your chart accordingly.
- **Moderate Redesign (for Gender):** Avoid the pink/blue stereotype. Try a cool color for men (e.g., blue or purple) and a warmer color for women (e.g., yellow, orange, or a warm green) as a less stereotypical alternative. Better yet, use two neutral, equally-weighted colors (e.g., purple and teal).
- **Comprehensive Approach (for Global Audiences):** When creating visualizations for a global audience, be wary of relying on color meanings. Either choose colors with near-universal meanings (e.g., natural colors on a map) or use a neutral palette and rely on clear, unambiguous labels.
