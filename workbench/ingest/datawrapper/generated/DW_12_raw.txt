## Guidance

Use a line chart to visualize how quantitative values change over a continuous period, such as poll numbers leading up to an election.

## Why

Line charts connect individual data points, making it easy for the brain to perceive trends, patterns, rates of change, and volatility. By conventionally plotting time on the horizontal axis, they provide an intuitive representation of change that is easy to interpret.

### Core Principle
Make the most important comparisons the easiest to see. For trends, the most important comparison is a value's position over time, which a line chart makes explicit.

## When it applies

- When you have a series of data points for one or more categories measured at regular or irregular intervals over time (i.e., time-series data).
- When the primary task is to understand trends, see patterns of change, or compare the evolution of multiple series.

## Exceptions

- If you only have a few discrete time points (e.g., two or three years), a bar chart or dot plot might be clearer and less presumptive about the trend between points.
- If you want to emphasize the individual values at each time point rather than the continuous trend, a dot plot could be a better choice.

## Trade-offs

- Can become cluttered and unreadable if too many lines are shown, a situation often called a "spaghetti chart."
- The perceived steepness of a trend can be dramatically influenced by the chart's aspect ratio and the scale of the Y-axis.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Spaghetti Chart:** The chart has so many overlapping and crossing lines that it is impossible to follow any single one.
- **Disconnected Dots:** The data is a time series, but it's shown as a scatter plot, forcing the reader to mentally connect the dots to see the trend.
- **Non-Sequential Axis:** The time axis is not sorted chronologically, making the line's path nonsensical.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** Use distinct, perceptually separated colors for each line and provide a clear, easy-to-read legend.
- **Moderate Approach:** Directly label the lines at the end or at key points. This reduces the cognitive load of looking back and forth between the lines and a legend. You can also highlight the most important line(s) by making them thicker or brighter than the others.
- **Comprehensive Redesign:** If you have many lines, consider breaking the chart into small multiples. This gives each line its own chart while maintaining a common scale, making comparisons possible without the clutter.

## Guidance

Consciously choose the level of smoothing (e.g., using a moving average or LOESS regression) for your trend lines to match your communication goal.

## Why

Raw data, like daily polls, can be very "noisy" or "ragged," making it hard to see the underlying trend. Smoothing averages out this noise to reveal a clearer signal. However, too much smoothing can hide important short-term events or create a misleading sense of stability. The choice represents an editorial judgment about what is signal and what is noise.

## When it applies

- When visualizing noisy time-series data, such as daily polling data, stock prices, or sensor readings, where the goal is to show a general trend.

## Exceptions

- When every individual data point is important and the volatility itself is the story.
- When presenting to a technical audience that needs to see the raw, unaltered data for analysis.
- When there are very few data points, as smoothing can be misleading.

## Trade-offs

- **Clarity vs. Fidelity:** Smoothing increases the clarity of the overall trend but at the cost of losing detail and fidelity to the original data. A smoothed line is an interpretation, not the raw data itself.
- **Risk of Obscuring Data:** A heavily smoothed line might hide a significant but brief spike or dip in the data that could be an important part of the story.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Detached Line:** The smoothed line looks completely detached from the actual data points, suggesting it is a poor model of the data.
- **Noise Overload:** The line is so "ragged" and noisy that no trend is discernible at all.
- **Hidden Method:** A line is smoothed, but there is no mention of the method used, leaving the reader unable to judge its validity.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** Start with a modest level of smoothing, like a 7-day or 10-day moving average for daily data, and see if it clarifies the trend without excessively distorting it.
- **Moderate Approach:** Show the raw data points faintly in the background behind the smoothed line. This provides both the clear trend and the context of the original data's spread and volatility.
- **Comprehensive Approach:** Clearly state the smoothing method in the chart's subtitle or notes (e.g., "Data shown as a 10-day moving average"). For a sophisticated audience, you could visualize the confidence band around the smoothed line to show the range of statistical uncertainty.

## Guidance

When visualizing data from samples, such as polls or surveys, include visual representations of uncertainty like confidence intervals or margins of error.

## Why

Polls and survey results are estimates derived from a sample, not certainties from the whole population. They inherently have a margin of error. Failing to show this uncertainty can lead the audience to believe the numbers are precise and final, causing them to draw false conclusions about small differences between categories (e.g., that one party is "ahead" when the difference is within the margin of error). Visualizing uncertainty is an act of intellectual honesty.

## When it applies

- Any time you are charting statistical estimates rather than exact counts. This is especially critical for polls, survey data, or scientific measurements.

## Exceptions

- When the uncertainty is extremely small and adding error bars would just add visual clutter without changing the interpretation.
- For audiences with very low graph literacy, the extra marks might cause confusion. In these rare cases, a clear textual note ("Margin of error is ±3%") might be a better, though less effective, alternative.

## Trade-offs

- **Cognitive Load:** Adds visual complexity to the chart, which can slightly increase the effort required to read it.
- **Explanation Needed:** May require a brief explanation for a general audience unfamiliar with the concept of statistical error.

## Signs of Trouble

- **False Precision:** A chart presents poll numbers with decimal-point precision but gives no indication of the inherent error, implying a level of accuracy that doesn't exist.
- **Overstating Differences:** An article or presenter makes a big deal about one category being "ahead" of another by a tiny amount that is well within the margin of error.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** Add a note below the chart stating the margin of error for the data (e.g., "Margin of error: ±3 percentage points").
- **Moderate Approach:** Add error bars to your bar chart or dot plot, or a confidence band to your line chart, to visually represent the interval of uncertainty.
- **Comprehensive Approach:** Use a gradient or transparency for the error bars/bands to emphasize that values near the center of the interval are more probable than those at the edges. Accompany the chart with a simple, plain-language explanation of what the margin of error means.

## Guidance

To help your audience understand the significance of current data, show it alongside relevant historical data.

## Why

A single number or a short-term trend is often meaningless without context. Is a 25% poll result good or bad for a party? Comparing it to previous election results instantly provides perspective, revealing whether this is a historic high, a catastrophic low, or business as usual. Context turns data into information.

### Core Principle
"To be truthful and revealing, data graphics must bear on the question at the heart of the problem." - Edward Tufte. Often, that question is "Is this number big or small, good or bad?" which can only be answered through comparison.

## When it applies

- Almost any time you are presenting a key metric, especially in topics like economics, politics, or climate.
- When presenting a value, show it against a baseline, an average, a target, or a time series of past values.

## Exceptions

- When the focus is exclusively on a very recent event where historical context is irrelevant or distracting (e.g., a real-time monitor of an unfolding event).
- When space is extremely limited, though even a small indicator (e.g., an arrow showing change from last year) is better than nothing.

## Trade-offs

- Requires more data gathering and can take up more space in the visualization.
- Can add complexity that might distract from a very simple, focused message.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Recency Bias:** A chart produces "recency bias" by making a small recent change look enormous because the time frame is too short.
- **Context-Free Numbers:** The audience is given numbers but has no way to judge if they are high, low, normal, or abnormal for the subject at hand.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** Add a single reference point or line for a key historical value, like the result from the previous election or the long-term average.
- **Moderate Approach:** Use a line chart or bar chart to show the data for the last few election cycles or decades, as shown in the article's examples.
- **Comprehensive Redesign:** Create an interactive chart where the user can select the time frame, allowing them to zoom from a short-term view to a long-term historical perspective, satisfying both immediate and exploratory needs.

## Guidance

When you need to compare the geographic distribution of several different categories (e.g., vote share for multiple parties), display the data as a series of small, side-by-side maps, also known as small multiples.

## Why

A single choropleth map can only show one variable at a time. Forcing a user to toggle between different views on an interactive map requires them to remember the pattern from the previous view to make a comparison—a task that is difficult and error-prone. Small multiples present all the patterns simultaneously, offloading the work from short-term memory to the eyes and making comparisons direct, easy, and powerful.

### Core Principle
Make comparisons easy by placing visual evidence next to each other. "Comparison is at the heart of quantitative reasoning." - Edward Tufte

## When it applies

- When you want to compare how different variables, categories (like political parties), or time periods play out across the same geographic area.

## Exceptions

- If you have too many categories to fit on a single screen, this approach becomes less effective.
- If the comparison is simple (e.g., only two categories), a single bivariate map might work, though it is often harder to interpret.
- For a narrative story, you may prefer to show one large, detailed map at a time.

## Trade-offs

- **Space:** Uses more screen or page space than a single interactive map.
- **Detail:** The individual maps must be smaller, which may reduce the visibility of fine geographic detail.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Click-to-Compare:** The user has to click a button or dropdown menu many times to switch between different parties on a single map, making comparison tedious and reliant on memory.
- **Overloaded Map:** The visualization tries to cram too much information into one map using complex symbols or overlapping layers that are hard to decode.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** If you can only show one map, choose the most interesting or relevant category and mention the patterns for others in the accompanying text.
- **Moderate Approach:** Create a GIF or animation that cycles through the maps for each category. This is better than manual toggles but still relies on memory.
- **Comprehensive Redesign:** Design a grid of small maps. Crucially, they must all use the same geographic extent and the same color scale, so that a given color means the same thing on every map. Label each map clearly.

## Guidance

In an interactive visualization, use tooltips (information boxes that appear on hover or click) to provide secondary, detailed information about a data point without cluttering the main view.

## Why

This creates a layered information experience. The main visualization provides the "big picture" or an overview (e.g., which party won), while the tooltips provide "details-on-demand" (e.g., by how much they won). This approach keeps the chart clean, scannable, and aesthetically pleasing, while still making rich data accessible to curious users who choose to interact.

## When it applies

- On any interactive chart (maps, scatter plots, line charts, etc.) where each data point has additional attributes you want to make available to the user without overwhelming them.

## Exceptions

- For static, non-interactive charts (like in print or a PDF), all important information must be visible directly on the chart, as there is no interaction.
- If the secondary information is absolutely critical to the main point of the chart, it should not be hidden in a tooltip where some users might miss it.

## Trade-offs

- **Discoverability:** Information in tooltips is hidden by default and requires user interaction, making it less discoverable than information that is always visible.
- **Accessibility:** Can be difficult for users with motor control impairments to trigger. Requires careful implementation to be accessible to screen readers and on touch devices (which lack a "hover" state).

## Signs of Trouble

- **Empty Chart:** The main chart is barren and uninformative without hovering; its entire meaning is hidden in tooltips.
- **Cluttered Tooltip:** The tooltips are so crammed with unstructured text and numbers that they are unreadable.
- **Mobile Failure:** On a mobile device, tapping an element navigates away or does nothing, instead of showing the tooltip information.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** Ensure your tooltips are well-formatted with clear labels and present the most important secondary information first.
- **Moderate Approach:** For mobile and touch devices, ensure that a single tap on a data element reveals the tooltip, and a second tap (or a tap elsewhere on the chart) dismisses it.
- **Comprehensive Redesign:** Carefully decide what information is primary (must be visible to all) and what is truly secondary (can be in a tooltip). For critical information, find a way to encode it directly in the main chart (e.g., using color saturation to show the margin of victory in addition to using hue for the winning party).

## Guidance

When the key question is which combination of parts can achieve a target threshold, create a visualization that explicitly tests and displays these combinations.

## Why

In many systems, the performance of individual components is less important than how they combine to meet a goal. In parliamentary governments, the ultimate outcome depends on which parties can work together to form a majority. A standard bar chart of party support forces the reader to do mental math to figure this out. A dedicated coalition visualizer answers the question directly, providing immediate insight into potential outcomes.

## When it applies

- In election analysis for parliamentary systems to show viable governing coalitions.
- In project management to show which combination of tasks or resources can be completed within a budget or timeline.
- In any scenario where you need to show which combination of components meets a specific goal.

## Exceptions

- In two-party or "winner-take-all" systems where coalitions are not a primary factor.
- When the number of possible combinations is astronomically large and cannot be presented clearly.

## Trade-offs

- This is often a highly specialized chart type that may not be immediately understood without a clear title and explanation.
- It moves the focus away from individual performance and onto group composition.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Mental Math:** The audience is left to mentally add up bars on a bar chart to figure out which combinations are viable.
- **Complex Matrix:** The chart is a complex matrix or diagram that is difficult to decipher, obscuring the simple "yes/no" answer.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** Use a simple annotated list, clearly separating the viable combinations from the non-viable ones, as shown in the article's example. Use color and icons for quick comprehension.
- **Moderate Approach:** For each potential coalition, create a small stacked bar chart that shows the combined share of the parties against a reference line for the required threshold (e.g., 50%).
- **Comprehensive Redesign:** Build an interactive tool where users can select parties to add to a potential coalition and see their combined vote share calculated in real time. This allows them to explore possibilities themselves and understand the dynamics more deeply.
