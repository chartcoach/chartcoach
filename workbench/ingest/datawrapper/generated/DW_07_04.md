## Guidance

Before publishing, test your visualizations using a colorblindness simulator to identify colors that are difficult for people with color vision deficiencies to distinguish.

## Why

Simulators provide a quick and easy way to get an approximation of how your visualization will appear to someone with protanopia, deuteranopia, or tritanopia. This allows you to spot and fix issues where important distinctions are lost before your audience sees the chart, improving the accessibility and professionalism of your work.

## When it applies

-   Any time you use color to encode distinct categories or values in a visualization.
-   As a standard quality check before publishing any chart or map.

## Exceptions

-   Simulators are not a perfect representation of human vision and should not be the *only* method of testing. The article cautions, "They can give you an impression, but that’s it. Everyone is different." Real user feedback is always superior. However, in the absence of user testing, simulators are an essential tool.

## Trade-offs

-   Relying solely on simulators can give a false sense of security. It's a necessary step, but not a sufficient one for guaranteeing perfect accessibility.
-   It adds an extra step to the design workflow, but the time investment is minimal compared to the benefit of creating a more inclusive visualization.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Designing Blind:** You or your team regularly publish visualizations without ever checking them for colorblind accessibility.
-   **Identical Shades:** In a simulation for deuteranopia or protanopia, two different colors in your chart (e.g., a green and an orange) become the exact same shade of brownish-yellow.
-   **Lost Information:** A key insight in your chart disappears when viewed through a simulator.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick approach:** Take a screenshot of your final chart and upload it to a web-based simulator like Coblis.
-   **Moderate approach:** Install a browser extension or desktop app like Color Oracle. This allows you to check your work in real-time as you are designing, which is more efficient than taking screenshots.
-   **Comprehensive approach:** Integrate automated color accessibility checks into your design system and development workflow. Use tools like Datawrapper that provide built-in warnings, or configure linters in your code to flag problematic color combinations.
