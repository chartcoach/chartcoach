## Guidance

To compare the *shape* of trends among categories with vastly different absolute values, use small multiples with independent y-axes for each panel. This scales each line to the full height of its panel, revealing its relative change over time.

## Why

When plotted on a shared y-axis, a line with a very large magnitude (e.g., sales for a flagship product) can visually suppress lines with smaller magnitudes (e.g., sales for a new product). The smaller lines appear flat and uneventful, even if they are experiencing dramatic percentage growth or decline. By giving each line its own y-axis scaled to its own data range, you normalize their visual height, making the *shape* of their respective trends comparable.

### Core Principle

Match the visual scale to the analytical task. When comparing relative change or trend shape, a normalized scale is more effective than an absolute one.

## When it applies

- **You have data series with vastly different orders of magnitude.** For example, comparing the stock price of a $1,000 stock and a $10 stock.
- **The analytical goal is to compare the *pattern* of change**, not the absolute amounts. You want to answer questions like "Did these categories experience a similar dip in Q2?" or "Which category had the most volatile trend?"
- **The audience is interested in relative growth, percentage change, or volatility.**

## Exceptions

- **The audience needs to compare absolute values.** Using independent axes makes this comparison impossible and is highly misleading. If absolute comparison is the goal, a shared axis is mandatory.
- **The audience is likely to misinterpret the chart.** If you cannot be certain that viewers will notice and understand the independent scales, the risk of misinterpretation may be too high. For a general, non-expert audience, it's a dangerous technique.

## Trade-offs

- **Gains:** Reveals important trends in lower-magnitude series that would otherwise be invisible.
- **Sacrifices:** Completely removes the ability to compare absolute values across panels. Introduces a significant risk of viewer misinterpretation if not handled carefully.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Flat Lines:** In a standard line chart, you have one or more lines that look almost completely flat, hovering near the bottom of the chart, because another line's values are so much larger.
- **False Conclusions:** A viewer looks at two panels with independent axes and concludes the two categories have similar values because their lines reach the same height in their respective panels.

## How to Improve

- **Implement Independent Scales:** In your charting tool, select the option for "free" or "independent" axes for your small multiples.
- **Crucially, Signal the Change:** You **must** make it obvious to the reader that the scales are different. Use explicit text annotations like "Note: Y-axis scales differ between panels to show individual trends." This is an ethical obligation to prevent misinterpretation.
