## Guidance

When constructing a stacked chart, ensure you are only plotting the component parts that make up the whole. Do not include a data series for the total itself in the stack.

## Why

The entire purpose of a stacked chart is that the visual sum of the parts (the total height of the column or length of the bar) represents the total. If you include the total as one of the segments to be stacked, you are double-counting it. This creates a chart where the total height is a meaningless, inflated number (often double the true total) and fundamentally misrepresents the data. It is a common but serious data preparation error.

### Core Principle
A visualization must be logically and mathematically sound. The visual representation of the sum should equal the sum of the parts shown.

## When it applies
- When preparing data for any type of stacked chart (column, bar, or area).
- This is often a pre-processing check in your spreadsheet or data preparation tool.

## Exceptions
- There are no exceptions. Including the total as a segment in a stack is always an error.

## Trade-offs
- There are no trade-offs. This is a rule of correctness.

## Signs of Trouble
- **A Dominant Segment:** One segment of your stack is exactly as large as all the other segments combined.
- **Nonsensical Totals:** The y-axis scale goes up to a number that is far higher than what you know the total should be.
- **Redundant Legend:** The legend contains entries for the parts and for the total (e.g., "Men," "Women," and "All Respondents").

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Remove the Total Series.** Go back to your source data and remove the row or column that contains the pre-calculated total before creating the chart. The charting software will calculate and display the total for you by stacking the parts.
