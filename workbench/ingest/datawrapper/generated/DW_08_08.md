## Guidance

Always check your chosen color palette using a color blindness simulator to ensure it is readable by people with common forms of color vision deficiency (CVD).

## Why

You cannot assume your audience sees color the same way you do. The most common form of CVD, red-green color blindness, affects up to 8% of the male population. For these individuals, certain color pairs—most notably red/green but also others like blue/purple or green/brown—can become indistinguishable. If your chart relies on these color pairs to convey information, that information is completely lost to a large segment of your audience. Testing your palettes is not an optional extra; it is a fundamental step to ensure your work is inclusive, accessible, and effective for everyone.

### Core Principle

Design for everyone. An effective visualization must be readable by the widest possible audience, and that includes people with disabilities like color vision deficiency.

## When it applies

- This rule applies to **every single visualization** you create that uses color to convey information.
- It is especially critical for categorical palettes with multiple colors and for diverging palettes that use red and green.

## Exceptions

- There are no exceptions. Even if you believe your audience does not include people with CVD, your work can be shared and seen by others. Designing accessibly is a universal best practice.

## Trade-offs

- **Aesthetic Constraints:** Prioritizing colorblind-safe palettes can sometimes limit your aesthetic choices. Some color combinations might be less trendy or visually punchy than inaccessible alternatives. This is a minor cost for the major benefit of making your work accessible to all.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Red vs. Green:** Your palette's primary comparison is between red and green elements.
- **Hue-based Distinctions:** Your palette relies solely on differences in hue (like a red, a green, a brown) without also varying brightness or saturation. A color blindness simulation will often cause these to collapse into a single muddy color.
- **"I can't tell the difference":** A user tells you they can't distinguish between two colors in your chart. Take this feedback seriously—they may have CVD.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Run a Simulation.** Take a screenshot of your finished chart and upload it to an online simulator like Coblis. Check the deuteranopia and protanopia views. If any key colors become indistinguishable, you need to change them.
- **Moderate Approach: Choose Better Pairs.** When picking colors, avoid problematic pairs. Instead of red/green, use blue/orange. Instead of blue/purple, use blue/yellow. Varying both hue and brightness/saturation is key. For example, a light blue and a dark orange is a very robust pair.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Start with Accessible Palettes.** Don't pick colors and then test; pick colors that are already tested. Use tools like ColorBrewer or the Datawrapper palette creator and enable the "colorblind safe" filter from the beginning. This ensures your foundation is accessible, and you can build your design on top of that with confidence. Also, consider adding redundant cues (symbols, patterns, labels) so color is not the *only* way to get the information.
