## Guidance

Prioritize visual channels like position (dot plots) or length (bar charts) when readers need to accurately compare specific quantitative values. Reserve color gradients for showing broad patterns, especially in maps.

## Why

Humans are poor at accurately judging specific quantitative values from color shades. It's difficult to map a specific color to a precise number or to accurately gauge the difference between two similar colors. Position along a common scale is the most accurate perceptual task, making it the best choice for comparisons.

### Core Principle

Visual encodings should be chosen based on the task. For precise comparison or value lookup, use more perceptually accurate encodings like position or length. For broad pattern detection, color can be effective.

## When it applies

- When the primary goal is for the audience to compare individual data points (e.g., "Which state has a higher value?").
- When showing key metrics that require precise interpretation.
- When an alternative chart type (like a bar chart or dot plot) can represent the same data without losing essential context.

## Exceptions

- When visualizing spatial patterns on a choropleth map is the primary goal. In this case, color is the most effective channel for showing geographic distribution, and the task is pattern detection, not precise value lookup.
- When space is extremely limited and a heatmap is the only viable option. Even then, consider adding values as text within the cells.

## Trade-offs

- Using a bar chart or dot plot may lose the geographic context that a map provides. A potential solution is to show a map alongside a bar chart.
- A bar chart can take up more space than a compact choropleth map.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Value Ambiguity:** It's hard to tell if two similarly-colored regions on a map have the same value or slightly different ones.
- **Legend Overload:** Viewers must constantly refer back and forth between the chart and a complex gradient legend to interpret values.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Labels.** If you must use a color-encoded chart like a map, add direct text labels for the most important values to provide an escape hatch for precision.
- **Moderate Redesign: Provide an Alternative Chart.** Accompany the map with a sorted bar chart of the same data. This allows for both pattern detection (on the map) and precise comparison (on the bar chart).
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Switch the Primary Chart Type.** If precise comparison is the main goal, change the visualization from a choropleth map to a bar chart or dot plot.

## Guidance

When using color to distinguish categories, avoid using more than seven distinct colors. If you have more categories, either group them or choose a different chart type.

## Why

The more colors used, the harder it is for a reader to quickly distinguish between them and remember what each color represents. This increases cognitive load, as the reader must frequently consult the legend, slowing down comprehension. It also becomes increasingly difficult to find a set of colors that are all visually distinct from each other and from the background.

### Core Principle

Reduce cognitive load by simplifying the visual encoding. The human working memory has a limited capacity for remembering arbitrary associations like color-to-category mappings.

## When it applies

- When encoding nominal (categorical) data with color in charts like line charts, scatter plots, stacked bar charts, and pie charts.
- When dealing with high-cardinality categorical data where each category needs to be distinguished.

## Exceptions

- When colors are not meant for individual comparison but to show a larger, complex pattern, and direct interaction (like hovering) is available to reveal details on demand.
- When using a bar chart where each bar is directly labeled, colors can serve as a secondary, weaker encoding.

## Trade-offs

- Grouping categories into an "Other" bucket results in a loss of data granularity.
- Switching to another chart type (like a table or a series of small multiples) might be less visually engaging but is often much clearer.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Rainbow Puke:** The chart is a riot of colors, making it visually overwhelming and difficult to focus.
- **Legend Over-Reliance:** A viewer cannot understand the chart without constantly looking back and forth between the data and a long legend.
- **Ambiguous Hues:** The palette includes colors that are too similar to be reliably distinguished (e.g., teal vs. light blue, orange vs. light brown).

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Group Categories.** Combine less important or smaller categories into a single "Other" category. Ensure this group is colored a neutral grey to de-emphasize it.
- **Moderate Redesign: Highlight Key Categories.** Identify the 1-3 most important categories for your story. Give them distinct, saturated colors and color all other categories in various shades of grey.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Change Chart Type.** For high-cardinality data, a bar chart where each category has a label is often clearer than a color-coded line chart. Small multiples, where each category gets its own chart, are another excellent alternative.

## Guidance

When creating a series of charts within a single report, dashboard, or article, use the same color for the same category or variable consistently. Once a color is assigned to a category, do not reuse it for a different category in the same context.

## Why

Consistency builds a "visual language" for the reader. Reusing colors for different things creates confusion, undermines comparability, and increases the risk of misinterpretation. Consistent coloring reduces cognitive load, as the reader learns the color-category association once and can apply it across all related visualizations.

### Core Principle

Establish and maintain internal consistency to create a coherent narrative and reduce the cognitive burden on the audience.

## When it applies

- In any document, dashboard, or presentation containing more than one chart where the same categories appear.
- When the same entities (e.g., countries, products, political parties) are shown in multiple visualizations.

## Exceptions

- If you are only using a single highlight color in all your charts (e.g., all charts are monochrome blue with grey context), it's acceptable for that blue to represent different key variables (e.g., unemployment in one chart, GDP in another), as it's not being used to create a categorical distinction within a multi-color palette. The role of the color is "highlight," not "identity."

## Trade-offs

- Requires more planning upfront to create a master color palette for the entire report or project.
- Can be challenging if different charts have overlapping but not identical sets of categories.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Reader Confusion:** A reader assumes the blue line in chart 2 represents the same thing as the blue bar in chart 1, but it doesn't.
- **Inconsistent Narrative:** The visual story feels disjointed and requires re-learning the color key for each new chart.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Audit and Correct.** Review all charts in your document. Create a list of all unique categories and assign a unique color to each. Update the charts to match this master palette.
- **Moderate Redesign: Create a Shared Legend.** In a dashboard, use a single, global color legend that applies to all charts, making the consistency explicit and reinforcing the connections.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Systematize Colors.** Define a color palette in your design system or style guide. For example, "USA is always blue," "Germany is always orange," "Product A is always green," etc., and apply this system programmatically.

## Guidance

Every time color is used to represent data values or categories, explain what those colors mean. Use a color key, direct labels, or an annotated legend integrated into the chart's title or text.

## Why

Unexplained visual marks are meaningless. A legend is essential for decoding the visualization. Without it, the reader cannot understand what the colors represent, making the chart useless and frustrating.

Make visualizations self-contained and decodable. Don't assume the reader can guess the meaning of your visual encodings.

## Guidance

Employ the color grey for background elements, secondary data series, or unselected items to make your primary, color-coded data points stand out more effectively.

## Why

Grey acts as a neutral backdrop that reduces visual noise and directs the viewer's attention to the most important parts of the story, which should be rendered in more saturated "highlight" colors. This creates a clear visual hierarchy, telling the reader what to look at first.

## Guidance

All text and important visual marks should have a high enough contrast ratio against their background to be easily readable. The W3C recommendation is a minimum ratio of **4.5:1 for normal text** and **3:1 for large text**.

## Why

Low contrast makes text and visuals difficult or impossible to read, especially for people with low vision or in non-ideal viewing conditions (e.g., screen glare, low-light). Ensuring high contrast is a fundamental requirement for creating accessible and effective visualizations.

### Core Principle

Design for legibility and accessibility. Information that cannot be clearly seen is useless.

## When it applies

- Any text on a visualization: titles, labels, annotations, legends, source lines.
- Any visual marks (lines, dots) that need to be distinguished from the background.
- This is a universal guideline that applies to all charts, mediums, and audiences.

## Exceptions

- **Logos & Incidental Text:** Text that is part of a logo or is purely decorative and conveys no information is exempt from this requirement. However, this should be used with extreme caution in data visualization.

## Trade-offs

- Extremely high contrast (e.g., pure black `#000000` on pure white `#FFFFFF`) can sometimes cause eye strain for some readers. Using an off-black on an off-white is often more comfortable while still meeting contrast requirements.
- Aesthetic goals might favor subtle, low-contrast palettes, but these must be sacrificed for the sake of readability and accessibility.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Squint Test:** You have to squint or lean in to read text on the chart.
- **Disappearing Text:** Light grey text on a white background, or medium-colored text on a similarly-colored background, becomes nearly invisible.
- **Fails a Check:** A contrast checking tool reports a ratio below the recommended 4.5:1 threshold for your text and background colors.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Darken the Text.** The easiest way to increase contrast is usually to darken the foreground color (text) or lighten the background color. Change light grey text to a darker grey or black.
- **Moderate Redesign: Use a Contrast Checker.** Use a web-based or browser-integrated tool (like WebAIM's) to check all your color pairs. Adjust colors until they meet the minimum WCAG ratios.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Build an Accessible Palette.** Design your entire color palette from the start with contrast in mind. Define specific text colors, background colors, and accent colors that are all pre-checked for accessibility compliance. Avoid placing text on top of complex image or gradient backgrounds without a solid-colored "scrim" behind the text.

Using intuitive colors leverages the audience's existing mental models, reducing the cognitive load required to understand the chart. It makes the visualization feel more natural and easier to interpret. Conversely, violating these conventions can cause confusion or even lead to misinterpretation.

Reduce cognitive load by aligning visual encodings with the audience's existing knowledge and expectations.

- Showing good/bad or positive/negative metrics (green for good, red for bad in many Western cultures).

## Guidance

When using a sequential color gradient to encode quantitative data, the intuitive mapping for most readers is that lightness corresponds to magnitude. Your gradient should proceed from a **light color (representing low values)** to a **dark color (representing high values)**.

People naturally and intuitively associate darker colors with more intensity, density, or quantity. This is often called the "dark is more" heuristic. Violating this strong convention forces the reader to stop and consciously re-learn the scale, increasing cognitive load and the risk of misinterpretation.

### Core Principle

Align visual encoding with common perceptual heuristics to make interpretation faster and more accurate.

## When it applies

- Any chart using a sequential color gradient to show quantitative values.
- Most commonly used in choropleth maps and heatmaps.

## Exceptions

- In some very specific domains, a dark-for-low convention might exist (e.g., depth of water on some nautical charts), but this is rare and should only be used if you are certain it's the standard for your expert audience. For a general audience, always use light-for-low.

## Trade-offs

- None. This is a strong convention that is almost always better to follow for clarity and accuracy.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Inverted Scale:** The legend shows that dark colors represent low numbers and light colors represent high numbers.
- **Reader Confusion:** A viewer glances at a map and incorrectly assumes the dark areas represent the highest values, leading to a complete misreading of the data.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Invert the Gradient.** In your charting tool, find the option to "reverse" or "invert" the color scale. This is usually a single checkbox or button and immediately corrects the problem.

## Guidance

To represent nominal (unordered) categories, use a palette of different hues (e.g., blue, orange, green). Do not use shades of a single hue (e.g., a sequential gradient from light blue to dark blue).

## Why

A single-hue gradient (a sequential palette) implies order, rank, and magnitude through its change in lightness. Using it for categories that have no inherent order (like countries, products, or departments) is misleading, as it will make viewers incorrectly assume that the "darker" categories are more important or have a higher value. Different hues are perceived as qualitatively different, which correctly matches the nature of categorical data.

### Core Principle

The visual encoding should match the structure of the data. Use a categorical (qualitative) color scheme for categorical data, and an ordered (sequential/diverging) scheme for quantitative/ordered data.

## When it applies

- When assigning colors to categories in a pie chart, donut chart, stacked bar chart, line chart, or scatter plot.
- Any time the data being colored is nominal/categorical.

## Exceptions

- If you are intentionally highlighting one category, you can color it with a distinct hue and use a greyscale gradient for all other (de-emphasized) categories.
- If categories are ordered (ordinal data, e.g., 'low', 'medium', 'high'), then a sequential scheme is appropriate.

## Trade-offs

- A multi-hue palette can look "too colorful" or less aesthetically cohesive than a monochrome palette. However, clarity and honesty should almost always take precedence over this type of aesthetic preference.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Implied Ranking:** A bar chart of "Sales by Department" uses shades of blue, leading viewers to wonder why the "dark blue" department is ranked higher than the "light blue" one, even when the bars are sorted alphabetically.
- **Identity Confusion:** It's much harder to talk about "the third-darkest-blue line" than "the orange line." Distinct hues give categories a clearer visual identity.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Switch to a Qualitative Palette.** In your tool, change the color palette setting from "Sequential" to "Qualitative" or "Categorical." Tools like ColorBrewer provide excellent pre-built options.
- **Moderate Redesign: Re-evaluate the Chart.** If the chart still feels too colorful with a proper categorical palette, it might be a sign that you have too many categories and should consider grouping them or switching to a different chart type (see `limit-categorical-colors`).

## Guidance

A color gradient used to represent quantitative data must have a clear and monotonic progression from light to dark. The gradient should be perceptually uniform, meaning the steps in lightness look consistent. Do not rely on changes in hue alone.

## Why

The human visual system is much better at perceiving order from changes in lightness than from changes in hue. A gradient that works well will still show the data's pattern if converted to greyscale. Rainbow palettes are particularly bad because they have uneven changes in lightness (e.g., yellow is much lighter than both red and blue), which distorts the data, creates false boundaries, and is not accessible to colorblind users.

### Core Principle

Lightness is the most effective color channel for communicating magnitude and order. A good gradient is essentially a greyscale gradient with color added.

## When it applies

- When designing or choosing a sequential or diverging color gradient.
- For choropleth maps and heatmaps.

## Exceptions

- None. A quantitative gradient without a strong lightness component is fundamentally flawed.

## Trade-offs

- A purely lightness-based gradient (greyscale) is clear but can be less engaging. Adding one or two hues can make it more aesthetically pleasing without compromising perceptual order (see `use-multi-hue-gradients`).

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Rainbow Problem:** The chart uses a "rainbow" or "spectral" color scale (red-orange-yellow-green-blue-purple).
- **Greyscale Test Failure:** When you view the chart in greyscale (or with a colorblindness simulator), the data pattern disappears or becomes distorted because different hues have similar lightness levels.
- **False Boundaries:** The gradient creates "stripes" of color that make it seem like there are abrupt changes in the data where none exist.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Use a Pre-built, Perceptually Uniform Palette.** Don't create your own gradients unless you are an expert. Use trusted sources like ColorBrewer, CartoColors, or the defaults in tools like Datawrapper (e.g., Viridis), which have been specifically designed to be perceptually uniform.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch from Rainbow to Sequential.** Replace the rainbow scale with a single-hue sequential scale (e.g., light blue to dark blue) or a well-designed multi-hue sequential scale (e.g., light yellow to dark green).
- **Comprehensive Approach: Use a Perceptual Color Space.** If you must design your own palette, do it in a color space like HCL (Hue-Chroma-Luminance) or L*a*b*. Create the gradient by primarily varying luminance (lightness) in a monotonic way.

## Guidance

When your quantitative data has a critical, meaningful center point (e.g., zero, a national average, or a baseline), use a diverging color gradient. This palette should use two distinct hues for the endpoints and a neutral, light color for the center.

## Why

A diverging palette is specifically designed to visually emphasize values on both sides of a central point. It uses one hue for values above the midpoint and a different hue for values below, with color saturation increasing towards the extremes. This immediately draws the eye to the highest and lowest values and clearly separates positive/negative or above/below-average data.

### Core Principle

The visual encoding should match the structure of the data. Use a diverging color scheme for data that diverges from a central point.

## When it applies

- Visualizing data that can be positive or negative (e.g., profit/loss, temperature change).
- Showing how regions compare to a national average (e.g., unemployment above or below the mean).
- Visualizing poll results showing support/opposition for a policy.

## Exceptions

- Do not use a diverging palette if there is no meaningful midpoint. If your data simply runs from a low to a high value (e.g., total population), a sequential palette is the appropriate choice. Using a diverging palette in this case would be confusing.

## Trade-offs

- It can be harder to compare the magnitudes of two values if they are on opposite sides of the gradient (e.g., is a light blue value greater or less than a light red value?). The primary task for a diverging map is identifying the location and sign (positive/negative) of extremes, not fine-grained comparison across the midpoint.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Arbitrary Midpoint:** A diverging palette is used on data that doesn't have a natural center, forcing the reader to wonder what the neutral color signifies.
- **Bright Center:** The center of the diverging palette is a bright, saturated color (like white or yellow), which can incorrectly draw attention to the middle values instead of the extremes. The center should be a muted, light neutral like grey.
- **Wrong Tool for the Job:** A sequential gradient is used for diverging data, hiding the critical midpoint and making it hard to distinguish positive from negative values.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Use a Pre-built Diverging Palette.** Select a palette from a trusted source like ColorBrewer (e.g., "RdBu," "BrBG," "PiYG"). These are balanced and use an appropriate neutral center. A blue-orange palette is a very safe choice that avoids red-green colorblindness issues.
- **Moderate Redesign: Set a Meaningful Center.** Ensure your charting tool is centering the gradient on the correct data value. If you're comparing to an average of 15.3%, the center of your gradient must be set to 15.3%, not an arbitrary value like 0.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Design a Balanced Palette.** If designing your own, pick two distinct hues. Ensure the lightness steps are balanced on both sides of the light, neutral center color so that a value of +X and -X have the same perceptual weight.

## Guidance

Ensure that your chosen colors can be differentiated by people with common forms of color vision deficiency (CVD). Rely on variations in lightness, not just hue, and use simulation tools to check your palettes.

## Why

A significant portion of the population (up to 8% of men and 0.5% of women) has some form of CVD. If your palette relies on hue distinctions that are invisible to them (like red vs. green), your chart will be unreadable or misleading for this large audience. Designing for accessibility is crucial for effective and inclusive communication.

### Core Principle

Design for all users, including those with disabilities. Information must be robustly encoded so it does not fail when a single visual channel (like hue perception) is compromised.

## When it applies

- Always. This is a universal best practice for any visualization that uses color to encode information.

## Exceptions

- None. There is no good reason to knowingly create a visualization that is inaccessible to a large part of your potential audience.

## Trade-offs

- Designing CVD-safe palettes slightly constrains your aesthetic choices, as you must avoid problematic color combinations (like red/green or blue/purple of similar lightness). This is a minor cost for a major gain in accessibility and clarity.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Red/Green Combo:** The palette relies heavily on distinguishing between red and green, the most common problem for CVD.
- **Simulator Failure:** When viewed through a color blindness simulator, two or more distinct categories or gradient steps merge into the same or very similar colors.
- **Hue-Only Palettes:** The chosen colors have very different hues but almost identical lightness levels, making them indistinguishable without full-color vision.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Use a Safe Palette.** Start with a color palette that is explicitly designed to be colorblind-safe. Trusted sources include ColorBrewer (filter for "colorblind safe"), the Okabe-Ito palette, or palettes like Viridis.
- **Moderate Redesign: Test and Adjust Your Palette.** Use an online tool (like Viz Palette or Coblis) to upload your chart and simulate how it looks to users with different forms of CVD. If two colors blend together, adjust one of them, usually by making it significantly lighter or darker.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Use Redundant Encodings.** Do not rely on color alone to convey information. This is a core principle of WCAG. Add other visual cues to distinguish elements, such as different shapes for points in a scatter plot, different dashing patterns for lines in a line chart, or direct text labels. This makes your chart more robust for all users.
