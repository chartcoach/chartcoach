## Guidance

For quantitative data that is fairly evenly distributed, use a linear color scale interpolation. This applies to both stepped (classed) and continuous (unclassed) scales.

## Why

Linear interpolation creates a direct, proportional relationship between a value and its assigned color. Each step in value corresponds to an equal step in color. This is the most intuitive and transparent way to represent the data's full range, and it accurately highlights the magnitude of outliers.

### Core Principle

Visual encoding should be directly proportional to the data values unless there is a specific reason to distort the scale.

## When it applies

- When visualizing quantitative data (e.g., on a choropleth map or heatmap) where the values are spread out relatively evenly across the range.
- When there are no significant skews or extreme outliers that would dominate the dataset.
- When your primary goal is to show the absolute magnitude of values, including outliers, in a transparent way.

## Exceptions

Do not use linear interpolation for heavily skewed data. Outliers will consume most of the color range, "washing out" the variation in the denser parts of the distribution and making most data points appear in the same low-intensity color.

## Trade-offs

While honest to the full data range, linear interpolation can be less effective at showing subtle differences within dense clusters of data compared to methods like quantiles or natural breaks, which are designed to reveal such patterns.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Washed-Out Map:** Most of your map or heatmap is one uniform, light color, with only a few tiny areas in dark colors. This indicates a skewed distribution where the linear scale is failing to show detail.
- **Empty Legend Bins:** In a stepped legend, some color steps correspond to very few or no data points, while one step contains almost all of them.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** If your data is only slightly skewed, you can try using a diverging scale centered on the median instead of a sequential one to better balance the color distribution.
- **Moderate Approach:** Switch to a different interpolation method. A quantile scale will ensure each color is used for an equal number of data points, revealing variation in the dense areas.
- **Comprehensive Approach:** First, visualize your data's distribution with a histogram. If it is highly skewed, choose an interpolation method designed for that distribution, such as quantile or logarithmic scaling, to reveal more detail where most of the data lies.
