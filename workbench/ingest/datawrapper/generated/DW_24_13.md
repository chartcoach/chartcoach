## Guidance

Set the default sort order of your table based on the most important or interesting column, rather than simply sorting the first column alphabetically.

## Why

The first few rows of a table receive the most attention. An alphabetical sort is arbitrary and rarely surfaces the most meaningful data. Sorting by a key quantitative metric (e.g., highest population, biggest change, top sales) immediately answers important questions for the reader ("Who is number one? What's the biggest value?") and surfaces the main story without requiring any interaction.

### Core Principle

Don't make the user do work you can do for them. By pre-sorting the data thoughtfully, you deliver insight on first glance.

## When it applies

- When creating any table with more than a few rows.
- Especially important for long tables where most data is hidden by scrolling or pagination. The top-sorted items are the only ones guaranteed to be seen.

## Exceptions

- If the table is a dictionary or reference where alphabetical lookup is the primary and expected function, then alphabetical sort is the correct choice.
- If there is no single "most important" metric, you might allow the user to choose the sort order, but you still must pick a sensible default.

## Trade-offs

- Choosing a default sort order imposes a specific narrative on the data. This is usually a good thing, but it de-emphasizes other potential stories. This trade-off is mitigated by offering interactive column sorting.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "A"s Have It:** Your table's top rows are filled with entries for Afghanistan, Albania, and Algeria, while the most impactful data for China or the United States is buried hundreds of rows down.
- **The Buried Lede:** The most interesting finding or most extreme value in your dataset is not visible without scrolling or re-sorting the table.

## How to Improve

- **Action: Re-sort the Table.** Identify the column that best tells the story or contains the most significant values. Sort the entire table by that column in descending (or ascending) order. You can even sort by a column that you choose not to display (an "invisible column"), such as sorting cities by population without showing the population number itself.
