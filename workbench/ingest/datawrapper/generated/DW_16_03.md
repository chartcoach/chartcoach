## Guidance

Do not use color as the only visual means of conveying information. Always provide a redundant, non-color-based encoding such as direct text labels, distinct positions (e.g., separate columns), or unambiguous patterns/icons.

## Why

A significant portion of the population has some form of color vision deficiency (CVD), most commonly an inability to distinguish between red and green. Relying solely on color to differentiate categories or convey meaning will make a visualization unreadable or misleading for these users. Providing a secondary encoding ensures that your message is accessible to the widest possible audience.

### Core Principle

Design for accessibility by providing multiple ways to understand the information. Redundant encodings (e.g., color + label, color + position) make a visualization more robust and inclusive.

## When it applies

-   Always. This is a universal accessibility principle that applies any time color is used to encode information.
-   It is especially critical when using common color pairs that are problematic for CVD, such as red/green or blue/purple.

## Exceptions

-   There are no exceptions to this rule. While using a "colorblind-safe" palette is a good practice, it is not a substitute for providing a redundant encoding, as different people experience CVD with different severities.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The Grayscale Test:** If you convert your chart to grayscale (or print it in black and white), do key distinctions disappear? If so, you are relying too heavily on color.
-   **Legend-Dependent:** The chart becomes meaningless if the color legend is removed.
-   **Simulator Failure:** A colorblindness simulator tool shows that important categories merge into the same or indistinguishable colors.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** The simplest solution is to add text labels directly onto or next to the colored elements of your chart (e.g., on the slices of a pie chart, at the end of bars, or next to lines).

-   **Moderate Approach: Use Patterns or Shapes.** In addition to color, use distinct patterns (e.g., stripes, dots), line styles (e.g., solid, dashed), or marker shapes (e.g., circles, squares) to differentiate series.

-   **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Positional Encoding.** Redesign the visualization to use position as the primary encoding for categories. The source article's solution is a perfect example: by moving from color-coded donut slices to separate, explicitly labeled columns in a table, the distinction between "poor," "fair," and "good" is made with position and text. Color becomes a secondary, supportive visual cue rather than the primary source of information.
