## Guidance

Use a single, saturated color to draw attention to the most important data in your chart. Render all other contextual or comparison data in a neutral color like grey.

## Why

Color is a powerful pre-attentive attribute that guides a viewer's eye before they even consciously think about it. By highlighting the data that supports your main point and pushing everything else into the background, you create a strong visual hierarchy. This makes the chart easier and faster to read, reduces cognitive load, and ensures the viewer focuses on the intended message.

### Core Principle

Use visual salience to match thematic importance. The most important information should be the most visually prominent.

## When it applies

-   When your chart contains multiple data series but you want to tell a story about one of them in particular.
-   When you have added contextual data (see 'provide-comparison-context') and need to visually separate it from the primary data.

## Exceptions

-   When all data series are of equal importance and the task is pure exploration rather than communication of a specific insight.
-   In a categorical comparison where the task is to distinguish between all categories equally.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The Rainbow Effect:** Every bar or line has a different, bright color, creating visual noise and suggesting all are equally important.
-   **Lost in the Crowd:** The most important data series uses a color that is no more prominent than any other, forcing the reader to search for the story.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix:** In your chart tool, change the color of all contextual lines or bars to a light or medium grey. Change the color of your main data series to a more saturated, distinct color.
-   **Moderate Approach:** Ensure your chosen highlight color is not only distinct from the grey but also accessible for users with color vision deficiencies. It should have sufficient contrast and not rely on a red/green distinction alone.
