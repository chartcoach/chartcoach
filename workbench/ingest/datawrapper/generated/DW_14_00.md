## Guidance
Before choosing any colors or even a chart type, first decide on the primary message you want to communicate. Write down what the viewer absolutely must learn from your visualization, and prioritize your insights.

## Why
Design choices, especially color, should serve a purpose. Without a clear communication goal, color is often applied decoratively, which at best adds nothing and at worst creates confusion. By starting with your intended message, you can make deliberate, functional choices—like using color for emphasis—that guide your audience to the correct conclusion quickly and efficiently.

### Core Principle
If everything is emphasized, nothing is emphasized. A clear visual hierarchy requires a clear information hierarchy.

## When it applies
- Always, when creating an explanatory visualization intended to inform or persuade an audience.
- At the very beginning of the design process, before you are committed to a specific visual form.

## Exceptions
- **Exploratory Analysis:** When you are using visualizations for your own analysis to explore a dataset and find insights, you do not yet have a defined goal. In this phase, you are the audience, and the goal is discovery, not communication.

## Trade-offs
- **Reduces Serendipity:** By focusing on one narrative, you guide the viewer away from discovering other, potentially interesting patterns in the data. This approach prioritizes clarity of one message over the breadth of all possible messages.
- **Requires Upfront Effort:** It takes time and critical thinking to distill a complex dataset into a single, clear message. It's often faster (but less effective) to simply "show all the data."

## Signs of Trouble
- **"So What?" Reaction:** You present a chart, and your audience's reaction is "Okay... so what?" This indicates the chart lacks a clear point.
- **Decorative Coloring:** Colors are used because they "look nice" or are the software default, but they don't correspond to the data's meaning or your message.
- **Analysis Paralysis:** You find yourself endlessly tweaking the design because you're not sure what the most important part of the chart is.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Write a Title First.** Before making the chart, write the title or headline you would give it. This forces you to summarize the main finding. Then, design the chart to support that title.
- **Moderate Approach: The "Big Idea" Exercise.** Articulate your message in a single, complete sentence that states your unique point of view and its significance. For example: "Q4 sales in the North region increased by 30%, exceeding targets, due to our new marketing campaign." This sentence becomes your guide for all design decisions.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Storyboarding.** For a complex presentation, sketch a sequence of charts and annotations that build a narrative. This ensures each chart has a specific role in a larger story, forcing you to define the goal for each visual.
