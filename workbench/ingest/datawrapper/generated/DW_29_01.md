## Guidance

Instead of using a separate legend or color key, place labels directly on or next to the chart elements (lines, bars, pie slices) they describe.

## Why

Forcing a reader to look back and forth between a chart and its legend is inefficient. This "eye-travel" increases cognitive load, as the reader must hold the color and its meaning in their working memory while scanning the chart. Direct labeling eliminates this mental work, making the visualization faster to read, easier to understand, and less prone to error.

### Core Principle

Reduce the cognitive work required to decode your visualization. Every unnecessary mental step is a chance for the reader to get lost or give up.

## When it applies

- For charts with multiple categories distinguished by color, shape, or pattern, such as multi-line charts, pie charts, and stacked or grouped bar charts.
- When you want to make a chart as easy to read as possible, especially for a general audience.

## Exceptions

- In very dense charts where direct labels would overlap and become unreadable (e.g., a line chart with dozens of noisy, crisscrossing lines). In these cases, interactive tooltips or highlighting can be a better alternative.
- When a legend serves an interactive function, such as allowing users to click to filter or highlight specific data series.

## Trade-offs

- **Effort:** Direct labeling often requires more manual effort than using an auto-generated legend provided by charting software.
- **Clutter:** In a crowded chart, it can be challenging to position labels clearly without them overlapping other data elements.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Back-and-Forth:** You (or your users) have to repeatedly glance between the chart's data and a separate legend to understand what is being shown.
- **The Color Matching Game:** The primary task for the reader becomes a game of "match the color" rather than interpreting the data's patterns.
- **Accessibility Issues:** A legend that relies solely on color is inaccessible to users with color vision deficiency. Direct labels provide a redundant text-based cue.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Move the legend.** If you must use a legend, move it as close as possible to the data it describes instead of sequestering it in a corner.

- **Moderate Fix: Label directly.** For line charts, place labels at the end of each line. For bar or pie charts, label the segments directly. A best practice is to color the label text to match the data element it describes.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Eliminate the legend.** Consider alternative chart forms that don't require a legend. For example, instead of a multi-line chart with a color legend, use small multiples where each data series gets its own small chart with a clear title.
