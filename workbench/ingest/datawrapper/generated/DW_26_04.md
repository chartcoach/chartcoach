## Guidance

When visualizing a large number of categories (e.g., more than 8-10) that lack established color conventions, use a "confetti" or "bonbon" palette composed of a wide variety of perceptually distinct hues.

## Why

It is perceptually impossible to create a large set of colors that are all both semantically meaningful and clearly distinct. When the number of categories is high, the primary goal must shift from meaning to pure differentiability. A confetti palette abandons the search for meaning and instead uses the full spectrum of hues to ensure that each category has a unique visual identifier. The color choices are arbitrary, but they are effective because they are distinct.

## When it applies

-   Visualizing data with high-cardinality categorical variables (e.g., >10 unique values).
-   Situations like primary elections with many candidates, detailed survey results with many response options, or product breakdowns with numerous sub-categories.
-   When no pre-existing color conventions or logical groupings exist for the categories.

## Exceptions

-   If the many categories can be logically grouped into a smaller number of super-groups, it may be better to use a few base hues and vary the lightness/saturation within each group to show the hierarchy (e.g., different shades of blue for 5 related products, different shades of green for another 4).

## Trade-offs

-   The color assignments are arbitrary and have no semantic meaning, so viewers must rely entirely on the legend to decode the chart.
-   Can result in a very colorful, almost chaotic aesthetic that may not be appropriate for all contexts.
-   Finding more than about 20 truly distinct colors is a significant challenge even for automated tools.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Color Overload:** The sheer number of colors makes the chart feel overwhelming and difficult to read.
-   **Legend Hunt:** The legend is so long that it becomes a chore for the user to find the category they are looking for.
-   **Lookalikes Emerge:** In your palette of 15 colors, you accidentally have a 'sky blue' and a 'light blue' that are nearly impossible to tell apart on the actual chart.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Use an Automated Palette Generator.** Don't try to pick 15 distinct colors by hand. Use a tool like Colorgorical or IWantHue designed specifically for generating large, perceptually-distant palettes.
-   **Moderate Approach: Reduce Categories.** Question if you really need to show every single category. Can you group smaller categories into an "Other" bucket? Prioritizing and simplifying the data is often more effective than finding a perfect 20-color palette.
-   **Comprehensive Approach: Don't Use Color to Categorize.** For very high cardinality, color is often the wrong tool. Switch to a different chart type that doesn't rely on a color legend. A simple sorted bar chart with direct labels is far more effective for comparing 50 categories than a pie chart with 50 colored slices. Use small multiples to break the data down into manageable chunks.
