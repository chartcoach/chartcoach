## Guidance

For categorical palettes where all categories are of equal importance, ensure all colors have a similar perceived brightness (luminance). This prevents some categories from unintentionally standing out more than others, ensuring a fair visual comparison.

## Why

The human eye is highly sensitive to differences in brightness. If one color in your palette is much brighter or darker than the others (e.g., bright yellow vs. dark blue), it will "pop" and draw the viewer's attention. This implies an importance that doesn't exist in the data, creating a misleading visual hierarchy. Equalizing brightness creates a more balanced, professional aesthetic where all categories receive equal visual weight.

### Core Principle

Visual salience should encode meaning in the data, not be an artifact of arbitrary color choices.

## When it applies

- When using multiple colors to distinguish between discrete categories of equal importance (e.g., in a multi-line chart, a grouped bar chart, or a scatter plot with different colored points).

## Exceptions

- This does not apply to sequential or diverging palettes, where changes in brightness are *intentionally* used to encode quantitative value.
- Do not apply this when you intentionally want to highlight a specific category. In that case, making one color brighter or more saturated is a valid technique.

## Trade-offs

- A palette with perfectly uniform brightness can sometimes feel a bit muted or less vibrant than one with varied brightness.
- It can be more difficult to find a large number of distinct hues that all work well at the same brightness level and maintain high contrast with the background.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Yellow" Problem:** A bright, pure yellow category is far more eye-catching than a dark blue or maroon one in the same chart.
- **Accidental Emphasis:** Your eye is immediately drawn to one specific color, even though that category isn't the focus of the story.
- **Grayscale Test Failure:** When you convert your chart to grayscale, some categories become nearly indistinguishable while others remain distinct, revealing their luminance differences.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** Manually adjust the most obviously problematic color. For example, replace a bright, pure yellow (`#FFFF00`) with a darker, more muted gold or ochre.
- **Moderate approach:** Use a color tool that displays the lightness/luminance value (e.g., in HSL, LCH, or Oklab). Adjust each color in your palette to have a similar lightness value (e.g., aim for all colors to have a Lightness value between 60 and 70 on a scale of 100).
- **Comprehensive approach:** Build your palette from the ground up in a perceptually uniform color space like LCH or Oklab. Fix the Lightness (L) and Chroma (C) values to a desired level, and then pick your colors by choosing evenly spaced Hue (H) angles. This mathematically ensures all colors have the same brightness and saturation.
