## Guidance

When visualizing data about political parties, prefer using established "political colors" that represent ideologies over the official brand colors from party logos or style guides.

## Why

Official party brand colors are designed for marketing and are often not distinct enough for data visualization. Two or more parties may use very similar colors (e.g., different shades of red), making charts and maps confusing. Political colors, which link a color to an ideology (e.g., green for environmentalism, red for socialism), often provide a more distinct and conventional palette that many readers already implicitly understand, reducing their cognitive load.

### Core Principle

Leverage existing conventions and prioritize perceptual distinctness over brand fidelity to ensure clarity. The goal of the chart is to communicate data clearly, not to serve as a brand asset for the entities within it.

## When it applies

-   When visualizing election results, polling data, or any other data categorized by political party.
-   When the parties involved have well-known ideological color associations within that country or region.

## Exceptions

-   If a party's official brand color is both highly unique and strongly associated with it in the public mind (e.g., UKIP's purple in the UK).
-   In contexts where no established political color conventions exist. In such cases, other strategies like arbitrary assignment must be used.

## Trade-offs

-   You sacrifice alignment with a party's official branding, which might be a concern in some contexts.
-   Political color associations can sometimes be ambiguous or contested, requiring an editorial judgment call.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Color Twins:** Two or more distinct parties in your chart share a nearly identical color.
-   **Legend Overload:** Viewers constantly have to refer back to the legend because the color choices are not intuitive or conventional.
-   **Brand Confusion:** The chart uses colors from a party's logo that are not the color the public associates with that party (e.g., using red for the German CDU).

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Research Conventions.** Before choosing colors, do a quick search for how major news outlets in that country represent the political parties. Adopting the existing convention is usually the best choice.
-   **Moderate Approach: Build a Palette from Ideology.** If no strong convention exists, build your own based on widely understood political color meanings. Consult resources like Wikipedia's "Political colour" page for common associations.
-   **Comprehensive Approach: Document and Standardize.** For your organization, create a documented, standardized palette for political parties in the regions you cover. This ensures consistency across all your publications.
