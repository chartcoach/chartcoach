## Guidance

Do not use colors that are stereotypically associated with racial or ethnic groups. This includes avoiding black for Black people, white for white people, yellow for Asian people, and red for Indigenous peoples.

## Why

These color associations are rooted in outdated and often racist historical classification systems. Using them, even unintentionally, can perpetuate harmful stereotypes, reduce complex identities to a single physical trait, and make readers feel disrespected or misrepresented. The goal is to represent people respectfully, not to reinforce historical biases.

### Core Principle

Visual choices should actively work to dismantle stereotypes, not reinforce them. Representing human groups requires a higher degree of care to ensure the visualization is inclusive and respectful.

## When it applies

- When creating any visualization that categorizes data by race or ethnicity, including bar charts, line charts, pie charts, and maps.

## Exceptions

None. While there might be rare, complex narrative reasons to subvert this, the risk of causing harm far outweighs potential benefits. It is safer and more respectful to always avoid this practice.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Direct Mapping:** Your color legend shows a direct and obvious link between a color and a racial stereotype (e.g., a yellow swatch for "Asian").
- **Historical Echoes:** The color choices resemble those found in colonial-era or pseudoscientific "race maps."

## How to Improve

- **Comprehensive approach:** Select a categorical color palette where the colors are abstract and have no connection to physical appearance or historical stereotypes. Use tools like `Viz Palette` or `ColorBrewer` to find a balanced, non-referential set of colors. Assign these colors arbitrarily to the groups in your data.

## Guidance

Avoid using the color white or a very light gray to represent the "White" category in a visualization. Assign it a distinct color with a visual weight comparable to other categories.

## Why

Using white can make this group appear as a neutral default or a background, rather than one group among many. This visually diminishes the presence and importance of other racial groups, unintentionally centering the "White" category as the norm against which others are measured.

### Core Principle

All categories representing groups of people should be given equal visual importance to ensure they are perceived as equally valid subjects of the data story.

## When it applies

- When visualizing data broken down by race, and one of the categories is "White."

## Exceptions

- The Datawrapper article notes a rare, high-risk exception: if the narrative focus is explicitly on how other groups' data compares to or is "catching up" with the white population, using white as a baseline *might* be defensible. However, this is a very advanced and risky choice that should be made with extreme care and clear justification.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Invisible Bar:** The bar, line, or area for the "White" category is colored white and seems to disappear into the chart's background.
- **Default vs. Deviation:** The visualization creates an impression of colored "other" groups being compared against a neutral, un-colored "default" group.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** Change the color from pure white to a light but distinct hue (e.g., a light orange, light blue, or off-white with a colored tint).
- **Comprehensive approach:** Choose a balanced, categorical palette where every color has a similar level of saturation and brightness. Assign one of these colors to the "White" category, ensuring it stands on equal footing with all others.

## Guidance

Refrain from using a palette of browns, olives, beiges, or other colors that resemble human skin tones when representing any racial or ethnic group.

## Why

Using colors that are literal or figurative representations of skin tone is reductive and can be dehumanizing. It boils complex, multifaceted identities down to a single physical attribute, which can make people feel uncomfortable and misrepresented, regardless of their own race.

### Core Principle

Represent people as people, not as their physical characteristics. Visualizations should respect the complexity of human identity.

## When it applies

- When choosing colors for charts that categorize people by race, ethnicity, or any other demographic characteristic.

## Exceptions

- An explicit artistic or narrative project about skin color itself (like Angelica Dass's "Humanae" project, which photographs people against a Pantone color matched to their skin) is a clear exception, as the color is the subject, not just a label. For general data visualization, there are no common exceptions.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Flesh-Tone Palette:** The color palette for your racial or ethnic groups consists of shades of brown, tan, beige, or pink that evoke skin.
- **Audience Discomfort:** Viewers express that the colors make them feel "weird" or "uncomfortable."

## How to Improve

- **Comprehensive approach:** Completely replace the skin-tone palette with an abstract categorical color palette. Choose hues like blues, purples, greens, and oranges that have no common association with skin color. Ensure the chosen colors are distinct and accessible.

## Guidance

When visualizing categories like "Other," "Multiracial," or "Two or more races," assign them a distinct color with the same visual prominence (saturation and brightness) as all other categories. Do not default to using gray.

## Why

Using gray or a faded color visually communicates that this group is less important, an afterthought, or "othered" from the "main" groups. All categories representing people deserve equal respect and visual weight in the design. Graying out a category treats people like footnotes rather than as subjects of the data.

- **Hierarchy of Color:** The color scheme creates a clear visual hierarchy where some groups are vibrant and others are muted, and the muted group is "Other."

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** Replace the gray with a distinct color from your existing palette. If you need another color, choose one that is visually equidistant from the others.
- **Comprehensive approach:** When planning your palette, treat the "Other/Multiracial" category as a first-class citizen. Create a palette with N colors for your N categories, and ensure all have similar visual weight using a tool like `Viz Palette`.

## Guidance

Prefer less saturated (i.e., more muted or pastel) colors over highly saturated (i.e., bright, primary "crayon") colors when creating categories for race and ethnicity.

## Why

Highly saturated colors carry strong and often subconscious emotional connotations. For example, a vibrant red can signal danger, warning, or importance, while a bright green can signal "good" or "go." Applying these emotionally loaded colors to groups of people is inappropriate and risks injecting unintended, biased meaning into the chart. Desaturated colors are more visually neutral and carry less emotional baggage.

## When it applies

- When choosing a color palette for visualizing sensitive demographic data, particularly race and ethnicity.

## Trade-offs

- **Accessibility vs. Neutrality:** Less saturated colors can be harder for all people to distinguish and may fail to provide sufficient contrast for visually impaired users or those with color vision deficiencies. This trade-off must be actively managed.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Crayon Box Palette:** The chart uses a palette of bright, primary colors that feel more appropriate for a children's drawing than for sensitive data.
- **Unintended Alarms:** A specific group is colored with a jarringly bright red or other color with a strong cultural meaning, drawing undue attention or implying a judgment.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** Take your existing palette and reduce the saturation of each color. In most color pickers, this means moving the selector away from the vibrant edge and closer to the gray center.
- **Comprehensive approach:** Design or choose a palette specifically composed of muted, desaturated colors. Crucially, pair this with a strategy to mitigate the accessibility trade-off, such as adding high-contrast outlines to chart elements, using direct labels, or applying patterns.

## Guidance

If using a less saturated or pastel palette, you must verify that the colors are still distinguishable and meet accessibility contrast standards. If they are not, add redundant visual cues to ensure clarity for all users.

## Why

The benefit of using muted colors for emotional neutrality is completely negated if some users cannot perceive the differences between them. An inaccessible chart fails at its primary goal of clear communication. Ensuring distinguishability respects all users, including those with color vision deficiencies (CVD) and low vision.

## When it applies

- Always, but it is especially critical when you have intentionally chosen a desaturated, pastel, or low-contrast color palette.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Squint Test:** If you squint at your chart, different colored categories blur into a single mass.
- **Colorblind Simulation Failure:** Using a CVD simulator reveals that two or more colors become identical.
- **Subtle Neighbors:** The palette includes colors that are very close in hue and brightness (e.g., a light green and a light cyan).

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** Place labels directly on or next to chart elements (bars, lines, slices). This makes color a secondary, rather than primary, channel for identification.
- **Moderate approach: Add High-Contrast Outlines.** Add a darker, saturated, or high-contrast stroke around your desaturated fills. This clearly defines the boundaries of each element even if the fill colors are not easily distinguished.
- **Comprehensive approach: Use Redundant Encoding.** In addition to accessible colors, use another visual channel to differentiate categories. For a line chart, use different stroke styles (solid, dashed, dotted). For a scatter plot, use different shapes (circles, squares, triangles). This provides multiple ways for users to decode the information.

## Guidance

Avoid creating a fixed, reusable color key for racial or ethnic categories that you apply across all your visualizations. Intentionally vary the colors assigned to these groups in different, unrelated projects or publications.

## Why

Consistently using the same color for the same group (e.g., always showing the Hispanic population in pink) can inadvertently create a new, sticky association in your audience's mind. Over time, this can become a de facto standard that carries its own unintended meaning. Shuffling colors forces a deliberate, thoughtful choice each time and prevents the calcification of new color-based stereotypes. It is a check against unconscious bias.

### Core Principle

Avoid creating new visual stereotypes while trying to dismantle old ones. Each visualization is a new context requiring fresh, mindful design choices.

## When it applies

- This is most relevant for organizations (like newsrooms, research institutes) and individuals who frequently publish visualizations on racial and ethnic data.

## Exceptions

- **Within a single context:** You *must* be consistent within a single article, report, dashboard, or presentation. The shuffling should only occur *between* distinct and unrelated projects.

## Trade-offs

- **Cognitive Effort:** It requires more conscious effort for each new project, rather than automatically applying a template.
- **Brand Inconsistency:** It prevents establishing a consistent, brand-level color system for these specific demographic categories, but this is precisely the point—to prioritize ethical representation over rigid branding.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Fixed Style Guide:** Your organization's style guide has a rule like: "Always use purple for Group A, green for Group B, and orange for Group C."
- **Autopilot Coloring:** You find yourself reflexively choosing the same color for the same group in every new chart you make without thinking about it.

## How to Improve

- **Moderate approach:** After completing a project, make a note of the color assignments. When starting the next project, consciously choose a different palette or re-assign the colors from your preferred palette to different groups.
- **Comprehensive approach:** Before starting a new visualization, ask yourself why you are about to pick a certain color for a certain group. If the answer is "because I used it last time," stop and deliberately make a different choice. Use this as a moment to check for any unconscious bias in your color selection process.
