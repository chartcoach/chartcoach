## Guidance

When selecting a palette, use blue as a foundational color for key categories, as it is the most reliably perceived hue across different forms of color vision deficiency.

## Why

Blue is the color that is perceived most consistently by people with normal vision and those with the most common forms of red-green colorblindness (protanopia and deuteranopia). Unlike red and green, which fall on a confusion line for these viewers, blue remains distinctly blue, making it a "safe" and predictable choice.

### Core Principle

Choose colors that are robust to perceptual shifts across different types of vision.

## When it applies

-   When choosing a single highlight color for a visualization.
-   When designing a categorical palette from scratch.
-   When you need to ensure at least one color in your chart is perceived as intended by the widest possible audience.

## Exceptions

-   When blue has a strong, conflicting semantic meaning in the context of your data (e.g., representing "cold" in a chart where that's not the intended message).
-   When designing for audiences with tritanopia (blue-yellow colorblindness), though this is much rarer. In this case, blue can be confused with teal, but it's still often a better choice than red/green combinations.

## Trade-offs

-   Over-reliance on blue can make palettes feel monotonous or generic, especially as the "blue and orange" combination has become a common default for accessibility.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Hue Confusion:** Your chart's primary distinction relies on a green versus red comparison.
-   **Simulator Failure:** When viewed in a red-green colorblindness simulator, two key colors in your chart become nearly indistinguishable.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick approach:** If you have a problematic color pair (like red/green), swap the green for a distinct blue.
-   **Moderate approach:** Re-anchor your color palette around blue. If you need multiple colors, start with blue and choose other colors that have high contrast with it for all viewers (like a vibrant orange).
-   **Comprehensive approach:** Adopt a pre-vetted, colorblind-safe palette that uses blue as a primary color.

## Guidance

For a simple and highly effective two-color palette, combine blue with a complementary orange or red.

## Why

The combination of blue and orange/red leverages colors that fall on different confusion lines for all major types of colorblindness. While red-green blind individuals perceive the orange/red as a shade of olive or brown, it remains clearly distinct from blue. Similarly, blue-blind individuals see the pair as teal and pink, which are also distinct. This makes the pair a robust choice for binary or diverging data.

### Core Principle

Choose colors that are far apart in the color space for all types of color vision deficiencies.

## When it applies

-   When you need a two-color categorical palette.
-   When creating a diverging color scheme to show deviation from a central point.
-   When highlighting a specific category against a baseline.

## Exceptions

-   When brand guidelines strictly prohibit these colors. In this case, you must find another pair that has high contrast in both hue and lightness.
-   When the colors have a strong semantic meaning that conflicts with your data (e.g., using orange to represent something negative when it is culturally associated with warmth or safety).

## Trade-offs

-   This specific combination has become so common as a colorblind-safe default that it can sometimes feel unoriginal.
-   Poorly chosen shades (e.g., a very light orange and a very light blue) might lack sufficient contrast, so lightness must also be considered.

-   **Problematic Pairs:** Your chart relies on red vs. green, or blue vs. purple of similar lightness.
-   **Simulator Failure:** When checking your two-color palette in a colorblindness simulator, the colors merge into a similar shade.

-   **Moderate approach:** Replace your existing two-color palette with a well-tested blue and orange combination, ensuring the two colors also differ in lightness.
-   **Comprehensive approach:** Build a complete color system for your organization based on colorblind-safe principles, with the blue/orange combination as a core option for categorical data.

## Guidance

Design your color palette so that colors differ significantly in lightness (brightness), not just in hue. A reliable test is to see if your visualization remains readable when converted to grayscale.

## Why

People with color vision deficiency, and indeed all people, are very good at perceiving differences in lightness. Even if two colors have hues that are confusing (like red and green), a difference in lightness will keep them distinguishable. This "get it right in black and white" principle also makes your charts robust for monochrome printing and readable in poor lighting conditions.

### Core Principle

Redundantly encode information in both color (hue) and lightness (luminance). The visual system is more sensitive to luminance contrast than to chrominance (hue) contrast.

## When it applies

-   Whenever you use multiple colors to distinguish categories in a chart.
-   When designing a palette that must work for both screen and print.
-   When using adjacent colors that could be confusing, such as red and green.

## Exceptions

-   In sequential color schemes, where a gradient of lightness is already the primary visual variable used to encode quantitative data. Here, the goal is smooth transition, not sharp distinction.

## Trade-offs

-   Finding a set of colors that are both aesthetically pleasing and have strong, distinct lightness values can be challenging. For example, it's hard to find a "light red" that still reads as red and contrasts well with a "dark blue".

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The Squint Test:** If you squint at your chart, do different colored categories blur into a single, muddy patch?
-   **The Grayscale Test:** When you print the chart in black and white or use a grayscale filter, do two or more categories become nearly identical shades of gray?
-   **Subtle Neighbors:** The palette includes colors like a medium-lightness green and a medium-lightness orange, which can easily be confused.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick approach:** Take one of the conflicting colors and use your software's color picker to make it significantly lighter or darker, then check again in grayscale.
-   **Moderate approach:** Manually adjust each color in your palette to ensure a clear "staircase" of lightness values. You can check the lightness value (e.g., the 'L' in HSL) for each color.
-   **Comprehensive approach:** Build your color palette from the ground up using a perceptually uniform color space (like L*C*h) to have precise control over lightness, chroma, and hue. Select colors with deliberately different lightness values.

## Guidance

Restrict the number of distinct colors used to encode categories, ideally to four or fewer. If you have more categories, find ways to group them or use a different chart type.

## Why

The more colors you introduce, the harder it becomes to find a set where every color is easily distinguishable from all others, especially for colorblind viewers. Limiting colors also reduces the cognitive load for all readers, as they don't have to memorize as many color-to-category associations or constantly scan back and forth to a legend.

### Core Principle

Simplify the visual task to reduce cognitive load. Every color added is another piece of information to be processed and remembered.

## When it applies

-   When visualizing categorical data with many levels (e.g., more than 5-7 categories).
-   When a chart appears cluttered or its legend is long and difficult to parse.

## Exceptions

-   When visualizing a large, fixed set of categories is the explicit goal and alternatives are not feasible (e.g., a map showing results for all 27 EU member states). Even in these cases, other techniques like interactivity should be used to aid interpretation.

## Trade-offs

-   To reduce colors, you may need to group smaller categories into an "Other" bucket, which involves a loss of detail.
-   Switching to a chart type that handles more categories better (like a bar chart or small multiples) might take up more space.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Legend Overload:** The color legend for your chart has more than five items and looks like a list.
-   **Rainbow Effect:** The chart uses a multitude of colors that look like a rainbow, making it hard to focus and distinguish individual categories.
-   **Subtle Shades:** The palette relies on multiple similar shades (e.g., teal, light blue, dark blue) to represent different categories.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick approach:** Group smaller or less important categories into a single "Other" category and color it with a neutral gray.
-   **Moderate approach:** Instead of coloring every category, highlight only the one or two most important categories with a distinct color and set all others to gray. This focuses the reader's attention on the main story.
-   **Comprehensive approach:** Reconsider your chart choice. Instead of a single, multi-colored chart (like a pie or stacked bar), use a chart type that handles many categories more gracefully, such as a simple bar chart with labels, a table, or small multiples.

## Guidance

Do not rely on color alone to convey information. Use a second, redundant visual variable—such as shape, pattern, line style, or symbols—to reinforce the distinction between categories.

## Why

If a viewer cannot distinguish between the colors you've chosen, a second visual cue ensures they can still interpret the data correctly. This makes your visualization robust not only for colorblind users, but also for situations like black-and-white printing or low-contrast screens. It provides two independent channels for the brain to decode the information.

### Core Principle

Encode important information redundantly. Never rely on a single visual channel (especially color) for critical distinctions.

## When it applies

-   In any chart where different categories are represented by color.
-   **Scatterplots:** Use different shapes (circle, square, triangle) for different categories.
-   **Line charts:** Use different line styles (solid, dashed, dotted) or widths.
-   **Area charts/Maps:** Use different patterns or textures (hatching, stippling).
-   **Tables:** Use symbols or icons (✔️, ▲) in addition to background color.

## Exceptions

-   In charts with a very large number of categories, it can become impractical to find enough distinct shapes or patterns, leading to clutter. In such cases, interactive filtering or small multiples may be better solutions.

## Trade-offs

-   Adding shapes or patterns can increase visual clutter if not done carefully. The article cautions against a "confetti" look in scatterplots and recommends limiting shapes to three or four.
-   Patterns can sometimes create distracting moiré effects or alter the perceived lightness of the color they fill.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Color-Only:** The only difference between two important data series is their color.
-   **Print Fail:** When you print the chart in black and white, it becomes unreadable.
-   **Ambiguous Overlap:** In a line chart, two similarly-colored lines cross, and it's impossible to tell which is which on the other side.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick approach:** In a line chart, change one of the lines from solid to dashed. In a table, add an icon next to the text.
-   **Moderate approach:** In a scatterplot with 3 categories, assign circle, triangle, and square shapes in addition to color. In a map, add a simple diagonal line pattern to one of two adjacent, similar-colored regions.
-   **Comprehensive approach:** Systematically review all chart types your organization produces and establish a standard of using redundant encoding for all categorical data.

## Guidance

Whenever possible, place labels directly on or next to the data they describe (e.g., on lines, bars, or pie slices) and remove the separate color legend.

## Why

A separate color legend forces the reader to perform a mentally taxing lookup task: find the category in the legend, remember its color, then scan the chart to find that color. This process is slow, inefficient, and a significant barrier for colorblind users who may struggle to match the colors. Direct labels eliminate this work entirely, making the chart faster and easier to understand for everyone.

### Core Principle

Reduce cognitive load by co-locating labels and the data they describe. Don't make your reader work to connect the dots.

## When it applies

-   In most charts with a manageable number of categorical series, particularly line charts, area charts, pie charts, and simple bar charts.
-   When you want to make a chart as easy to read as possible at a glance.

## Exceptions

-   In highly dense charts where there is no clear space for labels without them overlapping (e.g., a scatterplot with thousands of points from multiple categories). In these cases, interactive tooltips are a better solution.
-   On complex maps, where direct labeling can quickly lead to clutter and illegibility.

## Trade-offs

-   Placing labels effectively can be challenging. It may require manual adjustment or sophisticated labeling algorithms to prevent labels from overlapping with each other or with important data points.
-   Direct labels can sometimes make a chart feel more cluttered than a clean chart area with a separate legend, though the usability gain usually outweighs this.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The Back-and-Forth:** You or other viewers have to repeatedly look between the chart and the legend to understand what's being shown.
-   **Legend Ambiguity:** The legend contains two colors that are very similar, making the matching task difficult or impossible for some users.
-   **Orphaned Data:** The chart contains a legend, but it's positioned so far from the data that the connection is weak.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick approach:** In a line chart, add text labels at the end of each line. In a pie chart, add labels next to or inside each slice.
-   **Moderate approach:** Carefully design your chart layout to create space for direct labels. You may need to adjust the axis range or the chart's aspect ratio.
-   **Comprehensive approach:** Use a tool (like Datawrapper) that has built-in, sophisticated algorithms for automated direct labeling, and make it your default method for annotating categorical charts.

## Guidance

For interactive charts, implement hover or click effects that highlight associated elements, providing an unambiguous way for users to identify data series.

## Why

Interaction provides an "escape hatch" for users who struggle to distinguish elements by color alone. By hovering or clicking, a user can isolate a single series, causing it to be highlighted while others fade, or revealing a detailed tooltip. This action temporarily removes the ambiguity caused by similar colors, allowing any user to confidently identify and trace a specific data series.

### Core Principle

Leverage interaction to provide on-demand detail and clarification, helping users resolve ambiguity at their own pace.

## When it applies

-   For any visualization published on the web or in a digital application that uses color to encode categories.
-   Especially useful in charts with many overlapping or closely packed elements, like multi-series line charts or scatterplots.

## Exceptions

-   This guideline does not apply to static visualizations intended for print or formats like PDF and PNG. For static charts, redundant encoding (patterns, shapes) is necessary.
-   The interaction must be accessible via keyboard as well as mouse to support users with motor disabilities.

## Trade-offs

-   Requires additional development time and effort to implement compared to a static chart.
-   Poorly designed hover effects can be distracting or obscure underlying data.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Static Interaction:** An online chart looks and behaves like a static image, with no response to user input like hovering or clicking.
-   **Unhelpful Hover:** Hovering over a chart element does nothing, or only provides a cryptic tooltip (e.g., "series-3: 47.5").
-   **Unlinked Legend:** In an interactive chart with a legend, hovering over the legend items does not affect the main chart area.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick approach:** Implement tooltips that appear on hover, clearly stating the category name and data value for the hovered element.
-   **Moderate approach:** Create a highlight effect where hovering over a data series (or its legend item) makes it more prominent (e.g., thicker line, brighter color, dark stroke) while other series fade into the background.
-   **Comprehensive approach:** Design a fully linked interactive experience. Hovering a legend item highlights the series in the chart. Hovering a point on the chart highlights the point, its series, and its legend item simultaneously. Ensure all interactions are also accessible via keyboard navigation (e.g., tabbing through elements).
