## Guidance

When the main goal is to allow readers to find specific information or individual values, use a table rather than a chart.

## Why

Tables structure data in a way that facilitates direct lookup. Unlike charts, which are optimized for showing patterns and relationships, tables present data in a simple row-and-column format that makes it easy to scan for and retrieve precise values, such as the data for a specific city, company, or date.

### Core Principle

Design for the primary task. If the task is lookup, the design should optimize for finding specific items quickly and accurately.

## When it applies

- When your audience needs to find data points that apply specifically to them (e.g., their location, age group, industry).
- When the data is structured text (names, teams, states) rather than purely quantitative.
- When providing a reference or a directory-like resource.
- When the audience needs to know the exact value of a piece of data.

## Exceptions

- If the table becomes excessively long and the lookup task can be better served by an interactive tool (like a search bar that returns a single result), a full table might not be necessary.
- If identifying an overall pattern is more important than finding a single value, a chart is more appropriate.

## Trade-offs

- Tables are poor at revealing overall trends, patterns, outliers, or the "shape" of the data. By choosing a table, you prioritize precision and lookup over high-level visual discovery.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Constant Questions:** Readers are asking for specific numbers that are not easily read from your chart.
- **"Where am I?":** Users have difficulty finding the data point relevant to their own context in a visualization.
- **Text Clutter:** You have a long paragraph of text that is just listing structured data that would be easier to read in a table format.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** If you have a chart, add a small summary table with key values or provide a link to the full dataset in a table format.
- **Comprehensive Approach:** If the primary task is lookup, replace the chart entirely with a well-formatted table. Ensure columns are clearly labeled and consider adding search or sort functionality to aid lookup.

## Guidance

When the goal is to show the relationship between values, communicate a pattern, or highlight the overall shape of the data, use a chart instead of a table.

## Why

Charts translate numbers into visual objects (lines, bars, points), which allows the human visual system to rapidly perceive patterns, trends, and outliers. A table requires a user to read numbers sequentially and mentally compare them, making it very difficult to see a high-level story.

### Core Principle

Make the most important comparisons and patterns the easiest to see. Charts do this by encoding data visually, which our brains process more efficiently than text.

## When it applies

- When you want to show a trend over time.
- When you want to compare the magnitude of different categories.
- When you want to highlight the biggest or smallest value, or outliers.
- When you want to communicate a key message or story from the data at a glance.

## Exceptions

- If the "pattern" is trivial or the data consists of only two or three numbers, a simple sentence might be more effective than a chart.
- If precise values are more important than the pattern, a table might be a better choice (or a chart with a supplementary table).

## Trade-offs

- Charts sacrifice numerical precision for perceptual clarity. It is difficult to read exact values from most charts.

## Signs of Trouble

- **"So What?":** Viewers look at your table and cannot discern any clear takeaway or story.
- **Hidden Insights:** You find yourself manually pointing out the highest value, the lowest value, or an interesting trend within a large table.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** Add in-cell visualizations to your table, like bar charts or sparklines, to introduce a visual layer.
- **Comprehensive Approach:** Convert the table into an appropriate chart type. Use a line chart for trends, a bar chart for comparisons, or a scatter plot for correlations to make the underlying pattern immediately visible.

When your audience needs precise numbers to make specific decisions, provide the data in a table.

## Why

Charts are excellent for showing relative magnitudes and patterns but poor for communicating precise values. When a user needs to act on the data—such as comparing interest rates, prices, or technical specifications—the exact numbers are critical. A table presents these precise figures clearly, functioning as a tool for decision-making.

- When the data will be used to make financial, business, or personal choices.
- When users need to compare values with high precision.
- When the numbers themselves are the key information, rather than the relationship between them.

- **Moderate Approach:** Supplement your chart with a summary table showing the most important precise values.
- **Comprehensive Approach:** If precision is the top priority, use a table as the primary method of display. You can still use in-table visuals like bar charts to add a comparative layer without sacrificing the numbers.

## Guidance

For data types that people are accustomed to interpreting as raw numbers (like age or standard drink sizes), present them in a table rather than visualizing them.

## Why

Some numerical data comes with strong, built-in mental models and associations. For example, we understand the social and developmental meaning of ages like 6, 18, and 65 in ways that a bar chart cannot convey. Visualizing these numbers as simple magnitudes (e.g., a bar for 18 being three times taller than a bar for 6) can strip them of their conventional meaning and feel awkward or unhelpful.

## When it applies

- When dealing with data like age, where the number itself has a rich, culturally-understood meaning.
- For standardized measurements that are commonly understood as numbers, such as clothing sizes or drink volumes (0.25L, 0.5L).
- When the audience's internal understanding of the number is more nuanced than a simple quantitative comparison.

## Exceptions

- If you are comparing statistical distributions of these values (e.g., the age distribution of a population), a chart like a histogram is perfectly appropriate. The guideline applies to showing individual instances of the data.

## Trade-offs

- You lose the ability to make quick visual comparisons of magnitude, but you gain by aligning with the audience's existing mental framework, which reduces cognitive load.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Awkward Visuals:** A chart of ages or other conventional numbers looks strange or uninformative.
- **"Just tell me the number":** The audience finds the visualization to be a roundabout way of communicating a simple, familiar number.

## How to Improve

- **Action:** In your table, simply display the numbers in a column. For example, instead of creating a bar chart to show the ages of team members, list their ages in a "Age" column.

## Guidance

Use a table when you want to allow readers to compare values both across rows (e.g., different metrics for the same item) and down columns (e.g., the same metric for different items).

## Why

Most chart types are optimized for a single direction of comparison. For example, a grouped bar chart makes it easy to compare categories within a group, but harder to compare the same category across different groups. A table's grid structure naturally supports both horizontal and vertical comparisons, offering more flexibility to the reader.

## When it applies

- When you have a matrix of data where both the rows and columns represent distinct, comparable categories.
- When you cannot easily decide which comparison direction is more important for your audience.
- When you want to provide a flexible data exploration tool for the reader.

## Exceptions

- If there is one primary comparison that is far more important than any other, a chart designed specifically for that comparison will be more effective.

## Trade-offs

- **Increased Cognitive Load:** While offering flexibility, tables force the reader to do the comparative work (mental math, scanning back and forth) themselves.
- **Missed Insights:** Important trends, outliers, or patterns that would be immediately obvious in a chart are likely to be missed in a table.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Chart indecision:** You are struggling to decide whether to group your bar chart by region or by year, because both comparisons seem equally important.
- **Complex needs:** Your audience wants to compare both a company's performance against its competitors this year, and that same company's performance against its own results from last year.

## How to Improve

- **Action:** Structure your data in a simple table with clear row and column headers. This empowers the reader to make their own comparisons as they see fit. For added clarity, consider using a heatmap within the table to guide the eye to high and low values.

## Guidance

When displaying ranks or other strictly ordinal data, use a sorted table rather than a chart that encodes rank as a magnitude (e.g., bar length).

## Why

Rank data is ordinal, not quantitative. The difference between rank 1 and 2 is not necessarily the same as the difference between rank 10 and 11. Using a bar chart to visualize ranks can be misleading because it encourages viewers to interpret the bar lengths quantitatively (e.g., "Rank 1 is twice as good as Rank 2," which is false). A table simply presents the order, which is the true nature of the data.

## When it applies

- When your primary data consists of ranks (1st, 2nd, 3rd...).
- When you want to show a sorted list of items.
- When the underlying quantitative values that determined the rank are not available or not important to show.

## Exceptions

- If you want to show the rank *and* the underlying quantitative value, a bar chart sorted by value (which implicitly shows the rank by order) is an excellent choice. This guideline applies when the rank itself is the data being displayed.

## Trade-offs

- You lose the immediate visual impact of a chart, but you gain logical integrity and avoid misleading your audience.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Misleading Bars:** You have a bar chart where the y-axis is "Rank" and the bars represent Rank 1, Rank 2, etc. This incorrectly represents ordinal data as quantitative.
- **Confused Audience:** Viewers are trying to interpret the *size* of the difference between ranks based on your visualization.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** If you have a bar chart of ranks, change the title and labels to make it clear that the bars simply represent rank order and their length is not a meaningful quantity.
- **Comprehensive Approach:** Replace the chart with a table. Create a column for the rank (e.g., "#") and a column for the item being ranked. Sort the table by the rank column. This is the clearest and most honest way to present rank data.

## Guidance

Keep tables focused by including only essential columns. For the columns that remain, make them as narrow as possible without sacrificing clarity.

## Why

Wide tables with many columns are difficult to read and process. They increase cognitive load as the reader's eye has to travel further, increasing the risk of errors. On smaller screens, wide tables force horizontal scrolling, which is a poor user experience that can cause readers to miss data entirely.

### Core Principle

Reduce the cognitive work required to decode your visualization; every unnecessary piece of information is a potential distraction.

## When it applies

- When designing any table, but especially for display on mobile devices.
- When a table feels cluttered or overwhelming.
- When a table requires horizontal scrolling to view all data.

## Exceptions

- In expert-facing exploratory tools, showing a large number of columns might be desirable to allow for in-depth analysis. In such cases, good filtering and column visibility controls are crucial.

## Trade-offs

- Removing columns means sacrificing some information. You must make a judgment call on what is truly essential for your audience and their task.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Horizontal Scrollbar:** Your table is wider than the screen or page width.
- **Reader Fatigue:** It feels like a chore to find information in the table.
- **Redundant Information:** The same word or unit appears in every cell of a column.
- **Number Clutter:** Numbers are shown with excessive, unnecessary precision (e.g., `1,300,000.00` when `1.3m` would suffice).

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Abbreviate and Round.**
    - Use standard abbreviations (e.g., 'Q1' for 'Quarter 1').
    - Round numbers to fewer decimal places or use compact formats (e.g., `1.3M` instead of `1,300,000`).
    - Use icons instead of text where universally understood (e.g., a country flag).
- **Moderate Redesign: Consolidate Headers.** Move repeating words or units from the cells into the column header. For example, change a column of `$10, $20, $30` to have a header "Price ($)" and cells `10, 20, 30`.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Re-evaluate and Remove.** Critically assess each column. Ask "What decision does this column help the reader make?" If there's no clear answer, consider hiding it by default or removing it entirely. For responsive designs, configure less important columns to be hidden on mobile screens.

## Guidance

Whenever possible, structure your data so that your table has more rows and fewer columns (a "tall" or "long" format).

## Why

Humans find it easier and faster to scan information vertically than horizontally. We are trained to do this by reading lists, dictionaries, and contacts on our phones. A tall, narrow table is easier to skim, especially if sorted, and avoids the usability problems of horizontal scrolling on screens.

## When it applies

- When you have a "wide" dataset (e.g., one row per country, with columns for 2010, 2011, 2012, etc.).
- When designing for screen display, particularly on mobile devices where horizontal space is limited.
- When one of the main tasks is to scan for items in a sorted list.

## Exceptions

- When the number of items (which would become rows) is very small, and the number of attributes (which would become columns) is very large, a wide format might be unavoidable.
- If bidirectional comparison is a key task, a wide format may be necessary.

## Trade-offs

- Transposing data can sometimes feel less intuitive for certain datasets, depending on what the user considers the primary entity.
- It can lead to repetition in some columns (e.g., the entity name being repeated for each year), but this is generally a worthwhile trade-off for improved scannability.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Horizontal Scrolling:** Your table is too wide for the screen.
- **Difficult Comparisons:** It's hard to compare the same metric across many items because you have to scan horizontally across a large distance.

## How to Improve

- **Action: Transpose Your Data.** Restructure your table. For example, instead of a table with columns `Country | 2021 | 2022 | 2023`, transpose it to have columns `Country | Year | Value`. This creates a taller, narrower table that is easier to sort, filter, and read.

For tables with many columns, apply a subtle background color to every other row (known as "zebra striping" or "row banding").

In a wide table, it's easy for the eye to slip and jump to an adjacent row when scanning from a label on the left to a value on the far right. ## Guidance

For very long tables on a web page or article, enable pagination to break the table into shorter, screen-sized chunks.

## Why

A table that is taller than the user's screen can create a "false bottom." Users may see the end of their screen filled with table rows and assume they have reached the end of the article, causing them to miss any content that appears below the table. Pagination prevents this by keeping the table to a manageable height and clearly indicating that more data is available on other "pages."

## When it applies

- When a table is very long (e.g., taller than a typical desktop screen height, roughly 50+ rows).
- When there is important content (text, other charts) that appears after the table in a linear document like a blog post or report.

## Exceptions

- If the primary task requires users to sort the entire dataset or use Ctrl+F to find content, pagination can be a hindrance. An alternative is to contain the table within a vertically scrollable `div` with a fixed height, though this can also have usability drawbacks.
- For print, pagination is handled by the page breaks of the document itself.

## Trade-offs

- Pagination hides most of the data by default, requiring clicks to access.
- It makes it impossible to see all the data at once or to sort the entire dataset globally.

## Signs of Trouble

- **"I didn't see that":** Users are consistently missing the content that comes after your long table.

Set the default sort order of your table based on the most important or interesting column, rather than simply sorting the first column alphabetically.

## Why

## Guidance

Use cell background color (a heatmap) to visualize the magnitude of numbers in a table, providing a space-efficient way to see patterns. Apply color scales consistently and logically.

## Why

A heatmap allows you to add a visual layer to a table without taking up extra space. By mapping value to color intensity, you make it easy to spot high and low values, clusters, and patterns across multiple rows and columns at a glance. Correctly applying color scales is crucial for this to work.

### Core Principle

Visual encodings must be consistent to allow for valid comparisons. The same color should mean the same thing within a comparable set of data.

- When you have one or more columns of comparable numeric data.
- When you want to show patterns and distributions across the table grid.
- When horizontal space is too limited for bar charts.

- A poorly chosen color gradient can obscure patterns or make text unreadable.

- **Apples-to-Oranges:** Two different metrics (e.g., Temperature and Rainfall) are shown using the same blue color gradient, falsely inviting comparison between them.
- **Unreadable Text:** The background color is too dark, making the text on top of it difficult or impossible to read.

## How to Improve

- **Comprehensive Approach: Apply Scales Logically.**

    - **For Different Metrics:** If you have heatmaps for different, non-comparable metrics in the same table (e.g., Approval % and Disapproval %), use **two clearly distinct color palettes** (e.g., a blue scale for one, an orange scale for the other).
