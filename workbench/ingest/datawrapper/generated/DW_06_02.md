## Guidance

Enhance your quantitative color key by integrating a small histogram or density plot that shows the distribution of the data across the color scale.

## Why

A standard color key shows what values the colors represent, but it doesn't show how frequent those values are. By adding a distribution plot, you provide crucial context. The viewer can see if the data is skewed, if there are outliers, and whether a particular color on the map represents a common or a rare event. This prevents readers from making false assumptions based on the amount of area a color occupies on the map.

### Core Principle

Provide context to prevent misinterpretation. The meaning of a data point is often relative to the distribution of all other data points.

## When it applies

- For choropleth maps and heatmaps where the underlying data distribution is not uniform (e.g., it's skewed or has outliers).
- When the frequency of values is as important to the story as the values themselves.
- When you want to help the reader distinguish between a visually dominant but rare value and a visually subtle but common one.

## Exceptions

- **Uniform Distribution:** If the data is uniformly or normally distributed, a histogram may add little extra information and could be omitted for simplicity.
- **Categorical Data:** This technique is designed for quantitative (continuous or binned) data, not categorical data.
- **Simplicity is Paramount:** If the chart is for a very quick glance and the audience needs the simplest possible message, the added complexity of a histogram might be a distraction.

## Trade-offs

- **Increased Complexity:** A key with a histogram is more complex than a standard one and may require a moment longer for the viewer to understand.
- **Implementation Effort:** Creating a legend with an integrated distribution plot is not a standard feature in many charting tools and may require custom design or code.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Nevada Problem:** A viewer looks at a US map of population density and concludes that large, sparsely populated states like Nevada are more significant than they are, simply because their color covers a large area.
- **Misinterpreted Skew:** A map shows income, and the reader assumes the values are evenly spread across the color range, not realizing that the vast majority of areas fall into the lower-income brackets.
- **"Is this value high?":** A viewer points to a medium-colored region and can't tell if that value is typical or unusual relative to the overall dataset.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Use Quantiles.** If you can't add a histogram, bin your data using quantiles (e.g., quintiles or deciles). This ensures each color covers an equal number of data points, which implicitly communicates some information about the distribution. Add a note like "Each color represents 20% of counties."
- **Moderate Approach: Add Descriptive Statistics.** Add text annotations to your legend that summarize the distribution, such as the mean, median, and interquartile range. This gives statistical context without a visual plot.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Integrate a Histogram.** Redesign your legend to include a small histogram or density plot. The x-axis of the histogram should align with the color scale, and the y-axis (height of the bars) should represent the count of data points in that bin.
