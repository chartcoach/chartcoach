## Guidance

Visualize the margin of error when presenting data from samples, such as election polls.

## Why

Polls and other sample-based data are estimates, not exact results. They have an inherent margin of error. Failing to show this uncertainty is misleading, as it presents the data with a false sense of precision. Visualizing the probable range prevents audiences from over-interpreting small differences between categories and provides a more honest representation of the data.

### Core Principle

Communicate uncertainty to foster accurate interpretation and build trust. Data from samples is probabilistic, not deterministic, and visualizations should reflect this reality.

## When it applies

- When visualizing data from statistical samples, like opinion polls, surveys, or scientific measurements where uncertainty is a known factor.
- This is especially critical in close races or comparisons where the margins of error for different competitors overlap.

## Exceptions

- When the margin of error is negligible or not available (though its absence should be noted).
- For very simple charts aimed at audiences where the concept might be too complex and cannot be explained, though it's generally better to educate than to oversimplify.

## Trade-offs

- Adds visual complexity to the chart, which can increase cognitive load.
- May require a brief explanation of what "margin of error" means for audiences unfamiliar with the concept.

## Signs of Trouble

- **False Precision:** A chart presents poll numbers as single, exact values (e.g., a bar ending precisely at "29%") without any indication of the underlying uncertainty.
- **Over-Interpreted Gaps:** Small differences between bars or points are reported as significant "leads" or "gaps," when in reality the values fall within each other's margin of error.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add a Note.** In the chart's description or subtitle, state the margin of error (e.g., "Source: XYZ Poll, n=1,500, margin of error: +/- 3 percentage points").
- **Moderate Redesign: Use Error Bars.** On bar charts or dot plots, add error bars or range plots to visually represent the confidence interval for each category. This makes the uncertainty an integral part of the visual.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Show the Distribution.** For a more statistically robust representation, use advanced visualizations like gradient plots or quantile dot plots to show the full probability distribution of the estimate, rather than just the hard endpoints of a confidence interval.

## Guidance

Display data from multiple pollsters simultaneously instead of relying on a single source.

## Why

Different polling organizations may have varying methodologies or inherent biases ("house effects") that lead to consistently different results. Any single poll can also be a statistical outlier due to random chance. Showing results from several polls provides a more complete and reliable picture, highlighting both the general consensus and the range of disagreement among pollsters.

### Core Principle

Aggregate evidence to provide a more robust and stable signal. Avoid drawing strong conclusions from a single, potentially noisy, data point.

## When it applies

- When reporting on topics where multiple sources of sampled data exist, such as election polling, economic forecasts, or public opinion on a specific issue.
- When there is known variation or methodological disagreement between data sources.

## Exceptions

- If only one reliable poll or data source is available.
- If the goal is to specifically analyze the methodology or results of a single, particular poll, in which case this context should be made clear.

## Trade-offs

- Increases information density and cognitive load for the viewer.
- Can be more labor-intensive to gather, clean, and aggregate data from multiple sources.
- May be confusing if polls differ wildly without any explanation for the variance.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Cherry-Picking:** A report focuses exclusively on one poll's results, especially if that poll supports a particular narrative, while ignoring other available polls with different findings.
- **Single-Source Syndrome:** All reporting on a topic is based on a single recurring poll, presented as definitive truth without acknowledging the existence or results of other pollsters.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Contextual Text.** In the text accompanying a single-poll chart, mention the results of other recent polls to provide context (e.g., "While this poll shows a 5-point lead, other polls this week have shown a lead between 2 and 7 points.").
- **Moderate Redesign: Use a Comparison Table.** Create a table that lists the results from multiple pollsters for each party/candidate. This allows for easy lookup and comparison. The example from the article uses split bars within a table.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Visualize the Distribution.** Use a dot plot where each dot represents one pollster's result for a given candidate, showing the full distribution of poll results. Alternatively, calculate and visualize a polling average, while still showing the individual polls that contribute to it.

## Guidance

Visualize polling data over time using a line chart to reveal trends and momentum.

## Why

A single poll is just a static snapshot. Public opinion is dynamic, changing in response to news and campaign events. A line chart is the most effective way to show the trajectory of support, providing crucial context about momentum, volatility, and the impact of events. This shifts the narrative from "who is ahead now" to "how has the race evolved."

### Core Principle

For time-series data, visualize the trend. The story is often in the change over time, not in a single point in time.

## When it applies

- When you have time-series data, such as multiple polls conducted over a period of weeks, months, or years.
- When the goal is to understand trends, momentum, or the impact of events on the data.

## Exceptions

- When the goal is simply to show the result of a single, specific poll (e.g., an exit poll on election day), although even then, comparison to a pre-election poll can be valuable.
- When there are only two or three time points, a slope chart or dot plot might be clearer than a full line chart.

## Trade-offs

- Can become cluttered and hard to read if there are too many overlapping lines (a "spaghetti chart").
- Requires access to and processing of historical data, which can be more work than showing a single poll.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Snapshot Fixation:** Reporting focuses solely on the latest poll results, presented in a static format like a bar or pie chart, with no historical context.
- **Lost Narrative:** A major campaign event is reported, but the accompanying visualization doesn't show its potential impact on the polls in the following days or weeks. The data and the story are disconnected.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Trend Context.** Alongside a chart of the latest poll, include text describing the trend (e.g., "Party X is up 3 points from last week and has been steadily rising for a month").
- **Moderate Redesign: Create a Line Chart.** Convert the polling data into a line chart showing the polling average for each major party/candidate over the course of the campaign.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use an Annotated Line Chart.** Create a line chart and use annotations, labels, and shaded regions to mark key political events (e.g., debates, scandals, policy announcements). This directly connects the data's movement to the real-world narrative, explaining *why* trends might be changing.

## Guidance

Add text annotations to your chart to highlight key points and explain them in the context of real-world events.

## Why

Data doesn't exist in a vacuum. Annotations provide crucial context, bridging the gap between *what* the data shows and *why* it might show it. They guide the reader's attention to important features (like peaks, troughs, or inflection points) and offer explanations, transforming the chart from a passive display of numbers into an active, explanatory story.

### Core Principle

Don't just show data; explain it. Guide your audience to the key insights and provide the context needed for understanding.

## When it applies

- On any chart where external events can influence the data, especially time-series charts (line, area) and scatter plots.
- When you want to draw attention to specific outliers, clusters, or points of interest and explain their significance.

## Exceptions

- In purely exploratory visualizations where the goal is for the user to discover their own patterns without being led.
- If the chart is already extremely dense, adding annotations might create too much visual clutter. In this case, the explanation may need to live in the surrounding text.

## Trade-offs

- Can add clutter if overused or poorly placed.
- Requires editorial judgment to decide which events are significant enough to annotate, introducing a potential for author bias.
- Well-placed annotations can take time to design and compose.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "So What?" Chart:** A chart shows a dramatic change, a sharp spike, or a clear outlier, but provides no explanation, leaving the viewer wondering, "So what happened there?"
- **Reader Guesswork:** Viewers are left to speculate about the causes of trends or outliers on their own, potentially arriving at incorrect conclusions. The chart raises more questions than it answers.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Use an Explanatory Title.** Craft a title or subtitle that explains the main event or takeaway shown in the chart (e.g., "Support for Party X surged after the leader's debate").
- **Moderate Redesign: Add Direct Annotations.** Add one or two annotations with lines pointing to the most significant features of the chart. Briefly explain their context (e.g., "New policy announced," "Competitor enters market").
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Create a Narrative Chart.** Build a richly annotated chart that walks the reader through the data's story. Use a combination of pointers, text labels, and shaded time periods to explain multiple key events and their apparent impact on the data, creating a guided analytical experience.
