## Guidance

In visualizations comparing racial groups or world regions, avoid the default choice of using the color blue exclusively for "White" people or for "Europe."

## Why

In many Western and global business contexts, the color blue carries strong positive associations: trust, authority, professionalism, stability, and even royalty. When this culturally loaded color is assigned to a historically dominant group, it can unintentionally reinforce stereotypes of superiority and centrism. Being conscious of this association allows for more equitable color choices.

## When it applies

- When choosing categorical colors for data on race, ethnicity, or world regions, especially for a general or international audience.

## Exceptions

- If blue is a non-negotiable brand color that must be used.
- If the visualization uses multiple different shades of blue to represent many different categories, which dilutes the special status of any single blue.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Default Blue:** In a multi-group chart, the "White" or "European" category is colored with a standard, corporate-style blue while other groups are not.
- **Unintended Endorsement:** The visualization appears to subtly favor or grant authority to one group over others through a culturally powerful color choice.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** Shift the blue towards a different hue like turquoise or purple to lessen the "authoritative blue" association.
- **Moderate approach:** Intentionally assign the color blue to a non-dominant or historically marginalized racial or regional category in your visualization.
- **Comprehensive approach:** Opt for a balanced color palette that avoids using a single, primary blue altogether, instead relying on other hues like green, purple, orange, and magenta to create distinctions.
