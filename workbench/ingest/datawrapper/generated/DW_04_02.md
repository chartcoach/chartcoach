## Guidance

Use a diverging color scheme for quantitative data that has a meaningful midpoint. The scheme should use two distinct hues that diverge from a light, neutral central color.

## Why

A diverging color scheme is designed to draw attention to both ends of the data range—the low and high extremes—equally. The neutral midpoint signifies a break or a zero point in the data. This visual structure naturally maps to data like profit/loss, positive/negative change, or a split between two opposing groups (e.g., political parties), making the visualization intuitive to read.

### Core Principle

The visual structure of the encoding should match the conceptual structure of the data. Diverging data has two directions from a central point, and a diverging color scheme mirrors this structure.

## When it applies

-   Your data has a critical, meaningful midpoint, such as zero (e.g., population growth/decline, temperature change).
-   You are visualizing a balance or margin between two opposing groups (e.g., vote share difference between two parties).
-   You want to show values that are above or below a significant threshold, like an average or a baseline.

## Exceptions

-   If your data ranges from low to high and does not have a meaningful midpoint (e.g., unemployment rate from 0% to 100%), a sequential color scheme is more appropriate. Using a diverging scheme in this case would create a confusing and arbitrary visual break.

## Trade-offs

-   **Pro:** Clearly and intuitively communicates deviation from a central value in two directions.
-   **Con:** Can be less effective at showing fine-grained differences among values on the same side of the midpoint compared to a sequential scheme that uses its entire range for one direction.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Arbitrary Midpoint:** You're using a diverging palette for data like total population or life expectancy, forcing a visual break where no conceptual break exists.
-   **Unbalanced Lightness:** The two hues used in the scheme have different lightness profiles, causing one side of the data to visually dominate the other.
-   **Vibrant Center:** The color at the midpoint is bright and saturated instead of light and neutral, drawing attention to the least interesting part of the data.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Center on the Midpoint.** Ensure your data is centered on the meaningful value (e.g., 0 for change, 50% for a two-party vote share).
-   **Moderate Redesign: Choose a Proper Palette.** Select a palette with two distinct hues (e.g., blue and red, or purple and green) that meet at a light, neutral color (like light gray, white, or pale yellow). The colors at the extremes should be the darkest and most saturated. Use a tool like ColorBrewer to find a balanced, colorblind-safe palette.
-   **Comprehensive Approach: Clarify in Text.** In your title, description, and legend, explicitly state what the midpoint and the two extremes represent to ensure correct interpretation. The legend should clearly label the center value (e.g., "0").
