## Guidance

When categories have a natural hierarchy, use a single color for each parent group and use thin strokes or borders to distinguish the sub-categories within that group.

## Why

This technique visually organizes your data according to its inherent structure. It reduces the total number of colors needed by assigning them at the group level, not the individual level. This creates a cleaner chart that allows a viewer to make comparisons on two levels: comparing the overall groups (by color) and seeing the breakdown of sub-categories within each group.

## When it applies

- When your categorical data has a natural parent-child relationship (e.g., Continents and Countries; Departments and Employees).
- In part-to-whole charts like stacked bars or treemaps.
- When you want to show both the total for a group and the composition of that total.

## Exceptions

- When your categories have no logical hierarchical grouping.
- When the chart is rendered at a very small size where the separating strokes would become invisible or create messy visual noise.

## Trade-offs

- **Gained:** A clear visual hierarchy; a reduction in the number of colors; ability to see group totals and their composition simultaneously.
- **Sacrificed:** Direct comparability of sub-categories across different parent groups is harder (e.g., comparing a specific "blue" product to a specific "green" product).

## Signs of Trouble

- **Hidden Structure:** Your chart has many categories that could be logically grouped (e.g., North America, Central America, South America), but they are all assigned disparate, unrelated colors.

## How to Improve

- **Moderate Redesign: Color by Group, Divide by Stroke.**
  1.  Identify the hierarchical structure in your data.
  2.  Assign a single, distinct color to each parent category.
  3.  In your chart, apply the parent color to all of its child sub-categories.
  4.  Add a thin, light-colored (often white) stroke or border around each sub-category element to keep them visually separate.
