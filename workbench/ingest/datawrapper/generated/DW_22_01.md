## Guidance

When displaying numbers in columns—such as in a table, on a chart's axis, or as data labels—always use tabular figures.

## Why

Tabular figures (also called monospaced numerals) ensure that every digit (0-9) occupies the same horizontal width. This vertically aligns numbers in a column, making it easy for the eye to scan up and down to compare magnitudes. The alternative, proportional figures, have variable widths (e.g., '1' is narrower than '8'), which causes columns of numbers to look ragged and misaligned, hindering quick and accurate comparison.

### Core Principle

Make valid comparisons easy. Alignment is a powerful perceptual cue, and by aligning numbers vertically, you reduce the cognitive effort required to compare them.

## When it applies

-   In any data table.
-   For labels on a quantitative axis (typically the y-axis).
-   For data labels placed directly on or next to chart elements (e.g., values at the end of bars).
-   Any time numbers are listed vertically and intended to be compared.

## Exceptions

-   **Numbers in running text:** For numbers appearing within a sentence or paragraph (e.g., "Sales increased by 1,200 units in 2023."), proportional figures are often preferred as they fit more naturally and harmoniously with the surrounding text. Tabular figures can create awkward spacing in this context.

## Trade-offs

-   **Aesthetics in prose:** Using a font that only has tabular figures can make numbers look slightly odd when embedded in paragraphs, as they might not space as nicely as proportional figures. This is a minor trade-off, and most modern fonts designed for data or UI offer both styles.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The "Wobbly Column":** When you look at a column of numbers, the decimal points don't line up, and the digits seem to jitter left and right.
-   **Slow Comparison:** It takes you a moment longer than it should to determine which of two numbers in a table is larger.
-   **Unprofessional Look:** The table or chart labels look slightly messy and disorderly, even if you can't pinpoint why.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix:** Check if your font has a tabular option. Many system fonts do. If not, switch to a data-friendly font that does, such as Lato, Inter, Fira Sans, or PT Sans.
-   **Moderate Approach (Web):** If you are working on the web, activate the OpenType feature for tabular figures using CSS. Apply the rule `font-feature-settings: 'tnum' on;` to the elements containing your numerical data.
-   **Comprehensive Approach (Design Systems):** In your design system or style guide, explicitly define that all components used for displaying columnar data (tables, charts) must use fonts with tabular figures enabled by default. Document this as a core principle for data presentation.
