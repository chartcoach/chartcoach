## Guidance

Use a bright, saturated color and/or a heavier weight for the most important data series in your chart. Relegate secondary or contextual data to muted colors, such as light grey.

## Why

Not all data in a chart is equally important. By making the primary data series visually distinct, you create a clear visual hierarchy. This acts as a signpost, immediately directing the reader's attention to the most important part of the story. Using muted colors like grey for context allows it to be available for inspection without competing for attention.

### Core Principle

Emphasize the most important data; de-emphasize the rest. Make the most critical comparisons the easiest ones to see.

## When it applies

- Any time a chart includes both primary data (the focus of the story) and contextual data (e.g., historical values, averages, benchmarks, competitor performance).
- This is especially effective in line charts, scatter plots, and bar charts.

## Exceptions

- When all data series are of equal importance, and the goal is to allow the user to freely compare them all. In this case, a palette of distinct, perceptually uniform colors is more appropriate.

## Trade-offs

- **Editorial Stance:** This technique is inherently editorial. By emphasizing certain data, you are guiding a specific interpretation, which may not be appropriate for all contexts.
- **Inspectability:** De-emphasized data can be harder to inspect in detail if a reader happens to be interested in the context rather than the main story.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Rainbow Effect:** Every line, bar, or point is a different, bright color, creating visual noise where everything competes for attention and nothing stands out.
- **"Where do I look?":** The viewer's eye darts around the chart, unsure of where to focus first because all visual elements have equal prominence.
- **Accidental Emphasis:** A color choice unintentionally draws attention to the least important part of the chart simply because it is the most salient.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Go grey.** Change all contextual or secondary data series to a light grey color. This will immediately make your primary data series (in any color) stand out.

- **Moderate Fix: Define a hierarchy.** Choose one bold, saturated color for your primary data series. If you have a secondary series of importance (like an average), use a less saturated but still distinct color. Make all other contextual data grey.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use multiple channels.** Combine color with other visual channels to reinforce the hierarchy. For example, make the primary line thicker and solid, the secondary line normal weight and solid, and the contextual lines thin and/or dashed.
