## Guidance

When visualizing data about political parties, prefer using established "political colors" that represent ideologies over the official brand colors from party logos or style guides.

## Why

Official party brand colors are designed for marketing and are often not distinct enough for data visualization. Two or more parties may use very similar colors (e.g., different shades of red), making charts and maps confusing. Political colors, which link a color to an ideology (e.g., green for environmentalism, red for socialism), often provide a more distinct and conventional palette that many readers already implicitly understand, reducing their cognitive load.

### Core Principle

Leverage existing conventions and prioritize perceptual distinctness over brand fidelity to ensure clarity. The goal of the chart is to communicate data clearly, not to serve as a brand asset for the entities within it.

## When it applies

-   When visualizing election results, polling data, or any other data categorized by political party.
-   When the parties involved have well-known ideological color associations within that country or region.

## Exceptions

-   If a party's official brand color is both highly unique and strongly associated with it in the public mind (e.g., UKIP's purple in the UK).
-   In contexts where no established political color conventions exist. In such cases, other strategies like arbitrary assignment must be used.

## Trade-offs

-   You sacrifice alignment with a party's official branding, which might be a concern in some contexts.
-   Political color associations can sometimes be ambiguous or contested, requiring an editorial judgment call.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Color Twins:** Two or more distinct parties in your chart share a nearly identical color.
-   **Legend Overload:** Viewers constantly have to refer back to the legend because the color choices are not intuitive or conventional.
-   **Brand Confusion:** The chart uses colors from a party's logo that are not the color the public associates with that party (e.g., using red for the German CDU).

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Research Conventions.** Before choosing colors, do a quick search for how major news outlets in that country represent the political parties. Adopting the existing convention is usually the best choice.
-   **Moderate Approach: Build a Palette from Ideology.** If no strong convention exists, build your own based on widely understood political color meanings. Consult resources like Wikipedia's "Political colour" page for common associations.
-   **Comprehensive Approach: Document and Standardize.** For your organization, create a documented, standardized palette for political parties in the regions you cover. This ensures consistency across all your publications.

## Guidance

Maintain a consistent color-to-party mapping across all charts, maps, and articles within a single report, dashboard, or publication. Strive for consistency with broader media conventions where they exist.

## Why

Readers learn color associations quickly. When they see a color used for a party in one chart, they expect it to mean the same thing in the next. Inconsistency breaks this expectation, forcing them to stop, re-read the legend, and increase their cognitive effort. Over time, consistency builds a powerful visual shorthand that makes visualizations faster and easier to understand.

### Core Principle

Don't make your audience think more than they have to. A consistent visual language allows viewers to focus on understanding the data's message, not on decoding the visualization itself.

## When it applies

-   When creating a series of charts or a dashboard about an election or political topic.
-   When publishing multiple articles over time about the same political landscape.
-   In any situation where a reader will encounter multiple visualizations about the same set of categories.

## Exceptions

-   A convention may need to be updated if a new major party emerges whose color conflicts with an existing one, or if a previously used color is no longer appropriate. Such changes should be made deliberately and applied consistently going forward.

## Trade-offs

-   Sticking to a convention might mean using a color that isn't ideal from a purely aesthetic or perceptual standpoint. However, the cognitive benefits of consistency usually outweigh minor aesthetic drawbacks.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Internal Inconsistency:** Your own publication uses different colors for the same party in different articles or even on the same page.
-   **Reader Feedback:** You receive comments from readers who are confused about which color represents which party.
-   **The Double-Take:** You find yourself doing a double-take when looking at your own charts because a color doesn't match your expectation for that party.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Audit and Align.** Before publishing, check all charts in the series to ensure the color mapping is identical.
-   **Moderate Approach: Follow the Leader.** Research the color conventions used by major, reputable news organizations covering that political region and adopt their system.
-   **Comprehensive Approach: Create a Style Guide.** For your organization, create and maintain a simple style guide that specifies the official colors for political parties in the jurisdictions you cover. Make it an accessible internal resource for all content creators.

## Guidance

Ensure that the colors in a categorical palette are perceptually distinct, meaning an average viewer can easily and unambiguously tell them apart, even when they are used for small chart elements.

## Why

The entire purpose of using color to encode categories is to allow viewers to differentiate them. If colors are too similar, the encoding fails. Viewers will struggle to match the data marks to the legend, leading to misinterpretation, frustration, and a higher cognitive load. This problem is magnified for small marks (like dots in a scatterplot) and for viewers with color vision deficiencies.

### Core Principle

Each color in a categorical palette should be an unambiguous representative of its category. If there's any doubt about which category a color represents, the palette is not effective.

## When it applies

-   Any time you use color to represent discrete categories (e.g., parties, countries, product types).
-   Especially critical when chart elements are small (dots, thin lines) or when there are many categories.

## Exceptions

-   When using different shades of a single hue to represent an ordered sequence or magnitude. In that case, the colors are *meant* to be related, not distinct in the same way as a categorical palette.

## Trade-offs

-   A highly discriminable palette may not always be the most aesthetically harmonious. Prioritizing clarity over pure aesthetics is usually the correct trade-off for data visualization.
-   Finding a large number of truly distinct colors (> 8-10) is extremely difficult.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The Squint Test:** If you squint at your chart, do different colored categories blur into a single visual group?
-   **Subtle Neighbors:** Does your palette contain two very similar colors (e.g., a light green and a lime green, or a magenta and a purple)?
-   **Colorblind Simulation Failure:** When you check your chart with a colorblindness simulator (like in Viz Palette), do two or more colors become nearly identical?
-   **Back-and-Forth:** Do you find yourself repeatedly glancing between a chart element and the legend to be sure which category it is?

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Use a Pre-built, Tested Palette.** Instead of picking colors by hand, use a color palette designed for data visualization, such as those in Datawrapper, ColorBrewer, or Tableau. These are built to maximize perceptual distance.
-   **Moderate Approach: Check and Tweak.** Use a tool like Viz Palette to test your current color choices. If two colors are too similar, adjust the hue, saturation, or lightness of one of them until they are clearly distinct, both for standard vision and in simulations.
-   **Comprehensive Approach: Encode with Something Else.** If you have too many categories for color to be effective (>10), don't force it. Recode the data using a different visual channel. For example, use small multiples (a grid of charts) or use direct labels and a neutral color instead of relying on a complex color legend.

## Guidance

Intentionally use color saturation and lightness to create a visual hierarchy that guides the reader's attention. Assign more saturated, vibrant colors to more important categories and more muted, desaturated colors to less important ones.

## Why

Saturated, bright, and dark colors have more "visual weight" — they naturally draw the human eye more than pale, muted, or gray colors. This perceptual phenomenon can be harnessed to guide your audience's attention. By making the most important data points the most visually prominent, you can help readers process the information more efficiently and focus on the main story. Conversely, de-emphasizing less critical data (like "Other" categories) reduces clutter.

### Core Principle

Visual hierarchy should match information hierarchy. Make the most important elements look the most important.

## When it applies

-   When visualizing categories that have a clear hierarchy of importance (e.g., major vs. minor parties, top competitors vs. the rest of the market).
-   When you want to draw attention to a specific category or finding.
-   When dealing with an "Other" category that needs to be shown but de-emphasized.

## Exceptions

-   In purely exploratory visualizations where you don't want to impose a pre-determined hierarchy and want all categories to have equal visual weight.
-   When all categories are considered equally important.

## Trade-offs

-   This is an explicit act of editorializing. You are using visual properties to impose your judgment of what is important onto the data. This is a powerful storytelling technique, but it can be perceived as biased if not done thoughtfully and transparently.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Misleading Emphasis:** A minor data point is the most eye-catching element in your chart due to a poor color choice.
-   **Visual Chaos:** All colors are highly saturated, creating a visually noisy and overwhelming experience with no clear focal point.
-   **Lost in the Gray:** The most important finding is rendered in a pale, muted color and is easily overlooked.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Mute the 'Other'.** If you have an "Other" or "Miscellaneous" category, always assign it a neutral, low-saturation color like light gray. This is the single easiest way to start building visual hierarchy.
-   **Moderate Approach: Identify and Emphasize.** Decide on the one or two most important categories in your chart. Keep their colors bright and saturated, but convert the colors for all other categories to more muted or desaturated versions.
-   **Comprehensive Approach: Create Tiered Palettes.** Develop a color system with different tiers of emphasis. For example, a "main" palette of saturated colors for primary subjects, a "secondary" palette of muted colors for context, and a gray for non-data elements or de-emphasized categories.

## Guidance

When visualizing a large number of categories (e.g., more than 8-10) that lack established color conventions, use a "confetti" or "bonbon" palette composed of a wide variety of perceptually distinct hues.

## Why

It is perceptually impossible to create a large set of colors that are all both semantically meaningful and clearly distinct. When the number of categories is high, the primary goal must shift from meaning to pure differentiability. A confetti palette abandons the search for meaning and instead uses the full spectrum of hues to ensure that each category has a unique visual identifier. The color choices are arbitrary, but they are effective because they are distinct.

## When it applies

-   Visualizing data with high-cardinality categorical variables (e.g., >10 unique values).
-   Situations like primary elections with many candidates, detailed survey results with many response options, or product breakdowns with numerous sub-categories.
-   When no pre-existing color conventions or logical groupings exist for the categories.

## Exceptions

-   If the many categories can be logically grouped into a smaller number of super-groups, it may be better to use a few base hues and vary the lightness/saturation within each group to show the hierarchy (e.g., different shades of blue for 5 related products, different shades of green for another 4).

## Trade-offs

-   The color assignments are arbitrary and have no semantic meaning, so viewers must rely entirely on the legend to decode the chart.
-   Can result in a very colorful, almost chaotic aesthetic that may not be appropriate for all contexts.
-   Finding more than about 20 truly distinct colors is a significant challenge even for automated tools.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Color Overload:** The sheer number of colors makes the chart feel overwhelming and difficult to read.
-   **Legend Hunt:** The legend is so long that it becomes a chore for the user to find the category they are looking for.
-   **Lookalikes Emerge:** In your palette of 15 colors, you accidentally have a 'sky blue' and a 'light blue' that are nearly impossible to tell apart on the actual chart.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Use an Automated Palette Generator.** Don't try to pick 15 distinct colors by hand. Use a tool like Colorgorical or IWantHue designed specifically for generating large, perceptually-distant palettes.
-   **Moderate Approach: Reduce Categories.** Question if you really need to show every single category. Can you group smaller categories into an "Other" bucket? Prioritizing and simplifying the data is often more effective than finding a perfect 20-color palette.
-   **Comprehensive Approach: Don't Use Color to Categorize.** For very high cardinality, color is often the wrong tool. Switch to a different chart type that doesn't rely on a color legend. A simple sorted bar chart with direct labels is far more effective for comparing 50 categories than a pie chart with 50 colored slices. Use small multiples to break the data down into manageable chunks.
