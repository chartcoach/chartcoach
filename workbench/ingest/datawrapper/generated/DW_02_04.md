## Guidance

Include a descriptive subtitle or text block that clearly explains what the data shows, including units, geographic scope, and any important definitions or limitations.

## Why

Your readers have not been part of your data exploration process and lack your intimate knowledge of the dataset. A precise description or subtitle orients them, preventing misinterpretation. It clarifies what is being measured (e.g., sales in millions), the scope (e.g., worldwide), and any caveats (e.g., "selected products," "fiscal quarter"), which builds trust and ensures accurate understanding. This text is also critical for accessibility, as it provides context for screen reader users.

## When it applies

-   Always. Every chart needs to explain what its axes and visual marks represent in unambiguous terms.

## Trade-offs

-   Adding text can increase the visual density of the chart. However, the gain in clarity and accuracy almost always outweighs the cost of a slightly busier visual.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Ambiguous Axes:** Axis labels are single words like "Sales" or "Amount" without units ($, £, millions, etc.).
-   **Unclear Scope:** The reader cannot tell if the data is local, national, or global, or over what precise time period it was collected.
-   **Assumed Knowledge:** The chart relies on the reader knowing that "Q1" means "fiscal quarter" or that the data only includes certain products.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix:** Below your headline, add a sentence that concisely answers the key "W" questions: **What** is being measured? What are the **units**? **Where** is the scope (geography)? **When** is the scope (timeframe)? **Which** items are included (if it's a selection)?
