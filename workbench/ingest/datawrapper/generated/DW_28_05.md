## Guidance

When using small multiples to show how items change or rank across different conditions or time periods, assign a stable, distinct hue to each item. Maintain that color-to-item mapping across all charts in the series.

## Why

This technique turns color into a persistent visual label for each entity (e.g., "France is always green"). It allows the viewer to visually track an item's trajectory across the charts without having to re-read labels in each panel. This offloads the cognitive work of identification, freeing up the viewer's mental resources to focus on interpreting the pattern of change. This is a deliberate and powerful exception to the rule of using sequential colors for ranked data.

## When it applies

- Specifically for small multiples (also called facets or trellis plots) where the primary task is to compare an entity to itself across different contexts or to track its relative position over time.
- Common in line charts showing trends for multiple categories, or grouped bar charts across time.

## Exceptions

- If the primary task is to analyze the distribution *within* each small multiple, and tracking a single entity is secondary, then using a consistent sequential scale in each chart (e.g., dark blue is always #1 rank) would be better. The choice depends on what comparison you want to make easiest.

## Trade-offs

- You lose the ability to quickly see the rank of an item *within* a single chart based on its color. A green bar could be rank #1 in the first chart and rank #5 in the second. The viewer must use position (the y-axis) to judge rank within a chart. This approach prioritizes cross-chart comparison over within-chart comparison.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Recoding Hell:** Your viewer complains, "I keep losing track of which line is which" when looking at a series of charts. They have to repeatedly look back and forth between the legend and the data across multiple panels.
- **Color Meaning Changes:** The color red means "Highest" in Chart 1, but "Third Highest" in Chart 2, because the item that was highest has changed. This inconsistent meaning for the color encoding is a sign of high cognitive load.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Assign Stable Hues.** Create a consistent mapping: one category, one color. Apply this palette consistently across all your small multiples.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Combine Stable Hues with Highlighting.** Assign stable hues to all items, but make the key item(s) of interest more saturated while desaturating the others. This gives you both stable tracking and a clear focal point.
