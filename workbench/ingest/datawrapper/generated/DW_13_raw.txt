## Guidance

Use a diverging color scale (e.g., blue-white-red) when your quantitative data has a meaningful central value that you want to emphasize.

## Why

A diverging scale visually splits the data into two groups on either side of a central midpoint. This helps viewers quickly distinguish values that are above the midpoint from those below it, making the structure of the data immediately apparent and highlighting deviations from the center.

### Core Principle

Visual encodings should match the structure of the data. If the data has a meaningful dividing point, the visualization should make that dividing point visually obvious.

## When it applies

- When there is a natural zero point (e.g., positive vs. negative temperature, profit vs. loss).
- When comparing against a 50% threshold (e.g., election results between two parties).
- When showing deviations from an average, median, or target value.
- When highlighting values above and below an important, agreed-upon threshold (e.g., poverty line, passing grade).

## Exceptions

If the midpoint is arbitrary and not meaningful to the story, or if the primary narrative is about the overall magnitude from low to high (e.g., total population), a sequential scale is likely more appropriate and intuitive.

## Trade-offs

Diverging scales are less intuitive than sequential "darker-is-more" scales. They always require a clear legend to explain the meaning of the two different hues, which increases cognitive load compared to a simple sequential scale.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Lost Midpoint:** You are using a single-hue sequential scale (e.g., light blue to dark blue) to represent data that has a clear "good/bad" or "above/below" split, like profit and loss. Viewers can't easily spot the zero-crossing point.
- **Forced Interpretation:** Viewers have to carefully inspect the values on the axis or in tooltips to figure out which items are above or below the central threshold.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add a Reference Line.** If you must stick with a sequential scale, add a prominent reference line or annotation to your chart to explicitly mark the midpoint.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Switch to Diverging.** Change your color scale from sequential to diverging. Center the neutral, lightest color (e.g., white, light gray, beige) on your meaningful midpoint. Choose two distinct hues for the endpoints that are perceptually distant and, if possible, intuitively map to your data (e.g., blue for cold, red for hot).

## Guidance

If your story is about the contrast between the highest and lowest values, use a diverging color scale to give them equal visual emphasis.

## Why

A sequential scale (light to dark) naturally guides the viewer's eye to the single darkest extreme. A diverging scale, by using two different, saturated hues for its endpoints, makes both the top and bottom of the range stand out against a neutral middle. This is an editorial choice that frames the narrative around disparity, polarization, or a comparison of extremes.

### Core Principle

The choice of visual encoding is an editorial one that shapes the narrative. Use encodings that align with and reinforce the primary message of your chart.

## When it applies

- When you want to tell a story about the gap between two ends of a spectrum (e.g., high vs. low income, rich vs. poor).
- When both positive and negative extremes are equally important parts of the story (e.g., unusually high and unusually low temperatures).
- When the narrative focuses on polarization or deviation from a norm in both directions.

## Exceptions

If your story is primarily about identifying the top performers, highest values, or a progression toward a single goal, a sequential scale is more direct and effective. It focuses attention where it matters for that narrative.

## Trade-offs

Emphasizing the extremes inherently de-emphasizes the middle of the range, which is typically colored in neutral, low-saturation tones. Viewers may perceive less variation among mid-range values.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Narrative Mismatch:** Your chart's title or accompanying text talks about a "widening gap," "stark contrast," or "polarization," but your visualization uses a sequential color scale that only highlights one side of the story (e.g., the high end).
- **Hidden Extremes:** The lowest values in your data are important to your story, but they are rendered in very light, hard-to-see colors that fade into the background.

## How to Improve

- **Re-evaluate your narrative:** First, confirm that your primary goal is to highlight the contrast between both extremes.
- **Switch to a diverging scale:** If the narrative fits, change your color scheme. If there's no natural midpoint in the data, you can place the center of the scale at the median or average value to visually balance the data distribution between the two hues.

## Guidance

To make subtle variations in your data more visible, especially in dense visualizations, use a diverging color scale.

## Why

A diverging scale essentially splits your data range in half and applies a separate color gradient to each part (e.g., one for 0-50, another for 50-100). Because each gradient covers a smaller numerical range than a single sequential gradient would (e.g., 50 points vs. 100), the color change per unit of data is larger. This magnification effect "stretches" the color variation and makes small differences between data points easier to perceive.

## When it applies

- In dense visualizations like heatmaps or choropleth maps where many values are numerically close but their differences are meaningful.
- When you need to show both a primary, high-amplitude pattern and a secondary, low-amplitude pattern within the same chart.
- When a sequential scale results in a chart where most elements are colored in very similar, hard-to-distinguish shades.

## Exceptions

If the data range is very small, or if the primary goal is to show the overall trend from low to high without focusing on minor fluctuations, a sequential scale is simpler and clearer.

## Trade-offs

The increased perceptual resolution comes at the cost of increased complexity. A two-hue scale requires more cognitive effort from the viewer, who must consult the legend to understand the color-to-value mapping, especially around the midpoint.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Washed-Out Chart:** Your chart appears to have little variation, with most data points colored in very similar shades. You know from inspecting the raw numbers that there are meaningful, albeit small, differences that are being lost.
- **Hidden Patterns:** There is a known subtle pattern in your data that is not visible in the visualization because the color differences are too slight to be perceived.

## How to Improve

- **Switch to a diverging scale:** Replace your sequential scale with a diverging one. Center it on the data's median or another meaningful point to best "stretch" the perceptual distance across the range.
- **Choose an effective palette:** Ensure the two hues you choose for the extremes are distinct and that the midpoint is a neutral, light color like white, grey, or beige. This maximizes the perceptual range of both gradients.

## Guidance

For data that represents a simple low-to-high progression without a meaningful midpoint, use a single-hue sequential color scale (e.g., light green to dark green) for the most intuitive interpretation.

## Why

Sequential scales leverage a near-universal perceptual heuristic: "darker means more." This allows viewers to understand the general pattern of the data almost instantly, often without needing to consult a color key. Diverging scales lack this intuitive quality; viewers must read the legend to understand which color represents "high" and which represents "low," increasing cognitive load.

### Core Principle

Reduce the cognitive work required to decode your visualization. An intuitive encoding that requires no explanation is more effective than a complex one that does.

## When it applies

- When visualizing simple magnitudes, like population, revenue, age, or counts, that progress from low to high.
- When designing for a general audience or for quick, at-a-glance consumption.
- In dashboards or small multiples where including a detailed legend for every chart is impractical.

## Exceptions

When the data has a meaningful midpoint (like zero) or when the story is about emphasizing both high and low extremes, a diverging scale is more appropriate, despite its lower intuitiveness. In these cases, the structural clarity of the diverging scale outweighs the intuitive advantage of the sequential scale.

## Trade-offs

A sequential scale is less effective at highlighting a specific midpoint and may not reveal subtle data variations as well as a diverging scale. It focuses the narrative entirely on progression to the maximum value.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Legend-Reliant:** Your chart uses a diverging scale for a simple magnitude (e.g., population density), and you notice that viewers are confused about which color means "more" until they find and read the legend.
- **Unnecessary Complexity:** You are using two hues to show a simple range, forcing your audience to do extra work to understand a straightforward concept.

## How to Improve

- **Simplify the Encoding:** If your data is a simple low-to-high range without a meaningful midpoint, switch from a diverging scale to a sequential one.
- **Choose an Intuitive Color Ramp:** Pick a single hue and vary its lightness from light (for low values) to dark (for high values). This creates the clearest "darker means more" mapping.

## Guidance

If you use a diverging color scale, you must include a clear legend that explicitly explains the meaning of the colors.

## Why

Unlike sequential scales, diverging scales have no built-in intuitive meaning. A viewer cannot know whether red means "high" and blue means "low," or the reverse, without being told. An un-labeled diverging scale is ambiguous and forces the audience to guess, which is a critical failure in data communication.

### Core Principle

Make your encoding scheme explicit. A visualization's decoding key must be clear, visible, and unambiguous to ensure accurate interpretation.

## When it applies

This rule applies every single time a diverging color scale is used in a visualization intended for others.

## Exceptions

None. This is a mandatory requirement for clarity. The only conceivable exception is a visualization for personal, exploratory use where the creator has memorized their own color scheme, but this does not apply to any visualization meant for an audience.

## Trade-offs

Adding a legend and labels takes up space and can add minor visual clutter. However, this is a necessary and non-negotiable cost for making the chart readable and preventing misinterpretation.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "What-Does-Red-Mean?" Question:** Viewers ask, "So, is red good or bad here?" or "Which color is higher?"
- **Explanation in Prose:** You find yourself having to explain what the colors mean in the body text or caption because there is no legend.
- **Ambiguous Extremes:** Your chart uses a two-hue color scale (e.g., purple and green), and there's no cultural or intuitive convention to suggest which end is high or low.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add a Labeled Legend.** At a minimum, add a color key that shows the gradient and labels the two extremes with clear, descriptive text (e.g., "More Democratic," "More Republican").
- **Moderate Approach: Use a Self-Explanatory Title.** Integrate the color meaning into the chart's title or subtitle to provide immediate context. For example: "In most <span style='color:red;'>African and Asian countries</span>, less than half the population used the internet in 2015."
- **Comprehensive Approach: Annotate Directly.** In addition to a legend, use annotations directly on the chart to point out a key data point and explain its color. For example, an arrow pointing to a dark blue country with a label that reads "High Usage (>80%)." This reinforces the meaning in context and reduces the back-and-forth eye movement to the legend.
