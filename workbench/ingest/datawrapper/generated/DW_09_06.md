## Guidance

If using a less saturated or pastel palette, you must verify that the colors are still distinguishable and meet accessibility contrast standards. If they are not, add redundant visual cues to ensure clarity for all users.

## Why

The benefit of using muted colors for emotional neutrality is completely negated if some users cannot perceive the differences between them. An inaccessible chart fails at its primary goal of clear communication. Ensuring distinguishability respects all users, including those with color vision deficiencies (CVD) and low vision.

## When it applies

- Always, but it is especially critical when you have intentionally chosen a desaturated, pastel, or low-contrast color palette.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Squint Test:** If you squint at your chart, different colored categories blur into a single mass.
- **Colorblind Simulation Failure:** Using a CVD simulator reveals that two or more colors become identical.
- **Subtle Neighbors:** The palette includes colors that are very close in hue and brightness (e.g., a light green and a light cyan).

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** Place labels directly on or next to chart elements (bars, lines, slices). This makes color a secondary, rather than primary, channel for identification.
- **Moderate approach: Add High-Contrast Outlines.** Add a darker, saturated, or high-contrast stroke around your desaturated fills. This clearly defines the boundaries of each element even if the fill colors are not easily distinguished.
- **Comprehensive approach: Use Redundant Encoding.** In addition to accessible colors, use another visual channel to differentiate categories. For a line chart, use different stroke styles (solid, dashed, dotted). For a scatter plot, use different shapes (circles, squares, triangles). This provides multiple ways for users to decode the information.
