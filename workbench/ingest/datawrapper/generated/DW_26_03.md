## Guidance

Intentionally use color saturation and lightness to create a visual hierarchy that guides the reader's attention. Assign more saturated, vibrant colors to more important categories and more muted, desaturated colors to less important ones.

## Why

Saturated, bright, and dark colors have more "visual weight" — they naturally draw the human eye more than pale, muted, or gray colors. This perceptual phenomenon can be harnessed to guide your audience's attention. By making the most important data points the most visually prominent, you can help readers process the information more efficiently and focus on the main story. Conversely, de-emphasizing less critical data (like "Other" categories) reduces clutter.

### Core Principle

Visual hierarchy should match information hierarchy. Make the most important elements look the most important.

## When it applies

-   When visualizing categories that have a clear hierarchy of importance (e.g., major vs. minor parties, top competitors vs. the rest of the market).
-   When you want to draw attention to a specific category or finding.
-   When dealing with an "Other" category that needs to be shown but de-emphasized.

## Exceptions

-   In purely exploratory visualizations where you don't want to impose a pre-determined hierarchy and want all categories to have equal visual weight.
-   When all categories are considered equally important.

## Trade-offs

-   This is an explicit act of editorializing. You are using visual properties to impose your judgment of what is important onto the data. This is a powerful storytelling technique, but it can be perceived as biased if not done thoughtfully and transparently.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Misleading Emphasis:** A minor data point is the most eye-catching element in your chart due to a poor color choice.
-   **Visual Chaos:** All colors are highly saturated, creating a visually noisy and overwhelming experience with no clear focal point.
-   **Lost in the Gray:** The most important finding is rendered in a pale, muted color and is easily overlooked.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Mute the 'Other'.** If you have an "Other" or "Miscellaneous" category, always assign it a neutral, low-saturation color like light gray. This is the single easiest way to start building visual hierarchy.
-   **Moderate Approach: Identify and Emphasize.** Decide on the one or two most important categories in your chart. Keep their colors bright and saturated, but convert the colors for all other categories to more muted or desaturated versions.
-   **Comprehensive Approach: Create Tiered Palettes.** Develop a color system with different tiers of emphasis. For example, a "main" palette of saturated colors for primary subjects, a "secondary" palette of muted colors for context, and a gray for non-data elements or de-emphasized categories.
