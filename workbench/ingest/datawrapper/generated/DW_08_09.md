## Guidance

When selecting a color palette, test it against both a light (white/light gray) and a dark (black/dark gray) background to ensure all colors remain visible and distinct.

## Why

You rarely have full control over where your visualization will end up. A chart you design on a white background for a report might be screenshotted and pasted into a dark-themed presentation, embedded in a website with a dark mode, or printed. Colors with medium lightness are particularly vulnerable: dark navy can disappear on a black background, and light yellow can disappear on a white one. By choosing colors that are robust enough to work in both environments, you create a more versatile and durable visualization that won't fail unexpectedly when its context changes.

### Core Principle

Design for resilience. Anticipate that your work will be used in contexts you didn't plan for and make design choices that ensure it remains effective.

## When it applies

- This is a good general practice for any chart, but it's especially important for visualizations intended for wide distribution or reuse (e.g., in a style guide, a component library, or a public-facing report).
- Crucial for charts that will be used in presentations, which often have varied and unpredictable background colors.

## Exceptions

- If you are 100% certain that your visualization will only ever be displayed in a single, controlled context (e.g., a specific print publication with a fixed background color), then you only need to optimize for that one background. This is rare in digital environments.

## Trade-offs

- **Palette Constraints:** This rule disqualifies very light pastel colors and very dark, muted colors, which might limit some aesthetic options. You are trading a wider range of possible lightness values for greater robustness.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Disappearing Colors:** When you toggle your OS or an application to dark mode, some of the elements in your chart become invisible or very hard to see.
- **Low-Contrast Pastels:** Your palette includes very light colors (like pastel yellow or baby blue) that are barely visible against a white background.
- **Muted Darks:** Your palette includes very dark colors (like navy, maroon, or forest green) that blend in with a black background.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add a Background/Border.** If you can't change the colors, ensure the chart is always exported with a solid white background (or a white border). This "insulates" it from whatever background it's placed on, but it can look clunky.
- **Moderate Approach: Adjust Lightness.** For each color in your palette, check its contrast against both black and white. Avoid colors that are too close to either extreme. A good rule of thumb is to pick colors from the middle 50% of the lightness range—not too light, and not too dark.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Design a Dual-Theme Palette.** For maximum flexibility, design two explicit versions of your palette: one for light themes and one for dark themes. This is common practice in UI design systems and can be applied to data visualization, ensuring optimal readability in both contexts.
