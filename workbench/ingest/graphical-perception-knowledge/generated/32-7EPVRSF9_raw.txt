## Guidance

For tasks that require viewers to compare or rank the sizes of different categories, use a bar chart instead of a pie chart.

## Why

Humans are much more accurate at judging and comparing lengths from a common baseline (as in a bar chart) than they are at judging and comparing angles, areas, or arc lengths (as in a pie chart). Using a bar chart reduces cognitive load and leads to faster, more accurate interpretation.

### Core Principle

Humans judge quantities more accurately by comparing positions along a common scale than by comparing angles or areas. Make the most important comparisons the easiest and most accurate to perform.

## When it applies

- When the primary task is to compare the relative sizes of different parts of a whole.
- When you need to rank categories by value accurately.
- When you have more than 2-3 categories to display.

## Exceptions

- **Simple Composition:** If the goal is simply to show that a few (2-3) parts make up a whole and the values are starkly different (e.g., 80% vs. 20%), a pie chart can be effective.
- **Value Lookup:** If the task is just to read the labeled value of a single, specific slice (e.g., "What percentage is Category A?"), a pie chart with clear labels can perform as well as a bar chart.

## Trade-offs

- **"Part-of-a-Whole" Feel:** A bar chart might less intuitively convey the "part-of-a-whole" relationship compared to a pie chart. This can be mitigated by using a 100% stacked bar chart or explicitly stating that the bars sum to a total.
- **Space:** A bar chart, particularly a horizontal one, may require more screen space than a single pie chart.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Slice Similarity:** Two or more slices in your pie chart are so close in size that it's impossible to tell which is larger without reading labels.
- **Angle Estimation Game:** Viewers are forced to play a mental game of estimating angles to compare slices.
- **Legend Reliance:** The chart is unreadable without constantly referring back and forth to a color-coded legend.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** If you must use a pie chart, add clear data labels (values or percentages) directly onto each slice. This provides an "escape hatch" so viewers can read values instead of estimating angles.

- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Donut Chart.** Converting to a donut chart can slightly improve data-ink ratio and offers a center space to display a total or summary, but it does not fix the underlying perceptual issue with angles.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Convert to a Bar Chart.** The most effective improvement is to change the chart type to a bar chart. Sort the bars by value (unless there is a natural order) to make ranking and comparison tasks trivial.

## Guidance

When encoding quantitative data for comparison or ranking tasks, prioritize using position along a common, aligned scale. This is the primary encoding used in bar charts, dot plots, and scatterplots.

## Why

Humans are perceptually most accurate when judging quantitative values based on the relative positions of marks on a shared scale. Other visual channels like length (on unaligned scales), angle, area, or color saturation are decoded with significantly more error, leading to less accurate interpretations.

### Core Principle

Visual encodings are not created equal; their effectiveness for a given task follows a perceptual hierarchy. The most effective encoding should be used for the most important information.

## When it applies

- Anytime the primary goal is for users to make accurate quantitative judgments, comparisons, or rankings.
- When choosing a chart type for quantitative data, this principle favors bar charts, line charts, and dot plots over pie charts, treemaps, or bubble charts.

## Exceptions

- **Geographic Data:** In maps, position is already used to encode location. Therefore, another channel (like color in a choropleth map or size in a bubble map) must be used to encode quantitative values.
- **Composition Tasks:** When the primary goal is to show a part-to-whole relationship, area (treemap) or angle (pie chart) are often used because they intuitively convey the concept of a whole, even though they are less accurate for specific comparisons.
- **High-Density Data:** For very large datasets, where overplotting would make position-based charts (like scatterplots) unreadable, using color on a heatmap can be a more effective alternative to show density and general patterns.

## Trade-offs

- **Space Efficiency:** Position-based charts (like bar charts) can take up more space than area-based charts (like treemaps) that are designed to be space-filling.
- **Aesthetics vs. Accuracy:** A desire for a specific aesthetic or chart metaphor (e.g., using circles in a bubble chart) may override the goal of maximal perceptual accuracy.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Imprecise Comparisons:** Viewers can only make vague judgments like "this is bigger than that" but struggle to estimate how much bigger.
- **High Error Rate:** When tested, users make significant and systematic errors in estimating values from the chart.
- **Distorted Proportions:** A treemap or bubble chart gives a distorted sense of relative magnitudes compared to a bar chart of the same data.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Data Labels.** If you must use a less accurate channel like area or color, add explicit data labels. This provides an "escape hatch" that allows users to read precise values instead of relying on flawed perceptual judgments.

- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Better Chart.** If using a pie chart (angle), convert it to a bar chart. If using a treemap (area), consider if a bar chart would better serve the primary comparison task.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Provide Multiple Views.** If you are using a map where position is constrained, provide a linked bar chart view. This allows users to see the geographic pattern in the map but switch to the bar chart for accurate comparisons and ranking.

## Guidance

To help viewers accurately compare the heights of bars, either place the bars adjacent to one another or sort the chart by value to minimize the distance between bars of similar height.

## Why

The further apart two bars are, the more difficult it is for the human eye to accurately judge the difference in their heights. This effect, known as Just Noticeable Difference (JND), increases with separation distance. Reducing the distance between the items to be compared reduces the perceptual and cognitive load on the viewer, leading to faster and more accurate insights.

### Core Principle

Perceptual accuracy for comparison tasks degrades with increased separation between visual marks. Therefore, design should minimize the distance for key comparisons.

## When it applies

- When creating a bar chart where a key task is to compare the values of non-adjacent bars.
- When deciding on the sort order of a bar chart's categories.
- When a bar chart has many categories, increasing the potential distance between bars.

## Exceptions

- **Intrinsic Order:** If the categories in a bar chart have a natural, meaningful order (e.g., a time series, age groups, stages in a process), you should preserve that order. In this case, do not re-sort by value. Be aware that comparisons between distant bars will be more difficult.

## Trade-offs

- **Loss of Natural Order:** Sorting bars by value to improve comparison can disrupt a meaningful categorical order (e.g., alphabetical, geographical), making it harder to look up a specific category. You must choose which task to optimize for: comparison (sort by value) or lookup (sort by category).

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Eye-Dart Test:** Your eyes have to dart back and forth repeatedly to compare two bars that are far apart.
- **Comparison Uncertainty:** You find yourself unsure if two distant bars are the same height or if one is slightly different.
- **Ruler Use:** You feel the need to use your cursor or a physical object as a straight-edge to compare the tops of two distant bars.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Highlighting.** If you cannot change the sort order, use interaction to allow the user to select two bars for comparison, highlighting them and perhaps showing the exact difference.

- **Moderate Redesign: Sort by Value.** If the categorical order is not essential, sort the bars by their value (ascending or descending). This is the single most effective way to improve comparability across the entire chart.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use a Slope Chart.** If the primary goal is to compare values between two specific points (e.g., sales in 2023 vs. 2024 for various products), a slope chart is far more effective than a bar chart because it directly visualizes the difference and rank change.

## Guidance

When two or more graphical elements (e.g., bars, pie slices, bubbles) have values that are so close that their visual difference is imperceptible, add a secondary encoding like a text label to make the difference clear and unambiguous.

## Why

Every visualization has a "Just Noticeable Difference" (JND)—a minimum threshold below which differences in value cannot be reliably perceived. Relying on vision alone for these sub-threshold comparisons leads to high error rates and viewer misinterpretation. Explicitly annotating these cases provides an "escape hatch" from this perceptual ambiguity, preventing viewers from making false assumptions of equality.

### Core Principle

Do not force users to visually perceive differences that are below the Just Noticeable Difference (JND) threshold. If a small difference matters, state it explicitly.

## When it applies

- In any chart where precise comparison between very similar values is important.
- In bar charts where similar-height bars are far apart, which increases their JND.
- In pie or bubble charts, where angle and area judgments are inherently less precise, making small differences harder to spot.
- When communicating data to an audience that requires high precision.

## Exceptions

- **Emphasizing Similarity:** If the goal of the chart is to show that several values are "roughly the same" or fall within a tight cluster, then highlighting the small differences with labels may be counterproductive to the message.
- **Aesthetic Focus:** If the chart is primarily for aesthetic purposes or to show a general, high-level pattern, and precise comparisons are not an intended task, annotations may add unnecessary clutter.

## Trade-offs

- **Clutter:** Adding labels increases the visual clutter of a chart. Over-annotating can make the overall pattern harder to see and overwhelm the viewer.
- **Unwarranted Attention:** Labeling a very small difference can draw undue attention to it, making it seem more important than it is.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Are they the same?" Test:** You look at two elements and genuinely can't tell if they are the same size or if one is slightly larger.
- **Visual Merging:** When you squint at your chart, two different elements visually merge into one.
- **Measurement by Pixel:** You find yourself trying to count pixels or measure the screen to determine the difference between two marks.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Text Labels.** The simplest solution. Place the exact data value as a text label on or near the ambiguous elements. This is the most direct way to resolve ambiguity.

- **Moderate Redesign: Use Ordinal Glyphs.** For a group of similar items, if only their rank matters, add a secondary glyph (e.g., small icons, rank numbers) to indicate their order without adding the clutter of full numerical labels.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Change the Scale or Chart Type.** If many elements are below the JND, the chart's scale may be inappropriate. Consider changing to a log scale to emphasize multiplicative differences, or switch to a chart type that directly encodes the difference itself, such as a slope chart.
