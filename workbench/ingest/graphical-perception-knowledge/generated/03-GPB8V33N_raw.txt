## Guidance

For tasks involving identifying clusters in multivariate data, prefer Parallel Coordinates Plots (PCPs) over scatter plot matrices or raw data tables.

## Why

PCPs represent each multi-dimensional data point as a single polyline that connects axes representing different variables. Data points that are close to each other in the multi-dimensional space will have polylines that follow similar paths, forming a visually distinct "band". This makes it perceptually easier to identify groups of similar items (clusters) compared to mentally integrating information across multiple separate scatter plots or scanning a table of numbers.

### Core Principle

Make the most important comparisons the easiest to see. For multivariate clustering, the "comparison" is the overall similarity of data points across all dimensions, which a PCP visualizes as a single, coherent shape (a polyline).

## When it applies

- When your primary task is to identify groups or clusters of similar items in a dataset.
- When your data has three or more quantitative variables (i.e., is multivariate).
- When you need to compare items across all of their attributes simultaneously.

## Exceptions

- **Low Dimensionality:** For data with only two variables, a single scatter plot is more direct and effective for showing clusters.
- **Audience Unfamiliarity:** If the audience is completely unfamiliar with PCPs and there is no opportunity for explanation, the learning curve may be a barrier. A scatter plot matrix, while less effective, might be more approachable.
- **Extreme Data Scale:** With very high dimensionality (e.g., >20 axes) or a very large number of data points, visual clutter from overplotting in a PCP can obscure patterns, reducing its effectiveness without interactive features like filtering or aggregation.

## Trade-offs

- **Learnability:** PCPs are less familiar to a general audience than scatter plots and may require a brief explanation to interpret correctly.
- **Clutter:** As the number of data points increases, overplotting can obscure clusters. This often necessitates interactive features like brushing, linking, or adjusting opacity to explore the data effectively.
- **Axis Order:** The order of the axes in a PCP can significantly affect which patterns are visible. Discovering the optimal order is a non-trivial problem.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Cross-Plot Confusion:** Viewers struggle to track individual data points or groups across a matrix of different scatter plots.
- **Table Overload:** Viewers are presented with a large table of numbers and are unable to spot any patterns or groupings.
- **"Which one is it?":** Viewers repeatedly ask how to connect a point in one scatter plot to its corresponding point in another.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Enhance Scatter Plots.** If you must use a scatter plot matrix, use consistent coloring for categories and implement brushing-and-linking, where selecting a point in one plot highlights it in all other plots. This helps, but does not fully solve, the cognitive load problem.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Switch to a Parallel Coordinates Plot.** Replace the scatter plot matrix or data table with a PCP. This directly visualizes the multivariate relationships and makes clusters more apparent as bands of similarly-pathed lines.

## Guidance

To identify outliers or anomalies in multivariate datasets, use Parallel Coordinates Plots (PCPs).

## Why

In a PCP, outliers often manifest as polylines that follow a dramatically different path from the majority of other lines. A data point that is an outlier because it has an extreme value on one dimension will produce a line that peaks or dips sharply on that axis. A multivariate outlier that doesn't correlate with the rest of the data will have a line that crosses through the main "bands" of the chart. These visual deviations make anomalies perceptually "pop out" from the background pattern.

## When it applies

- When your primary task is to find data points that are unusual, extreme, or do not fit the general pattern.
- When analyzing data with three or more quantitative variables.
- When an "outlier" can be defined by its relationship across multiple variables, not just an extreme value in one.

## Exceptions

- **Single Dimension Outliers:** If you only need to find outliers based on a single variable, a simpler chart like a box plot or a histogram is more direct and efficient.
- **Audience Unfamiliarity:** As with other uses of PCPs, a steep learning curve for an unprepared audience can be a significant barrier.

## Trade-offs

- **Clutter:** In datasets with many data points, overplotting can make it difficult to trace individual lines, potentially hiding outliers within dense regions. Interactive filtering or highlighting is often necessary.
- **Definition of "Outlier":** The visual salience of an outlier in a PCP depends on the axis scaling and ordering. What appears as an outlier with one configuration might not with another.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Scanning Nightmare:** Viewers have to meticulously scan every point in every plot of a scatter plot matrix to find a potential outlier.
- **Hidden Deviations:** An outlier is not extreme on any single dimension, so it isn't obvious in box plots or histograms, and it is lost in a scatter plot matrix because its multi-dimensional deviation is not visible.
- **Slow Performance:** It takes viewers a very long time and high mental effort to confidently identify an outlier.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Highlight Extremes.** In a scatter plot matrix, add reference lines for means or quartiles to each plot to help extreme values stand out. Use linked selections to see if an extreme point in one plot is also extreme in others.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Switch to a Parallel Coordinates Plot.** Replace other chart types with a PCP. This allows outliers to be detected holistically based on their entire profile across all variables, making them easier to spot as uniquely-shaped polylines.

## Guidance

When the primary task for the user is to retrieve a specific, precise data value, a simple data table is often more effective and efficient than a graphical visualization.

## Why

Visualizations are optimized for revealing patterns, trends, and relationships at a glance—tasks that involve perception and estimation. Retrieving a precise value is a different kind of task: search and lookup. Forcing a lookup task into a visualization requires the user to first locate the graphical mark (e.g., a point or line) and then decode its value by referring to one or more axes. This is a multi-step cognitive process. A data table, by contrast, presents the exact value directly, reducing the task to a simple textual search.

### Core Principle

Match the tool to the task. Use visualizations for perceptual tasks (pattern-finding) and use tables for lookup tasks (value-finding).

## When it applies

- The user's primary goal is to find a specific number (e.g., "What were the sales for the Western region in Q3?").
- Precision is more important than understanding the overall pattern.
- The user already knows what they are looking for and just needs the exact value.

## Exceptions

- **Hybrid Tasks:** If the user needs to look up a value *within the context of a broader pattern* (e.g., "Find the value of the highest peak"), a visualization is necessary. The visualization reveals the pattern ("highest peak"), and then the user performs the lookup. Interactive tooltips on a chart are an excellent solution for this hybrid task.
- **Large Datasets:** For extremely large datasets, a table is impractical. A visualization can be used to first filter down to a region of interest, which can then be displayed as a smaller, more manageable table.

## Trade-offs

- **Pattern Obscurity:** A table is very poor at revealing trends, clusters, or outliers. By optimizing for lookup, you sacrifice the ability to see the "big picture."
- **Data Density:** A table can quickly become overwhelming as the number of rows and columns grows, making even lookup tasks difficult without sorting or filtering capabilities.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Axis Overload:** Your chart axes are cluttered with dense tick marks and labels because users need to read precise values from them.
- **Excessive Tooltips:** Every single data point on your chart requires a tooltip because the main purpose of the chart is for users to find individual numbers.
- **"Just give me the data":** Your audience regularly asks for the raw data file after you present them with a visualization.
- **Slow Lookups:** Users take a long time to find and report a specific value from your chart, often making small errors in reading the axes.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add a Summary Table.** Accompany your visualization with a small table that lists key values (e.g., the top 5, totals, or specific points of interest). This provides the "best of both worlds."

- **Moderate Approach: Use Interactive Tooltips.** In an interactive visualization, provide on-demand tooltips that show precise values when a user hovers over a data point. This keeps the main visualization clean while allowing for precise lookup when needed.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Provide Both Views.** Offer the user the ability to toggle between a chart view (for patterns) and a table view (for lookup). This explicitly supports both tasks without compromising either.

## Guidance

For identifying which items in a multivariate dataset have changed the most over time or across ordered stages, prefer Parallel Coordinates Plots (PCPs).

## Why

When the parallel axes of a PCP are ordered chronologically (e.g., Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4), the line segments connecting the axes directly visualize change over time. The slope of a line segment is a powerful perceptual cue for the rate of change: steep slopes indicate large changes, while flat lines indicate stability. This allows the human eye to quickly scan the visualization and identify the polylines (i.e., data items) with the most volatile or dynamic behavior.

## When it applies

- When the primary task is to find which items have changed the most or least.
- When the data has multiple attributes that represent ordered stages, such as points in time (e.g., yearly sales) or experimental phases.
- When comparing the trajectories of many different items simultaneously.

## Exceptions

- **Comparing Only Two Time Points:** If you are only comparing two points in time (e.g., "before" and "after"), a simpler chart like a slope chart or a dumbbell plot can be more direct and less visually complex.
- **Very Few Items:** If you are tracking change for only one or two items, a standard line chart is often clearer and more familiar to the audience.

## Trade-offs

- **Clutter:** With many data items (lines), the plot can become a "spaghetti graph," making it difficult to trace individual trajectories without interactive highlighting.
- **Axis Scaling:** The perception of slope is dependent on the scaling of the axes. If axes have different scales, it can be misleading to visually compare the steepness of lines across different segments. Normalizing the data may be necessary.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Mental Integration:** Viewers are forced to compare values across multiple bar charts or a scatter plot matrix to manually calculate the differences over time.
- **Lost in the Spaghetti:** In a standard line chart with many overlapping lines, it's impossible to track a single item's trajectory from start to finish.
- **Table Subtraction:** Users are exporting data to a spreadsheet to manually subtract columns and find the largest differences.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Create Slope Charts.** If you have many items but only two time points, use a series of small multiple slope charts. This declutters the view while still using the powerful perceptual cue of slope.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Switch to a Parallel Coordinates Plot.** For three or more time points, represent the data using a PCP with the axes ordered chronologically. This will make the trajectories and relative changes of all items visible in a single view. Consider using color to highlight specific items of interest.

## Guidance

When the primary goal is to assess the strength and direction of the relationship (correlation) between two quantitative variables, a scatterplot is the most effective visualization choice.

## Why

The human visual system is well-attuned to perceiving patterns in 2D point clouds. The overall shape, direction (upward or downward sloping), and tightness of the points in a scatterplot provide strong and reliable perceptual cues for judging correlation. Other visualizations, like parallel coordinates plots, require viewers to interpret patterns of line crossings, which has been shown to be a less accurate and more easily biased method for estimating correlation.

## When it applies

- When the main question is "Is there a relationship between variable X and variable Y?".
- When you need to communicate the strength (tight vs. loose) and direction (positive vs.negative) of a bivariate relationship.
- When analyzing two continuous quantitative variables.

## Exceptions

- **Multivariate Correlation:** If you need to assess correlations across many pairs of variables at once, a scatter plot matrix (a grid of many small scatterplots) or a heatmap of a correlation matrix is more efficient than creating dozens of individual scatterplots.
- **Categorical Data:** This guidance applies to quantitative data. To see relationships involving categorical variables, other chart types like box plots, violin plots, or grouped bar charts are more appropriate.

## Trade-offs

- **Overplotting:** With very large datasets, individual points in a scatterplot can blend into a solid mass, obscuring the density and structure of the relationship. In these cases, techniques like using transparency (alpha), sampling the data, or using 2D density plots (a heatmap-scatterplot hybrid) are necessary.
- **Limited to Two Variables:** A single scatterplot can only show the relationship between two variables at a time. To incorporate a third variable, you may need to use an additional visual channel like color or size, which are less perceptually accurate.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Incorrect Chart Choice:** You are using a dual-axis line chart or grouped bar chart to try and show correlation, which can be highly misleading due to independent axis scaling.
- **PCP for Correlation:** You are relying on a parallel coordinates plot and asking viewers to judge correlation based on line crossings, which is a perceptually difficult and error-prone task.
- **Inability to Judge Strength:** Viewers can tell if the trend is generally positive or negative but cannot distinguish between a strong and a weak correlation.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: State the Correlation Coefficient.** If you cannot change the chart, add a text annotation that explicitly states the correlation coefficient (e.g., "r = 0.85"). This provides the precise statistical relationship that the chart fails to communicate perceptually.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use a Scatterplot.** If the task is to show correlation, use the right tool for the job. Create a scatterplot mapping the two variables to the X and Y axes. Consider adding a regression line to make the trend more explicit.

## Guidance

When creating a static visualization for common analytical tasks like comparing values, ranking categories, or seeing a distribution, a bar chart is often a safe, effective, and versatile choice.

## Why

The primary visual encoding in a bar chart is length along a common scale, which is one of the most accurately judged perceptual tasks humans can perform (Cleveland & McGill, 1984). This makes it easy and intuitive to compare values, identify the largest and smallest, and see the shape of the data. Their simplicity and familiarity also reduce cognitive load for a general audience. A synthesis of dozens of empirical studies found that bar charts are a top-performing visualization type for a majority of common low-level analysis tasks.

## When it applies

- **Comparing** discrete quantitative values across different categories.
- **Ranking or Sorting** categories from highest to lowest.
- **Filtering** or finding data points that meet a certain threshold.
- Showing the **distribution** of data across a set of categories (as a histogram).
- Identifying **extreme values** (min/max).
- Showing **part-to-whole** relationships (when sorted and displayed as percentages).

## Exceptions

- **Showing Trends Over Continuous Time:** A line chart is superior for showing change over a continuous interval like time, as it visually connects the data points to emphasize trend and continuity.
- **Showing Correlation:** A scatterplot is the correct choice for visualizing the relationship between two quantitative variables.
- **Many Categories:** If you have a very large number of categories (e.g., > 30-40), a standard bar chart can become cluttered and unreadable. A different chart type or an interactive approach may be needed.
- **Systematic Bias:** Be aware that the aspect ratio of bars can introduce perceptual bias. Very tall, thin bars tend to be underestimated, while very wide, short bars are overestimated. Square-like bars show the least bias (Ceja et al., 2020, as cited in Zeng & Battle, 2023).

## Trade-offs

- **Space:** Vertical bar charts can use a lot of horizontal space, especially with long category labels (horizontal bar charts are a good solution here).
- **Nuance:** While great for discrete comparisons, bar charts are less effective at showing nuanced distributional shapes compared to alternatives like violin plots or density plots.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Axis starting above zero:** The y-axis of a bar chart does not start at zero, which breaks the length-encoding principle and leads to misinterpretation of the relative magnitudes.
- **Using bars for time series:** A bar chart is used to connect points in a continuous time series, which incorrectly implies discrete, independent values rather than a continuous trend. A line chart should be used instead.
- **Too many bars:** The chart is a dense "forest" of bars that are too thin to be clearly distinguished.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Sort Your Bars.** For nominal categories, always sort the bars in ascending or descending order. This makes comparisons easier and reveals the rank order of the data.

- **Moderate Redesign: Switch Orientation.** If category labels are long and getting truncated, switch from a vertical bar chart to a horizontal one. This provides ample space for readable labels.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Choose the Right Chart for the Task.** While bar charts are versatile, confirm they are the best fit. If the task is showing a trend over time, use a line chart. If it's correlation, use a scatterplot. Match the chart to the specific analytical goal.
