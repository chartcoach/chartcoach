## Guidance

When optimizing a categorical color palette that has existing meaning (e.g., brand colors, conventions, metaphors), use constraints to preserve that meaning. Fix essential colors in place or apply tight ranges to hue, saturation, or lightness to guide the optimization process.

## Why

Colors in visualizations often carry semantic meaning that aids cognition and recall. A purely mathematical optimization can destroy these valuable associations (e.g., turning a "danger" red into a magenta), increasing cognitive load and potentially breaking consistency with user expectations or brand identity. Constrained optimization provides a middle path, improving perceptual discriminability while respecting semantic intent.

### Core Principle

Effective visualization design balances perceptual goals (e.g., discriminability) with cognitive goals (e.g., meaning and memorability). Don't let the pursuit of one completely undermine the other.

## When it applies

- When you need to use a specific set of colors that have poor perceptual separation (e.g., a brand palette with several similar blues).
- When colors are tied to established conventions (e.g., red for Republicans/stop, blue for Democrats/go).
- When colors are used metaphorically (e.g., shades of red/orange for "hot", shades of blue for "cold").
- When extending a historical color scheme, like adding a new line to the London Tube Map, and you need to maintain consistency with the existing colors.

## Exceptions

- **No Pre-existing Meaning:** If you are creating a colormap from scratch for purely categorical data with no inherent meaning (e.g., arbitrary product IDs), you do not need to apply semantic constraints and can optimize for maximum perceptual distance freely.

## Trade-offs

- **Sub-optimal Perception:** The final, constrained palette will likely have a smaller minimum perceptual distance than a fully unconstrained, optimized palette. You are trading some amount of "perfect" discriminability for "good enough" discriminability plus semantic consistency.
- **Increased Complexity:** Setting up constrained optimization requires more thought and effort than simply clicking "optimize". You must decide which colors to fix and what range of variation is acceptable for others.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Lost Identity:** An optimized palette no longer reflects the brand or conventional colors it was derived from. A "Coke Red" has become pink.
- **Broken Metaphors:** The optimized colors no longer match their intended metaphor. The color for "hot" temperatures is now a cool purple.
- **User Confusion:** Viewers familiar with a previous version of the color scheme are confused by the changes, as the colors no longer map to the categories they remember.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Identify and Fix Key Colors.** Before optimizing, identify the 1-3 most important colors that must not change (e.g., the primary brand color, the color for "background"). Lock these colors and only allow the optimizer to change the less critical colors.

- **Moderate Approach: Constrain by Hue.** To preserve the general character of each color, apply a tight hue constraint (e.g., ±5% or ±10°) to each color before optimizing. This allows the algorithm to adjust saturation and lightness to improve separation while ensuring that blues stay blue and greens stay green. This is often the most effective and practical approach.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Layered Constraints.** For complex palettes, use a mix of techniques.
  1.  **Fix** unchangeable colors (background, main brand color).
  2.  Apply **tight hue constraints** to colors with strong semantic meaning.
  3.  Apply **looser constraints** (or none at all) to secondary colors with weaker semantic ties, giving the algorithm more freedom to find an optimal solution in the remaining color space.
