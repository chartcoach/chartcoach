## Guidance

When comparing values across different charts or groups (e.g., in small multiples), place the objects to be compared as close to each other as possible. The most accurate comparisons happen when objects are encoded on a single, shared scale.

## Why

The human visual system excels at comparing positions when they share a common scale and are close together. As the distance between them increases (especially perpendicular to the scale), the task becomes more difficult and error-prone. The brain must rely more on working memory to "hold" a value from one location and compare it to another, a process that is less accurate than direct perception.

### Core Principle

Minimize the cognitive distance for comparison. The less the eye has to travel and the less the brain has to remember, the more accurate the judgment.

## When it applies

- Anytime a user needs to compare quantitative values that are not directly adjacent.
- When designing small multiples, faceted displays, or multi-panel dashboards.
- When comparing points on two separate line or bar charts.

## Exceptions

- **Clutter:** Separating charts is often necessary for clarity, especially with many overlapping data series or complex individual charts. In these cases, the loss of direct comparability is an acceptable trade-off for reduced clutter and improved readability of each individual chart.

## Trade-offs

- **Clarity vs. Comparability:** Placing charts closer together or overlaying them can increase clutter and make individual series harder to read. Separating them improves clarity for each chart but makes comparison between them harder.

## Signs of Trouble

- **"Ruler-Waving":** Do viewers try to use their cursor or finger as a ruler to drag a level from one chart to another? This is a clear sign that the visual comparison is too difficult.
- **"Head-Bobbing":** Are viewers repeatedly looking back and forth between two charts to make a simple comparison? This indicates high cognitive load.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add a Guideline.** Draw a faint horizontal line or a shaded region across the small multiples at a key reference point (e.g., the average, a target value, or zero). This provides a shared visual anchor that aids comparison.
- **Moderate Redesign: Reorder the Facets.** If the layout is flexible, reorder your small multiples to place the two most important charts for comparison directly next to each other.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Overlay the Data.** If the scales are compatible and it won't create too much visual clutter, overlay the different data series on a single chart. This forces them to share a common scale, making comparison the most accurate.
