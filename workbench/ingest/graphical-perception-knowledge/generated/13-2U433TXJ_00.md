## Guidance

When users need to find similarly shaped time series patterns that may be shifted or stretched in time, use a horizon graph. This is common in fields like neuroscience (EEG analysis) or finance where the exact timing of a pattern is less important than its overall shape.

## Why

Horizon graphs use a dual encoding of color for high-level patterns (like peaks and valleys) and shape/position for details. This encourages viewers to focus on the overall shape and structure, making them less sensitive to local misalignments or "warping" along the time axis. This perceptual tendency aligns well with similarity search algorithms like Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) that are designed to ignore such temporal variations.

### Core Principle

The choice of visualization can guide a user's perception of similarity to align with specific algorithmic definitions. A chart that emphasizes shape over exact temporal position will naturally guide users toward a time-warping-invariant notion of similarity.

## When it applies

- When the primary task is to identify or cluster time series based on shape similarity, and the underlying similarity metric is (or should be) invariant to local stretching, compressing, or shifting in time (e.g., Dynamic Time Warping).
- When analyzing data where the timing of events can vary, such as EEG signals, stock market patterns, or sensor readings.

## Exceptions

- When the exact timing and alignment of data points are critical. In such cases, a standard line chart is more appropriate as it makes temporal deviations more salient.
- When the audience is unfamiliar with horizon graphs, as they can have a higher cognitive load and learning curve. The study found they were consistently the slowest to interpret.

## Trade-offs

- **Slower Performance:** While perceptually aligned for this task, horizon graphs are more complex. The source study found they led to significantly longer task completion times compared to line charts and colorfields.
- **Higher Cognitive Load:** The same study noted lower agreement among users and subjective feedback that horizon graphs were more difficult to use. You trade raw speed for better alignment with a specific type of similarity.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Fixation on Timing:** Users complain that two patterns "don't line up perfectly," even when their overall shapes are clearly similar. This suggests the current visualization (e.g., a line chart) is over-emphasizing temporal position.
- **Inconsistent Results:** Users manually searching for similar patterns produce results that are very different from what a DTW-based algorithm finds.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach: Educate the User.** If you must use a line chart, explicitly tell the user to "focus on the overall shape of the pattern, not its exact position in time." This provides a cognitive scaffold but doesn't change the perceptual bias of the chart.

- **Moderate approach: Switch to a Horizon Graph.** Replace the existing line chart or colorfield with a horizon graph. This directly changes the visual encoding to better support the task, guiding the user's perception toward shape-based similarity.

- **Comprehensive approach: Combine and Explain.** Use a horizon graph as the primary visualization and explicitly state the similarity criteria being used (e.g., "Showing patterns with the most similar shape, regardless of slight time shifts"). This combination of perceptual guidance (the chart) and cognitive framing (the explanation) provides the clearest direction.
