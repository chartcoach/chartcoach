## Guidance

For tasks requiring the comparison of multiple time series at a single, specific point in time (a "local" comparison), use a shared-space visualization like a simple line chart where all series are overlaid in the same plotting area.

## Why

Shared-space charts place all data series on a common set of axes, allowing for direct visual comparison with minimal eye movement. To compare values at a single point in time, the viewer only needs to scan vertically. This is perceptually and cognitively more efficient than scanning across multiple, separate charts (as in small multiples), which requires more time and working memory to hold and compare values.

### Core Principle

Make the most important comparisons the easiest to see. For local comparisons, proximity is key, and shared-space charts maximize proximity.

## When it applies

- The primary task is a **local comparison**, such as "Find the series with the highest value in Q3" or "Compare the values for Series A and B at this specific event."
- The number of time series is relatively low (e.g., under 8), where visual clutter and overlap do not completely obscure the data at the point of interest.

## Exceptions

- When the number of time series is very high, causing excessive overlap (a "spaghetti chart") that makes it impossible to distinguish the lines, even at a single point. In this case, interactivity (like hover) or a split-space approach may be necessary.
- If the individual shape and context of each time series are more important than the direct comparison, a split-space view like small multiples might be better even for local tasks.

## Trade-offs

- **Prioritizes speed for local tasks:** This approach is optimized for speed and accuracy in point-in-time comparisons.
- **Sacrifices clarity for global tasks:** The potential for line overlap and clutter makes it harder to follow individual series across their entire length or to compare dispersed trends.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Spaghetti Chart:** The chart is a tangled mess of overlapping lines, making it difficult to distinguish one series from another.
- **Back-and-Forth:** Viewers have to repeatedly look between a legend and the chart to identify which line is which, especially if colors are not easily distinguishable.
- **Task Mismatch:** The chart is used for a dispersed comparison task (e.g., "Which series had the greatest overall growth?"), and viewers struggle to trace individual lines through the clutter.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Interactivity.** If you must use a shared-space chart with many series, add hover tooltips that highlight a single series and display its value. This helps resolve ambiguity at specific points without changing the layout.

- **Moderate Approach: Emphasize Key Series.** Use color and line weight to draw attention to the most important series, while de-emphasizing others (e.g., by making them gray and thin). This preserves the direct comparison for the key series while reducing overall clutter.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Switch to a Split-Space Chart.** If the primary tasks involve dispersed comparisons or tracking individual series, break the chart into small multiples. This is a fundamental change but will significantly improve performance for those tasks.
