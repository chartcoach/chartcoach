## Guidance

To enable accurate comparisons between the sizes of different bars, ensure they all share a common, flat baseline (typically starting at zero). This applies to both simple and stacked bar charts.

## Why

Humans are far more accurate at judging and comparing positions along a common, aligned scale than they are at comparing two unaligned lengths. When bars are "floating" (such as the upper segments of a stacked bar chart), viewers must resort to less accurate length estimation. This task is not only harder but also more susceptible to interference from other visual elements (distractor bars).

### Core Principle

Humans judge quantities more accurately by comparing positions along a common scale than by comparing unaligned lengths.

## When it applies

- When the primary goal of a bar chart is for the audience to compare the magnitude of different categories.
- When choosing between a stacked bar chart and a grouped bar chart for a comparison task.
- This guidance is especially critical for stacked bar charts, where only the bottom-most segments share a common baseline.

## Exceptions

- When the primary goal is to show the composition of a total, a stacked bar chart may be appropriate. However, this comes with the explicit trade-off that comparisons between the unaligned, "floating" segments will be difficult and inaccurate.

## Trade-offs

- Stacked charts or other unaligned designs can save space compared to grouped bar charts or small multiples. However, this compactness is achieved by sacrificing the ability for viewers to make accurate comparisons between most of the components.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Floating Segments:** The chart requires viewers to compare the length of a bar segment in the middle or top of a stack to another segment.
- **Difficult Judgments:** It feels difficult to definitively say which of two unaligned bars or segments is larger.
- **Inconsistent Baselines:** The chart is a "floating" bar chart or range chart where the bars do not start from the same axis.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Limit Claims.** If you must use a stacked bar chart, only make or emphasize comparisons between the segments that sit on the common baseline. Avoid drawing conclusions about the relative size of the "floating" segments.

- **Moderate Redesign: Use a Grouped Bar Chart.** Convert the stacked bar chart to a grouped bar chart. This gives every bar its own common baseline, enabling accurate position judgments for all categories.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Line Charts or Small Multiples.** If comparing trends over time, switch from a stacked area/bar chart to a line chart, which uses a common baseline for all values. For categorical comparisons, consider small multiples of simple bar charts, which provides a common baseline for all values at the cost of more screen space.
