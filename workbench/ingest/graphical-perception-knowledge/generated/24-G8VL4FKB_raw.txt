## Guidance

To enable accurate comparison and ranking of quantities, encode them using **position along a common scale** (as in a bar or dot chart) rather than using **angle** (as in a pie chart).

## Why

Humans perceive differences in position along a common scale far more accurately and with less bias than they perceive differences in angles. Using position-based charts like bar charts reduces interpretation errors and makes it easier for viewers to correctly rank values and judge their relative magnitudes.

### Core Principle

Humans judge quantities more accurately by comparing positions along a common scale than by comparing angles, areas, or colors.

## When it applies

- When the primary task is to compare or rank the values of different categories.
- When visualizing parts of a whole (composition).
- When precision and low error rates are important for the audience's task.

## Exceptions

- **Single, Simple Proportion:** When showing a single part-to-whole relationship where the value is very close to a familiar fraction like 25%, 50%, or 75%, a pie chart can be intuitive as it leverages our ability to judge right angles.
- **Low Precision Needed:** If the goal is simply to convey a general "part-of-a-whole" message without any need for precise comparison (e.g., "Category A is a small part of the total"), a pie chart may be acceptable.

## Trade-offs

- A bar chart may take up more space (especially vertical space) than a single, compact pie chart.
- A pie chart more strongly emphasizes the "part-of-a-whole" nature of the data. This can be mitigated in a bar chart by adding a "Total" annotation or using a 100% scale.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Is it bigger?" Test:** Can you instantly and confidently tell which of two similarly-sized pie slices is larger? If it takes more than a moment of effort, angle is failing as an effective encoding.
- **Ranking Difficulty:** Is it hard to quickly rank all the slices from smallest to largest?
- **Cognitive Strain:** Do you find yourself trying to mentally rotate or move slices to compare them?

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Data Labels.** If you must use a pie chart, add direct percentage or value labels to each slice. This provides a "perceptual escape hatch," allowing users to read and compare the numbers instead of judging the flawed angles.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Switch to a Bar or Dot Chart.** Re-encode the data using a bar chart or dot plot. Sort the bars from largest to smallest to make ranking and comparison even easier and faster. This directly applies the more accurate perceptual task of judging position.

## Guidance

For comparing the magnitudes of different components, ensure they share a **common, aligned baseline** (e.g., in a grouped bar chart). Avoid chart types that require viewers to compare unaligned lengths (e.g., the inner segments of a stacked bar or stacked area chart).

## Why

Judgments of position along a common scale are perceptually more accurate and less biased than judgments of length. When objects are not aligned, the viewer's brain must perform a difficult and error-prone length comparison. Providing a shared baseline offloads this cognitive work to the much easier and more accurate task of position judgment.

### Core Principle

Make the most important comparisons the easiest to see. Aligning objects on a common scale enables a more accurate perceptual task.

## When it applies

- When the primary goal for the audience is to compare the individual values of components across different groups (e.g., comparing sales of 'Product B' in 2022 vs. 2023).
- In stacked bar charts, stacked area charts, or any visualization where segments to be compared do not start from the same axis.

## Exceptions

- **Comparing Totals:** If the primary task is to compare the *total* of the stacks, a stacked bar chart is effective, as the totals themselves share a common baseline.
- **Showing Composition:** If the goal is mainly to show the part-to-whole composition of each bar, and precise comparison of the inner parts is a secondary, less important goal.

## Trade-offs

- **Space:** Grouped bar charts typically use more horizontal space than stacked bar charts.
- **Total Obscurity:** Grouped bar charts make it harder to see the total for each group, whereas this is the primary strength of a stacked bar chart.

## Signs of Trouble

- **"Mental Calipers":** Do you find yourself trying to visually 'copy and paste' a segment from one stack to another to compare them? This is a sign you're being forced into a difficult length comparison.
- **Hidden Trends:** Is it difficult to tell if a specific category (e.g., the second segment from the bottom) is growing or shrinking across the stacks? If the trend isn't obvious, the chart is failing.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Annotations.** Add direct value labels to the key segments you want to compare. This lets users compare the numbers, bypassing the perceptual difficulty.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Grouped Bar Chart.** This places every bar on the same baseline, making component comparisons direct and accurate.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Small Multiples.** Create a separate, simple bar chart for each component category. This isolates the comparison for each component and makes its trend across groups very easy to see, at the cost of using more space.

## Guidance

When encoding quantitative values for comparison, prefer using **length** (e.g., bar charts) over **area** (e.g., bubble charts, treemaps).

## Why

Humans are notoriously bad at accurately judging 2D area. We tend to underestimate the size of larger areas relative to smaller ones, a perceptual bias described by Stevens's Power Law. This means if one bubble has twice the data value of another, viewers will perceive it as less than twice as large. Length judgments are far less biased and therefore lead to more accurate comparisons.

### Core Principle

Choose visual encodings that people can decode most accurately for the task at hand. The accuracy of human perception varies significantly by visual encoding channel.

## When it applies

- When the primary task is to make precise comparisons or rankings of quantitative data.
- When using bubble charts, proportional symbol maps, or treemaps.

## Exceptions

- **General Magnitude Only:** When you only need to show a general sense of magnitude (e.g., 'big', 'medium', 'small') and precise comparison is not important.
- **Constrained Space:** Area-based charts like treemaps can be very space-efficient for showing hierarchical part-to-whole data, but comparing the sizes of the individual (especially non-adjacent) rectangles remains perceptually difficult.

## Trade-offs

- **Space Efficiency:** Bar charts can be less space-efficient than area-based charts like treemaps or bubble charts, which can pack many data points into a compact area.
- **Additional Dimensions:** Bubble charts allow for encoding two other variables on the x and y axes, whereas a simple bar chart encodes only one quantitative value and one categorical value.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Scaling Confusion:** Are you scaling the radius or the area of your circles? Scaling by radius is a common mistake that creates a quadratic error in the perceived area. A circle with 2x the radius has 4x the area. Proper scaling should be `radius = sqrt(value)`.
- **Difficult Comparisons:** Is it hard to tell if 'Bubble A' is 10% or 50% bigger than 'Bubble B' without reading a tooltip? If the judgment isn't easy, the encoding is not effective for comparison.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Data Labels.** If you must use an area-based chart, label all areas (bubbles, squares) with their exact values. This allows users to read and compare the numbers, overriding their flawed perceptual judgments.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Switch to a Length-Based Chart.** Convert the visualization to a bar chart or dot plot. This switches the perceptual task from a difficult area judgment to a more accurate length/position judgment, dramatically improving comparison accuracy.

## Guidance

When comparing values across different charts or groups (e.g., in small multiples), place the objects to be compared as close to each other as possible. The most accurate comparisons happen when objects are encoded on a single, shared scale.

## Why

The human visual system excels at comparing positions when they share a common scale and are close together. As the distance between them increases (especially perpendicular to the scale), the task becomes more difficult and error-prone. The brain must rely more on working memory to "hold" a value from one location and compare it to another, a process that is less accurate than direct perception.

### Core Principle

Minimize the cognitive distance for comparison. The less the eye has to travel and the less the brain has to remember, the more accurate the judgment.

## When it applies

- Anytime a user needs to compare quantitative values that are not directly adjacent.
- When designing small multiples, faceted displays, or multi-panel dashboards.
- When comparing points on two separate line or bar charts.

## Exceptions

- **Clutter:** Separating charts is often necessary for clarity, especially with many overlapping data series or complex individual charts. In these cases, the loss of direct comparability is an acceptable trade-off for reduced clutter and improved readability of each individual chart.

## Trade-offs

- **Clarity vs. Comparability:** Placing charts closer together or overlaying them can increase clutter and make individual series harder to read. Separating them improves clarity for each chart but makes comparison between them harder.

## Signs of Trouble

- **"Ruler-Waving":** Do viewers try to use their cursor or finger as a ruler to drag a level from one chart to another? This is a clear sign that the visual comparison is too difficult.
- **"Head-Bobbing":** Are viewers repeatedly looking back and forth between two charts to make a simple comparison? This indicates high cognitive load.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add a Guideline.** Draw a faint horizontal line or a shaded region across the small multiples at a key reference point (e.g., the average, a target value, or zero). This provides a shared visual anchor that aids comparison.
- **Moderate Redesign: Reorder the Facets.** If the layout is flexible, reorder your small multiples to place the two most important charts for comparison directly next to each other.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Overlay the Data.** If the scales are compatible and it won't create too much visual clutter, overlay the different data series on a single chart. This forces them to share a common scale, making comparison the most accurate.
