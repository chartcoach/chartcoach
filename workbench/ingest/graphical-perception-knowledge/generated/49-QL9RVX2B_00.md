## Guidance

For tasks requiring comparison of parts to a whole with a small number of categories, use chart types other than treemaps, such as bar charts or pie charts.

## Why

Humans are less accurate and slower at judging and comparing the relative areas of nested rectangles (as in a squarified treemap) compared to angles in a pie chart or lengths in a bar chart for simple composition tasks. A 2019 study found no evidence that treemaps perform better than pie charts in this context; in fact, they performed significantly worse in both accuracy and speed.

### Core Principle

Choose visual encodings that are optimized for the primary analytical task. For accurate magnitude comparison, length and position along a common scale (bar charts) are superior to area (treemaps).

## When it applies

- When showing a part-to-whole relationship with fewer than 10-15 categories.
- The primary task is for viewers to compare or estimate the size of specific parts (e.g., "Which segment is largest?" or "How does segment A compare to segment B?").
- The data is not hierarchical.

## Exceptions

- **Hierarchical Data:** Treemaps are designed for and excel at visualizing hierarchical data (data with parent-child relationships). If you need to show both the part-to-whole relationship *and* the underlying hierarchy, a treemap is the appropriate choice. This guideline applies only when using a treemap as a direct alternative to a pie chart for non-hierarchical data.
- **Very Large Number of Categories:** For visualizing the composition of hundreds or thousands of items where individual comparison is not the goal, a treemap can be a space-efficient way to give a high-level impression of the distribution.

## Trade-offs

- While less perceptually effective for comparison, treemaps are more space-efficient than bar charts and avoid the negative stigma sometimes associated with pie charts. However, this aesthetic or spatial advantage comes at the direct cost of viewer accuracy and speed of interpretation.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Difficult Comparisons:** Viewers struggle to accurately rank the sizes of the rectangles, especially for non-adjacent rectangles or those with different aspect ratios.
- **Slow Reading:** It takes viewers a long time to find and estimate the value of a specific segment.
- **Misleading Insights:** Viewers draw incorrect conclusions about the relative proportions of the categories.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** Add large, clear labels with the percentage or value to each rectangle in the treemap. This provides a "cognitive escape hatch," allowing users to read the values directly instead of relying on flawed area perception.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Pie or Donut Chart.** For a small number of slices, a pie chart is more perceptually effective and faster to read than a treemap for part-to-whole tasks, according to the study.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use a Bar Chart.** The most effective solution is to switch to a simple bar chart, with bars sorted from largest to smallest. This uses length along a common baseline, the most accurate encoding for this type of comparison task.
