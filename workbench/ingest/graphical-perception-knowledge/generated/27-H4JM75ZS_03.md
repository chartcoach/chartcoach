## Guidance

When using shape to encode categorical data, select shapes from different perceptual families (e.g., filled vs. open, geometric vs. line-based, round vs. angular) to maximize their discriminability. Avoid using shapes that are too similar, such as a circle and a square, or a plus and an 'x'.

## Why

Not all shapes are created equal in terms of perceptual distance. Viewers naturally group shapes based on shared properties like "pointiness," "roundness," "filled-ness," or line-based structure. Shapes from within the same group are perceptually closer and therefore easier to confuse. By selecting a diverse set of shapes from different groups, you maximize the perceptual distance between your categories, making the chart easier and faster to read and reducing the risk of misinterpretation.

### Core Principle

Maximize the perceptual discriminability of visual encodings used for different categories. The harder it is to tell two categories apart, the higher the cognitive load and the greater the chance of error.

## When it applies

- In scatter plots or strip plots where shape is used to distinguish different categories of points.
- In maps where different types of locations are marked with different glyphs (proportional symbol maps).
- Any chart using a discrete shape legend, especially when there are 3-7 categories. (Beyond ~7 categories, shape becomes very difficult to use effectively).

## Exceptions

- **When shapes have semantic meaning:** If you are using universally recognized symbols (e.g., a male and female icon, an airplane and a car icon), their semantic meaning will override purely perceptual grouping. In this case, using the semantically correct shape is more important than maximizing perceptual distance.
- **Redundant encoding:** If shape is used as a secondary, redundant encoding alongside a stronger channel like color, the discriminability of the shapes is less critical. However, easily distinguishable shapes are still preferable.

## Trade-offs

- **Aesthetics vs. Clarity:** A set of highly uniform shapes (e.g., all filled circles of different colors) might appear cleaner or more aesthetically pleasing than a motley collection of different shapes. The latter, however, will be more robust and accessible, especially for users with color vision deficiency who may rely on shape.
- **Number of categories:** It is difficult to find more than about 5-7 shapes that are both highly discriminable and not overly complex. This limits the utility of shape as a primary encoding for data with high cardinality.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Squint and Confuse" Test:** Squint at your chart. Do the different categories of shapes blur into a single, indistinguishable group?
- **Similar Silhouettes:** The shapes you've chosen have very similar overall envelopes (e.g., a circle and a square of the same size, or a plus and an x).
- **Frequent Legend Checks:** You or your users have to constantly refer back to the legend to remember which shape corresponds to which category.
- **Small Multiples Inconsistency:** You have a small multiple plot where a specific shape seems to change its appearance or becomes hard to identify in some of the panels.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Diversify One Shape.** If you are using two similar shapes (e.g., circle and square), change one to be from a different perceptual group. For example, change the square to a triangle (different angles) or a plus sign (line-based).
- **Moderate Redesign: Create a Perceptually Diverse Palette.** Audit your shape palette. Ensure you have a mix of:
    - **Filled vs. Open:** (● vs. ○)
    - **Angular vs. Round:** (▲ vs. ●)
    - **Symmetrical vs. Asymmetrical/Oriented:** (+ vs. ►)
    - **Line-based vs. Solid:** (x vs. ■)
- **Comprehensive Approach: Re-order and Prioritize.** Based on the re-ordered palette from Demiralp et al.'s findings (Figure 16), prioritize using the most distinct shapes first for your most important categories. Their recommended order for high discriminability starts with: `+, ▷, □, ×, ▽, <, *`. This provides a research-backed starting point for a highly effective shape palette.
