## Guidance

When encoding ordinal data (ordered categories like "Low, Medium, High" or "Bad, Neutral, Good"), use visual channels that have a natural perceptual order. The most effective channels are:

1.  **Position** (e.g., ordering categories along an axis)
2.  **Color Saturation / Lightness** (e.g., a sequential palette from light to dark)
3.  **Color Hue** (only if the hues form a clear sequential progression)
4.  **Texture** (e.g., from sparse to dense)

Avoid using unordered channels like shape or a qualitative (rainbow) color palette.

## Why

Ordinal data has an inherent sequence, and the visualization should reflect that. The human visual system naturally perceives a progression in lightness or saturation (from light to dark) and density (from sparse to dense). However, a "rainbow" of different hues (e.g., red, green, blue) does not have a universally agreed-upon order, forcing viewers to memorize the legend and increasing cognitive load. Matching the data's ordered structure to a visually ordered encoding makes the chart intuitive and easier to interpret.

### Core Principle

**Match the visual structure to the data structure.** Ordered data should be represented by perceptually ordered visual variables.

## When it applies

-   Visualizing survey data with Likert scales (e.g., Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree).
-   Encoding any ordered categories, such as "Small, Medium, Large" or "Bronze, Silver, Gold".
-   Choosing a color palette for a choropleth map or heatmap that displays binned, ordered data.

## Exceptions

-   If the ordinal categories also have a very strong, conventional color association (e.g., "cold" to "hot" with a blue-to-red diverging scale), a multi-hue palette can be effective. However, this relies on cultural conventions and is not universally applicable.

## Trade-offs

-   **Discriminability vs. Order:** A single-hue sequential palette is great for showing order, but it can be harder to distinguish between adjacent steps than a palette with multiple hues. You are trading some discriminability for a clearer sense of order.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Rainbows for Ranks:** A "rainbow" or qualitative color palette is used to encode clearly ordered data.
-   **Legend Whiplash:** The viewer has to constantly look back and forth between the chart and the legend to understand the order of the categories.
-gaps in the logic.
-   **The "Fruit Salad" Effect:** The colors on the chart look like a random assortment of hues with no clear progression, despite the underlying data having a distinct order.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Switch to a Sequential Palette.** Change your color scheme from a qualitative (rainbow) palette to a sequential one. Most visualization tools have built-in "single-hue" or "sequential" color palettes (e.g., light blue to dark blue).

-   **Moderate Redesign: Use a Perceptually Uniform Palette.** Instead of a basic single-hue ramp, use a perceptually uniform sequential palette (like Viridis, Plasma, or a ColorBrewer palette). These are designed so that a given step in data value corresponds to an equal step in perceived color, making the representation more accurate.

-   **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Position.** If possible, redesign the chart to use position to represent the order. For example, instead of a colored scatterplot, you could use a small-multiples display where each chart represents one of the ordinal categories, arranged in the correct order. This leverages the most effective visual channel for showing order.
