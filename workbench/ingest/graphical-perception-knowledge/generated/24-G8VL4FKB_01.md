## Guidance

For comparing the magnitudes of different components, ensure they share a **common, aligned baseline** (e.g., in a grouped bar chart). Avoid chart types that require viewers to compare unaligned lengths (e.g., the inner segments of a stacked bar or stacked area chart).

## Why

Judgments of position along a common scale are perceptually more accurate and less biased than judgments of length. When objects are not aligned, the viewer's brain must perform a difficult and error-prone length comparison. Providing a shared baseline offloads this cognitive work to the much easier and more accurate task of position judgment.

### Core Principle

Make the most important comparisons the easiest to see. Aligning objects on a common scale enables a more accurate perceptual task.

## When it applies

- When the primary goal for the audience is to compare the individual values of components across different groups (e.g., comparing sales of 'Product B' in 2022 vs. 2023).
- In stacked bar charts, stacked area charts, or any visualization where segments to be compared do not start from the same axis.

## Exceptions

- **Comparing Totals:** If the primary task is to compare the *total* of the stacks, a stacked bar chart is effective, as the totals themselves share a common baseline.
- **Showing Composition:** If the goal is mainly to show the part-to-whole composition of each bar, and precise comparison of the inner parts is a secondary, less important goal.

## Trade-offs

- **Space:** Grouped bar charts typically use more horizontal space than stacked bar charts.
- **Total Obscurity:** Grouped bar charts make it harder to see the total for each group, whereas this is the primary strength of a stacked bar chart.

## Signs of Trouble

- **"Mental Calipers":** Do you find yourself trying to visually 'copy and paste' a segment from one stack to another to compare them? This is a sign you're being forced into a difficult length comparison.
- **Hidden Trends:** Is it difficult to tell if a specific category (e.g., the second segment from the bottom) is growing or shrinking across the stacks? If the trend isn't obvious, the chart is failing.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Annotations.** Add direct value labels to the key segments you want to compare. This lets users compare the numbers, bypassing the perceptual difficulty.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Grouped Bar Chart.** This places every bar on the same baseline, making component comparisons direct and accurate.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Small Multiples.** Create a separate, simple bar chart for each component category. This isolates the comparison for each component and makes its trend across groups very easy to see, at the cost of using more space.
