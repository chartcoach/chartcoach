## Guidance

To help viewers quickly and accurately identify the single largest change between two datasets, use an animated transition that morphs the first chart into the second.

## Why

During an animated transition, the velocity (speed) of each moving mark becomes a powerful, emergent visual cue. The mark corresponding to the largest data change will move the fastest, causing it to perceptually "pop out" and capture the viewer's attention. This offloads the cognitive work of comparing individual values and instead leverages the human visual system's innate sensitivity to motion.

### Core Principle

Make the most important comparison the easiest to see. By linking the magnitude of change directly to the perceptual salience of motion, you make the "biggest mover" task trivial.

## When it applies

-   When the primary goal is to find the single data point with the maximum delta (increase or decrease) between two states or datasets.
-   This technique is particularly effective for **bar charts** and **donut charts**.
-   When you are comparing only two datasets, as the effect may not scale to multiple simultaneous animations.

## Exceptions

-   **For judging overall similarity (correlation):** Animation is not effective for assessing the overall correlation between two datasets. In the study, it performed poorly for this task.
-   **For slope charts:** Overlaid (static) slope charts were found to be more effective than animation for the "max delta" task. The explicit line slopes in the static version already encode the change effectively.
-   **When multiple changes are large:** If many values change significantly, the resulting complex motion can be disorienting and counterproductive.
-   **For static media:** This technique is impossible in print or static image formats.

## Trade-offs

-   **Ephemeral Nature:** The insight is only available while the animation is playing, requiring user interaction (e.g., a "play" button) and focused attention.
-   **Scalability:** The perceptual benefit degrades quickly as the number of datasets increases beyond two.
-   **Potential for Disorientation:** Large, uncontrolled changes can create a chaotic visual effect that is more distracting than informative.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Comparison Fatigue:** Viewers report having to manually scan and compare values between two static charts to find the biggest difference.
-   **Missed Insights:** Viewers fail to notice the most significant change when presented with side-by-side or stacked charts.
-   **Ineffective Animation:** An animation is used for a correlation task, leading to confusion as viewers try to track multiple movements without a clear perceptual anchor.

## How to Improve

-   **Minimal Change: Add a "Highlight Difference" Feature.** If using static charts, add an interactive element that explicitly calculates and highlights the largest difference on demand. This provides the answer without relying on perception alone.

-   **Moderate Change: Switch to an Overlaid Layout.** If animation is not feasible, change from a side-by-side layout to an overlaid (superposed) chart. This co-locates the data points and was found to be the best static alternative for this task.

-   **Comprehensive Redesign: Implement Animated Transitions.** For screen-based media, replace the static comparison with an animated transition between the two states. Ensure the animation timing (e.g., 1.5 seconds) is brief enough to be perceived as a single event. Use cubic easing to make the maximum velocity more pronounced.

## Guidance

When asking viewers to judge the overall similarity or correlation between two bar charts, arrange them in a mirrored (center-aligned) layout rather than a standard adjacent (side-by-side) layout.

## Why

The human visual system is exceptionally sensitive to bilateral (mirror) symmetry. Arranging two charts as mirror images of each other leverages this powerful, pre-attentive perceptual mechanism. This allows for a more holistic and efficient comparison of the overall pattern between the two charts, making it easier to assess their similarity (correlation) than when the brain has to compare two identical, translated patterns.

### Core Principle

Align your visualization design with the innate strengths of the human visual system. Using mirror symmetry for comparison tasks taps into a highly efficient perceptual pathway.

## When it applies

-   When the primary task is for a viewer to assess the overall **correlation** or pattern similarity between two datasets.
-   When using **bar charts** arranged as small multiples.
-   When comparing exactly two datasets, as the mirrored layout is inherently pairwise.

## Exceptions

-   **For finding a single largest change (max delta):** Other layouts, like animation or overlaid charts, are more effective for this task. While mirrored layouts were slightly better than adjacent layouts, they were not the best for max delta.
-   **When the metaphor is unfamiliar:** A mirrored layout, where one axis is reversed, can be confusing to audiences who are not expecting it. Standard adjacent layouts are more conventional and may be safer for general audiences.
-   **When comparing more than two datasets:** The bilateral symmetry of a mirrored layout does not scale to three or more datasets.

## Trade-offs

-   **Conventionality vs. Performance:** You may sacrifice the familiarity of a standard side-by-side layout for a modest but significant gain in perceptual performance.
-   **Axis Complexity:** One of the two charts will have a reversed axis, which requires a moment of cognitive re-orientation for the viewer.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Inaccurate Judgments:** Viewers struggle to correctly identify which of two chart pairs is more "similar" or "correlated."
-   **Slow Comparisons:** Viewers take a long time to compare two side-by-side charts, often scanning back and forth between individual bars.
-   **Over-reliance on Calculation:** Viewers feel the need to estimate individual values to judge similarity, rather than perceiving the pattern holistically.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Reduce Noise.** If using a standard adjacent layout, ensure that the means and standard deviations of the data in the charts are comparable, as this was a control in the experiment to isolate the perception of correlation.

-   **Moderate Redesign: Implement a Mirrored Layout.** Modify your side-by-side bar chart layout. Keep the left-hand chart as is (e.g., with the y-axis on the left and bars growing to the right). For the right-hand chart, place its y-axis on the right and have its bars grow to the left, towards the center. This creates the mirrored effect.

-   **Comprehensive Redesign: Offer Layout Choices.** If building an interactive tool, allow users to toggle between adjacent and mirrored layouts. This empowers them to choose the conventional layout for general viewing and switch to the higher-performance mirrored layout for focused correlation analysis.

## Guidance

If animation is not an option, use an overlaid (superposed) layout to help viewers find the largest change between two datasets. This is especially effective for slope charts and line charts.

## Why

Placing data from two series in the same coordinate space (co-location) dramatically reduces the cognitive and perceptual load of comparison. Viewers do not need to shift their gaze back and forth between charts or hold values in working memory. Instead, they can directly perceive the difference between marks (e.g., the distance between two lines or the ends of two bars) within their focal area, a much faster and more accurate perceptual task.

### Core Principle

Reduce the cognitive work required to decode your visualization. By minimizing eye movements and memory load, you make comparisons more direct, efficient, and accurate.

## When it applies

-   When the task is to find the single data point with the **maximum change** between two datasets.
-   When using **static media** (print, images) or when animation is otherwise not feasible.
-   This technique is highly effective for **slope charts** and **line charts**, and is the best static option for **bar charts**.

## Exceptions

-   **Chart Clutter:** Overlaying can lead to a cluttered and illegible chart if the two series frequently cross over each other (occlusion) or if you are comparing more than two or three series.
-   **For judging correlation:** Mirrored small multiples were found to be more effective for judging correlation in bar charts. The benefit of overlaying is specific to discrete value comparison.
-   **When animation is possible:** For bar charts and donut charts, animation was found to be even more effective than overlaying for the max delta task.

## Trade-offs

-   **Potential Occlusion:** One data series can obscure another, especially in dense charts. This can be mitigated with transparency or by using different mark types (e.g., line vs. dots).
-   **Color Dependency:** This layout relies on color or another visual channel to distinguish the two series, making it vulnerable to issues of color vision deficiency if colors are not chosen carefully.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Chart Sprawl:** You are using two separate charts (side-by-side or stacked) where one overlaid chart would suffice, forcing users to do extra work.
-   **"Chart Spaghetti":** An overlaid line chart is so cluttered with crossing lines that it's impossible to follow any single series.
-   **Memory Strain:** Viewers complain they "lose their place" when looking back and forth between two separate charts to compare them.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Use Guiding Lines.** If you must keep charts separate, add subtle guiding lines or hover effects that connect corresponding points across the charts to aid comparison.

-   **Moderate Redesign: Combine into an Overlaid Chart.** Redesign your visualization to combine the two separate charts into one. Use distinct, perceptually separated colors (e.g., blue and orange) to represent the two series. For bar charts, you can place the bars for each category next to each other (grouped bar chart) or overlay them with transparency. For line charts, simply draw both lines on the same axes.

-   **Comprehensive Approach: Interactive Toggling.** In an interactive context, start with separate charts (small multiples) to give an overview of each, but allow the user to click a button to merge them into a temporary overlaid view for focused comparison.

## Guidance

When using small multiples for comparing values, arrange them side-by-side (adjacent) rather than one above the other (stacked). An adjacent layout ensures corresponding marks share a common baseline, which is critical for accurate perception.

## Why

Humans are far more accurate at comparing the positions or lengths of objects when they are aligned along a common scale or baseline. When charts are stacked vertically, corresponding data points (e.g., bars for the same category) are horizontally separated and unaligned. This forces the viewer to make a difficult and error-prone judgment of length or position without a shared reference point. An adjacent (side-by-side) layout aligns these reference points, making the comparison task much easier and more accurate.

### Core Principle

Position on a common scale is the most accurate visual encoding for quantitative comparison. Design your layouts to maximize the use of common baselines.

## When it applies

-   When using small multiples (bar charts, line charts, etc.) where the primary goal is to **compare** specific values or judge **correlation** between the charts.
-   Any time you need viewers to make precise judgments between corresponding data points in different charts.

## Exceptions

-   **When comparison is secondary:** If the main goal is simply to show the individual trend or distribution of many different series, and direct comparison is not critical, stacking might be an acceptable use of vertical space.
-   **Extreme space constraints:** If you have very wide charts and extremely limited horizontal space, stacking might be the only option. In this case, acknowledge that you are sacrificing comparative accuracy for layout constraints.

## Trade-offs

-   **Space Usage:** Adjacent layouts consume more horizontal space than stacked layouts. Stacked layouts are more space-efficient vertically. You are trading screen real estate for perceptual accuracy.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Unaligned Baselines:** In your small multiples layout, the bars or points you want users to compare do not start at the same position.
-   **Viewer Complaints:** Users say it's "hard to tell the difference" or that they have to "use a ruler" to compare your charts.
-   **Slow Task Performance:** It takes users a long time to answer simple comparison questions about your charts.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Re-orient the Layout.** Change your layout from `flex-direction: column` to `flex-direction: row`. Arrange the small multiples horizontally (side-by-side) instead of vertically (stacked). This simple change will immediately align the baselines and improve comparability.

-   **Moderate Approach: Add Guiding Lines.** If you absolutely must use a stacked layout due to space constraints, add interactive guidelines. For example, on hover, draw a faint horizontal line across all charts at the level of the hovered mark to provide a temporary common scale.

-   **Comprehensive Approach: Rethink the Chart Type.** If comparing many series is the goal, a stacked small multiple layout may be the wrong approach entirely. Consider an alternative visualization like a dot plot, a slope chart, or an overlaid line chart that places all data on a common scale from the outset.
