## Guidance

When the primary task is for a user to understand the rank or relative order of items, sort the bar chart by its quantitative value (e.g., from highest to lowest).

## Why

A sorted order offloads the cognitive work of scanning and comparing from the user to the chart itself. Instead of mentally ranking multiple items, the user can simply use a bar's position to determine its rank. Research shows this leads to faster, more accurate, and less error-prone judgments compared to unsorted (e.g., alphabetical) arrangements.

### Core Principle

Make the most important comparisons the easiest to see. By physically ordering the data, you make rank comparisons effortless and reduce the cognitive load required to interpret the chart.

## When it applies

- When a user's goal is to see which items are at the top, in the middle, or at the bottom of a distribution.
- For tasks like "find the top 5" or "see where this item ranks."
- When the number of bars is large enough (> 5-7) to make manual scanning difficult.

## Exceptions

- **When an intrinsic order is more important:** Do not sort if there is a conventional, more meaningful order to the categories, such as time (e.g., days of the week, months), or a standard sequence (e.g., age groups, Likert scale responses). In these cases, the inherent order of the data takes precedence.

## Trade-offs

- **Lookup vs. Comparison:** Sorting by value makes ranking easy but can make it harder to look up a specific, known item (which is easier in an alphabetically sorted list). This trade-off is most pronounced in static charts.
- **Audience Expectation:** If an audience is accustomed to a specific non-sorted order (e.g., geographical), changing it may cause initial confusion.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Alphabetical Trap:** The bars are sorted alphabetically, but the user's questions are about performance or magnitude (e.g., "Who's the top salesperson?").
- **Scanning Back and Forth:** You observe users scanning the entire chart back and forth repeatedly to find the highest or lowest values.
- **User Questions:** Users ask "Which one is highest?" or "Where does X fall in the ranking?" for an unsorted chart, indicating the design is not supporting their task.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Sort the Chart.** The most direct improvement is to reorder the bars in your visualization tool from largest to smallest (or vice versa). This single change dramatically improves usability for rank-related tasks.

- **Moderate Approach: Add a Search/Highlight Feature.** If both easy lookup (alphabetical) and ranking (sorted) are important in an interactive context, default to a sorted view but include an interactive search or highlight feature. This allows users to find a specific category of interest within the sorted list.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Use a Different Chart.** If you must preserve a non-sortable order (e.g., time) but ranking is also critical, a simple bar chart may not be the best tool. Consider a slope graph to show rank changes over time, or a connected dot plot (dumbbell plot) to show the change between two points for each category.
