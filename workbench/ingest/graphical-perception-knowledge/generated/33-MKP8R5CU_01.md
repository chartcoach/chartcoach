## Guidance

Be aware that when people estimate the rank of a bar in an unsorted chart, their perception is often biased toward the middle (the median rank). They tend to underestimate the rank of high-performing bars and overestimate the rank of low-performing ones.

## Why

This is likely due to a cognitive heuristic called "anchoring and adjustment" and the statistical phenomenon of "regression to the mean." Viewers may implicitly anchor their judgment on the 50th percentile (the visual "average" bar) and then insufficiently adjust their estimate up or down. This cognitive shortcut pulls their perception of extreme ranks closer to the middle. Research also shows that data characteristics like skewness have a strong influence, further contributing to large estimation errors.

### Core Principle

Human perception is not a perfect measurement tool; it is subject to systematic biases. Good design anticipates and mitigates these biases to ensure the intended message is received accurately.

## When it applies

- When displaying unsorted bar charts where the viewer needs to estimate the rank of items, especially those at the high or low end of the scale.
- When the underlying data distribution is highly skewed, as this amplifies estimation errors.

## Exceptions

- This bias is largely eliminated when the bar chart is **sorted by value**. Sorting transforms the task from a difficult perceptual estimation into a simple positional lookup, making the rank explicit and removing the need for judgment.

## Trade-offs

- The main trade-off comes from the solutions. Directly labeling bars adds clutter. Sorting the chart may disrupt a meaningful alphabetical or chronological order, making item lookup harder.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Unsorted Extremes:** An unsorted bar chart is being used to highlight a top or bottom performer. The viewer's takeaway of *how much* of a top performer it is may be muted by this perceptual bias.
- **High Precision Needed:** The chart is used in a context where small differences in perceived rank are critical (e.g., comparing rankings before and after an intervention), yet the bars are unsorted.
- **Highly Skewed Data:** The chart visualizes a dataset with high skewness (a long tail), which the source research found to be a dominant factor in causing large estimation errors.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add a Reference Line.** Add a reference line for the median (50th percentile) or average. This gives viewers a more explicit anchor for their judgments, though it does not eliminate the bias.

- **Moderate Approach: Add Direct Labels.** For key bars of interest (especially top and bottom performers), add direct labels for their values and/or ranks. This provides an "escape hatch" from perceptual estimation, allowing users to read the exact information.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Sort the Chart.** The most effective solution is to sort the bar chart by value. This completely eliminates the need for rank estimation and makes the rank of every bar clear through its position.
