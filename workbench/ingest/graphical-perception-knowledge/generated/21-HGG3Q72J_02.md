## Guidance

Choose your visual encoding based on the viewer's primary task. Use a color-based encoding (like a heatmap or "colorfield") for summary tasks like judging the average value or variance. Use a position-based encoding (like a line or bar chart) for identification tasks like finding the highest/lowest value or determining the range.

## Why

The human visual system appears to process color and position differently. Color seems to facilitate a more "global" or summative judgment, allowing for rapid estimation of the statistical properties of a collection (e.g., the average color of a region). Position, on the other hand, is better suited for identifying specific points, boundaries, and shapes, making it easy to locate peaks and troughs. Choosing the encoding that matches the task leads to more accurate and efficient interpretation.

### Core Principle

The effectiveness of a visual channel is not absolute; it is dependent on the task the viewer is trying to perform. Design requires matching the strengths of an encoding to the demands of the task.

## When it applies

- When visualizing dense data, such as time series or 2D spatial data.
- When you must decide between showing data as a line chart versus a heatmap.
- When designing interactive systems where a user might switch between wanting to see an overview (summary) and wanting to find specific details (identification).

## Exceptions

- This guideline is most relevant when there is a clear primary task. If summary and identification are equally important, a design that combines both (e.g., a line chart with a colored background indicating a range) or allows toggling between views may be necessary.
- For very sparse data, the benefits of a colorfield for summarization may be diminished as there are not enough data points to form a clear "ensemble."

## Trade-offs

- **Prioritizing one task over another:** By choosing color for a summary task, you make identification tasks (like finding the max value) harder. By choosing position for an identification task, you may make summary judgments less accurate.
- **Familiarity:** Line charts are generally more familiar to a general audience than heatmaps for time-series data, which might be a reason to prefer them even if the primary task is summarization.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Hunting for Peaks in a Heatmap:** Viewers are struggling to find the single highest or lowest value in a heatmap, scanning back and forth across the color legend.
- **"Eyeballing" Averages in a Line Chart:** Viewers are trying to estimate the average value of a highly volatile line chart, leading to inaccurate guesses.
- **Task Mismatch:** You have provided a line chart but the main question is "What was the average value in June?" or you have provided a heatmap and the main question is "On what day did we hit our peak?"

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Annotations.** If your chart's encoding is mismatched to the task, add annotations or labels that explicitly state the answer. For example, add a label for the "Peak Value" on a heatmap, or draw a "Monthly Average" line on a line chart.
- **Moderate Approach: Provide an Alternative View.** In an interactive context, provide a button or control that allows the user to switch between a position-based view (line chart) and a color-based view (heatmap) of the same data.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Choose the Encoding Matched to the Primary Task.** During the design phase, explicitly define the most critical task the viewer must accomplish. If it is summarization, choose a color-based encoding. If it is identification, choose a position-based encoding.
