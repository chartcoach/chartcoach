## Guidance

When encoding a third variable with size or color lightness in a scatterplot, recognize that the perceptual bias in judging the mean will be strongest when the third variable is highly correlated with the x and y variables.

## Why

When size or lightness is correlated with position, the most salient (largest or darkest) points naturally cluster together in one region of the chart. This grouping creates a strong, singular "center of gravity" that powerfully pulls the viewer's perceived average towards that cluster. This maximizes the "weighted average illusion," leading to the largest and most misleading errors in judgment.

## When it applies

- This is a condition of the data itself, not just a design choice. This guidance applies whenever you visualize trivariate data where the variable encoded by size or lightness is highly correlated (positively or negatively) with the variables encoded by position.
- This is especially critical in exploratory analysis, where the user may not be aware of the underlying correlations and is most vulnerable to being misled.

## Exceptions

- There are no exceptions where the bias disappears under these conditions. The only reason to ignore this guidance is if judging the unweighted mean is completely irrelevant to the chart's purpose and there is no risk of viewers attempting to do so.

## Trade-offs

- Showing highly correlated trivariate data in a single scatterplot is inherently risky. The primary alternative, using small multiples, requires more screen space and makes it harder for a viewer to perceive the overall three-way relationship at a glance. You trade single-chart simplicity for multi-chart perceptual accuracy.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Up-and-to-the-Right" Problem:** Do the marks in your chart get progressively larger or darker as you move from the bottom-left to the top-right? This is a classic sign of high positive correlation across all three variables and a major red flag for this bias.
- **One-Sided Salience:** Is one corner or side of the chart dominated by large or dark marks, while the opposite side contains all the small or light marks? This visual imbalance is a direct indicator of the data condition that causes the strongest bias.

## How to Improve

- **Acknowledge and Annotate (Mitigation).** If you must use a single chart, the most responsible action is to add explicit reference lines for the true unweighted mean and add an annotation that warns the viewer about the potential for bias. For example: "Note: The true average is shown by the solid lines; the visual average may appear higher due to the concentration of large circles in the top right."

- **Use Small Multiples (Elimination).** This is the most effective solution. Facet the data by quantiles of the third variable (e.g., binning into "small," "medium," and "large" sizes) and create separate plots for each group. This breaks the perceptual link between size/lightness and position, removing the source of the bias and allowing for accurate judgment of the mean within each subgroup.
