## Guidance

In a single plot with multiple categories encoded by shape, avoid using shapes that are all from the "open" family (e.g., asterisk and plus) or all from the "closed" family (e.g., circle and square).

## Why

Shapes that share a strong perceptual characteristic like "closedness" or "openness" create more interference for the visual system. It's harder for a viewer to tell them apart at a glance, which increases cognitive load and leads to slower, more error-prone interpretation.

### Core Principle

Reduce the cognitive work required to decode your visualization. Forcing viewers to distinguish between two perceptually similar shapes is an unnecessary mental step that can be easily avoided.

## When it applies

- When selecting two or more shapes to represent different categories in a single, dense visualization like a scatterplot.
- Especially for tasks like comparing counts (numerosity) or identifying trends within specific categories, where clear separation is vital.

## Exceptions

- If aesthetics or brand guidelines strictly require using a specific family of shapes (e.g., all geometric/closed). In this case, be aware of the performance cost and consider using another, stronger visual channel like color to reinforce the distinction.
- For very sparse, clearly separated data where perceptual interference is not a significant risk.

## Trade-offs

- Following this rule means you cannot create a purely "geometric" (all closed) or "symbolic" (all open) aesthetic for your chart's shapes.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Viewer Complaints:** Users report that "the circles and squares look too much alike" or they have to "stare for a while" to tell the groups apart.
- **High Error Rates:** When asked to compare the categories, users frequently make mistakes.
- **Back-and-Forth:** Viewers have to repeatedly look between the legend and the chart to confirm which shape corresponds to which category, indicating the shapes are not easily distinguishable.

## How to Improve

- **Simple Substitution:** Replace one of the shapes with an equivalent from the opposite category. For example, if using a circle and a square (both closed), change the square to a cross (open). This immediately breaks the perceptual grouping and reduces interference.

- **Add a Redundant Encoding:** If you must use same-category shapes, add a secondary visual cue like color to help distinguish them. For example, use blue circles and orange squares. Note that this does not eliminate the shape interference but can help mitigate it.
