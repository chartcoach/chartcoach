## Guidance

On visualizations with a light or white background, map larger quantitative values to darker colors. This is often called a "dark-is-more" encoding.

## Why

This approach aligns with two powerful, innate perceptual biases:
1.  **Dark-is-more bias:** People naturally associate darker colors with greater quantity or density.
2.  **Opaque-is-more bias:** People associate colors with higher contrast against the background as representing greater quantity.

On a light background, darker colors are also higher-contrast (more "opaque"). By making darker colors represent larger values, you align with both biases, making the visualization intuitive and significantly faster for viewers to process.

### Core Principle

Make your visual encoding align with viewers' inherent perceptual biases to reduce cognitive load and speed up interpretation.

## When it applies

-   This is a strong default for any visualization using a sequential color scale on a light background.
-   It is particularly relevant for **heatmaps** and **choropleth maps** where color lightness is used to encode quantitative data.
-   Applies when the viewer's task is to quickly assess where quantities are high or low (e.g., an aggregation or summary task).

## Exceptions

-   If a specific domain has a deeply ingrained and opposite convention (e.g., some fields in neuroscience or radiology use "light-is-more" to represent higher activity). Even in these cases, be aware that it may slow down interpretation for audiences outside that domain.
-   For diverging color scales, where the lightest color represents a meaningful midpoint (e.g., zero) and darker colors represent distance from that midpoint in two directions.

## Trade-offs

-   There are no significant perceptual trade-offs when using a light background; this is the most effective and conventional approach.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Legend Re-Checking:** Viewers have to repeatedly look back and forth between the legend and the chart to remember that lighter colors mean "more."
-   **Hesitation:** There's a noticeable pause as viewers interpret the chart, suggesting they are resolving a cognitive conflict.
-   **Inverted Legend:** The legend on your light-background chart shows the lightest color at the top labeled "Greater" or "More."

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Reverse the Scale.** Most visualization tools have a "reverse scale" option. If your colormap is currently 'light-is-more', simply reverse it.
-   **Comprehensive Approach: Choose a Sequential Scale.** When creating the visualization, explicitly select a sequential colormap and ensure its direction is set so that the darkest end of the scale corresponds to the highest values in your data.

## Guidance

When a visualization must be robustly readable on both light and dark backgrounds (e.g., in a slide template), use a "dark-is-more" encoding with a colormap that does **not** appear to vary in opacity. These are typically multi-hue scales (like Viridis) or complex single-hue scales that curve through color space, rather than simple linear gradients to white or black.

## Why

Simple linear gradient colormaps (e.g., white-to-blue) look like a transparent overlay. This activates an "opaque-is-more" perceptual bias.
-   On a **light background**, this bias reinforces the "dark-is-more" bias, making interpretation easy.
-   On a **dark background**, the biases conflict: the lightest colors are now the most "opaque" (highest contrast), but the "dark-is-more" bias still persists. This conflict increases cognitive load and slows down interpretation.

Colormaps that don't look like transparent overlays (e.g., Viridis, Magma, MATLAB's 'Hot') suppress the "opaque-is-more" bias. This leaves the "dark-is-more" bias as the single, consistent cue, making the chart's interpretation stable regardless of the background color.

### Core Principle

Design for predictable interpretation by isolating a single, dominant perceptual cue and minimizing conflicting ones, especially when the viewing context is variable.

## When it applies

-   When creating charts for templates, design systems, or reports that may be displayed on both light and dark themes.
-   When creating a visualization component intended for reuse in multiple contexts.
-   When you want to ensure your colormap is interpreted consistently by all viewers, regardless of their display settings (e.g., light/dark mode).

## Exceptions

-   If your visualization is guaranteed to *only* ever be displayed on a known light background. In this case, a simple linear gradient is effective and sufficient.
-   When the specific design goal *is* to evoke transparency (e.g., visualizing uncertainty with an alpha channel).

## Trade-offs

-   Robust, multi-hue colormaps might be slightly less optimized for a single, specific background compared to a scale designed perfectly for it. You sacrifice peak performance in one context for strong, consistent performance in all contexts.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The Inversion Effect:** A chart is clear on a white slide but becomes confusing or feels "inverted" when copy-pasted onto a dark slide.
-   **Context-Dependent Legend:** The "correct" way to read the chart seems to change depending on the background color.
-   **Linear Gradients:** Your colormap is a simple, linear ramp from white to a single color, or from a color to black. This type of scale is at high risk for the inversion effect on dark backgrounds.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Lock the Background.** If you must use a simple linear gradient colormap, constrain its use to a light background only. Prohibit its use on dark backgrounds.

-   **Moderate Approach: Test Your Palette.** Before finalizing, place your visualization on both a white and a black background. If the interpretation feels like it's inverting or becoming ambiguous on the black background, your colormap is not robust.

-   **Comprehensive Approach: Use Perceptually Uniform Colormaps.** Replace simple linear gradients with perceptually uniform, multi-hue colormaps like **Viridis**, **Plasma**, **Magma**, **Inferno**, or **Cividis**. These are specifically designed to be robust against changes in lightness and are standard in libraries like Matplotlib and D3. Ensure you still map them "dark-is-more" (e.g., Viridis goes from yellow (light) to purple (dark)).
