## Guidance

For tasks requiring the comparison of multiple time series at a single, specific point in time (a "local" comparison), use a shared-space visualization like a simple line chart where all series are overlaid in the same plotting area.

## Why

Shared-space charts place all data series on a common set of axes, allowing for direct visual comparison with minimal eye movement. To compare values at a single point in time, the viewer only needs to scan vertically. This is perceptually and cognitively more efficient than scanning across multiple, separate charts (as in small multiples), which requires more time and working memory to hold and compare values.

### Core Principle

Make the most important comparisons the easiest to see. For local comparisons, proximity is key, and shared-space charts maximize proximity.

## When it applies

- The primary task is a **local comparison**, such as "Find the series with the highest value in Q3" or "Compare the values for Series A and B at this specific event."
- The number of time series is relatively low (e.g., under 8), where visual clutter and overlap do not completely obscure the data at the point of interest.

## Exceptions

- When the number of time series is very high, causing excessive overlap (a "spaghetti chart") that makes it impossible to distinguish the lines, even at a single point. In this case, interactivity (like hover) or a split-space approach may be necessary.
- If the individual shape and context of each time series are more important than the direct comparison, a split-space view like small multiples might be better even for local tasks.

## Trade-offs

- **Prioritizes speed for local tasks:** This approach is optimized for speed and accuracy in point-in-time comparisons.
- **Sacrifices clarity for global tasks:** The potential for line overlap and clutter makes it harder to follow individual series across their entire length or to compare dispersed trends.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Spaghetti Chart:** The chart is a tangled mess of overlapping lines, making it difficult to distinguish one series from another.
- **Back-and-Forth:** Viewers have to repeatedly look between a legend and the chart to identify which line is which, especially if colors are not easily distinguishable.
- **Task Mismatch:** The chart is used for a dispersed comparison task (e.g., "Which series had the greatest overall growth?"), and viewers struggle to trace individual lines through the clutter.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Interactivity.** If you must use a shared-space chart with many series, add hover tooltips that highlight a single series and display its value. This helps resolve ambiguity at specific points without changing the layout.

- **Moderate Approach: Emphasize Key Series.** Use color and line weight to draw attention to the most important series, while de-emphasizing others (e.g., by making them gray and thin). This preserves the direct comparison for the key series while reducing overall clutter.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Switch to a Split-Space Chart.** If the primary tasks involve dispersed comparisons or tracking individual series, break the chart into small multiples. This is a fundamental change but will significantly improve performance for those tasks.

## Guidance

For tasks requiring the comparison of trends, shapes, or values across a wide span of a time series (a "dispersed" comparison), use a split-space technique like small multiples. Give each time series its own dedicated chart, arranged in a grid.

## Why

Overlaid line charts become cluttered and difficult to read as the number of series increases or as lines cross frequently. This "spaghetti" effect makes it perceptually and cognitively demanding to follow a single series' trajectory. Small multiples eliminate this visual clutter by giving each series its own space. This allows the viewer to easily perceive the shape and trend of each series individually, and then compare them across charts.

### Core Principle

Reduce cognitive load by preventing visual clutter. When tracking individual items is important, separation is more effective than superposition.

## When it applies

- The primary task is a **dispersed comparison**, such as "Which series showed the most volatility?" or "Compare the overall trend of Series A to Series B."
- The number of time series is moderate to high, where overlap would be a significant problem in a shared-space chart.
- You want to encourage comparison of the overall shape and pattern of each series.

## Exceptions

- When the primary task is a precise, local comparison at a single point in time. In this case, a shared-space chart is faster because it does not require eye movement between charts.
- When the absolute magnitude and direct comparison of values are more critical than the individual shapes of the series.

## Trade-offs

- **Prioritizes clarity for individual series:** This approach excels at making each series easy to read and follow.
- **Sacrifices precision of direct comparison:** Comparing the exact values of two series at the same point in time requires the viewer to mentally project from one chart to another, which is less accurate and slower than a direct vertical comparison in a shared-space chart.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Chart Overload:** The chart is a single plot with a tangled mess of overlapping, hard-to-distinguish lines (a "spaghetti chart").
- **Task Mismatch:** Viewers are asked to describe the trend of a single series but cannot visually isolate it from the others.
- **Legend-Heavy:** The chart relies on a large, complex color legend, and viewers must constantly refer back to it to understand the chart.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Highlight a Series.** If you must use a shared-space chart, use interactivity or color to allow a user to select and highlight one or more series, pushing all others to the background (e.g., as thin gray lines). This temporarily simulates the clarity of a split-space chart.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Convert to Small Multiples.** Re-design the visualization to use small multiples. Break the single chart into a grid of smaller charts, one for each time series. Ensure all small multiples share the same x-axis and y-axis scales to allow for valid comparison.

## Guidance

To help users judge and compare the overall trend or rate of change (slope) of multiple time series, both simple line graphs and small multiples are effective and efficient choices.

## Why

Estimating a trend involves assessing the overall direction and steepness of a line.
- **Simple line graphs** allow for the direct comparison of slopes within the same coordinate system. If the lines do not overlap excessively, it can be intuitive to see which line is steepest.
- **Small multiples** reduce visual clutter, allowing the viewer to clearly see the shape of each individual trend before making a comparison. This can be particularly helpful when there are many series.

The research shows that both approaches are performant, suggesting the choice can be guided by other factors, such as the number of series and the potential for clutter.

### Core Principle

Match the visualization's complexity to the task's perceptual needs. For trend estimation, simpler representations that clearly show line orientation are superior to complex encodings.

## When it applies

- The primary task is to judge the rate of change or overall trend, such as "Which product had the fastest sales growth?" or "Identify all series with a negative trend."
- You are choosing between different types of line chart visualizations.

## Exceptions

- Neither approach is ideal if there are so many series that a simple line graph becomes an unreadable "spaghetti chart" and a small multiples grid becomes too large to view at once. In such cases, analytical or aggregative methods may be needed.

## Trade-offs

- **Simple Line Graph:**
    - **Pro:** Enables direct, in-place comparison of slopes.
    - **Con:** Can become cluttered with many series, obscuring trends.
- **Small Multiples:**
    - **Pro:** Eliminates clutter, making each trend easy to see individually.
    - **Con:** Requires viewers to shift their gaze and make comparisons across different charts, which can be slightly more demanding.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Complex Encodings:** The visualization uses a complex technique (like horizon graphs) that requires cognitive effort to decode, slowing down the primary task of trend estimation.
- **Obscured Lines:** In a simple line graph, the series of interest is so frequently overlapped by other lines that its overall trend is difficult to perceive.
- **Inconsistent Scales:** In small multiples, the y-axes are not synchronized, making visual comparison of slopes invalid and misleading.

## How to Improve

- **If using a complex chart (e.g., braided, horizon):** Simplify the visualization to either a simple line graph or small multiples. This will likely improve the speed and ease of trend estimation.
- **If using a cluttered simple line graph:**
    - **Quick Fix:** Use color or interactivity to highlight one or two series at a time.
    - **Comprehensive Redesign:** Convert the chart to small multiples to eliminate overlap entirely.
- **If using small multiples:** Ensure all individual charts share an identical y-axis scale to make slope comparisons valid.
