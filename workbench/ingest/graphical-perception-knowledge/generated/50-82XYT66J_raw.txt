## Guidance

When viewers need to compare aggregate properties of two data series (like their mean or range), present them in vertically stacked bar charts rather than superimposing them in a single chart space.

## Why

Vertically stacking charts (a form of small multiples) creates two distinct visual groups. This allows the visual system to more easily form a 'gestalt' or global impression of each set, which is necessary for accurately judging aggregate properties like mean and range. Superimposing the charts forces the viewer to mentally segregate the two intermingled series, which increases cognitive load and leads to less precise comparisons for these types of tasks.

### Core Principle

Match the visual separation of charts to the conceptual separation of the comparison task. Use separate views for set-level (global) comparisons and shared-space views for item-level (focal) comparisons.

## When it applies

- When comparing aggregate or statistical properties (e.g., mean, range, overall distribution) of two or more data series.
- When using bar charts to encode the data.
- When the precision of the comparison is important.

## Exceptions

- When the primary task is to compare individual, corresponding items across the series (e.g., "which month had the biggest difference between series A and B?"). For these "focal" or item-level tasks, superimposing charts can be more effective as it places the items to be compared in closer proximity.
- When vertical screen space is extremely limited, forcing superposition. Even in this case, consider an alternative like a grouped bar chart before resorting to superposition.

## Trade-offs

- **Space:** Stacking charts uses more screen space (typically vertical space) than superimposing them.
- **Item-level comparison:** While better for aggregate comparisons, stacking makes direct comparison of individual corresponding bars slightly harder than superposition because of the physical distance between them.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Cognitive Strain:** Viewers report that it's "hard to see" or "takes a lot of effort" to compare the overall trend of two overlaid series.
- **Intermingled Mess:** The two superimposed series cross over frequently, creating a visually complex and cluttered chart that looks like "spaghetti".
- **Incorrect Global Judgments:** When asked which series has a higher average or wider spread, viewers are slow, uncertain, or frequently incorrect.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Enhance Separation.** If you must use a superimposed chart, use highly distinct, perceptually separated colors (not just different hues, but also different luminance/saturation) to help viewers mentally group the bars for each series.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to Grouped Bars.** Instead of superimposing, place the bars for each category next to each other (a grouped bar chart). This puts all bars on a common baseline and is more precise than superposition, though not as precise as stacking for aggregate tasks.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Vertically Stack the Charts.** Create two separate bar charts, one for each series, and align them vertically with a common x-axis. This provides the clearest visual separation and leads to the most precise aggregate comparisons, as demonstrated by Jardine et al. (2020).
