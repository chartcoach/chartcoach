## Guidance

To visualize and assess the relationship or correlation between two continuous numerical variables, use a scatterplot.

## Why

Scatterplots are specifically designed for this task. They map one variable to the x-axis and the other to the y-axis, allowing the human visual system to perceive the overall shape, direction, and strength of the relationship as a single emergent pattern.

### Core Principle

Make the most important comparison the easiest to see. For correlation, the relationship between two variables is the story, and a scatterplot makes that relationship the primary visual element.

## When it applies

- You have two quantitative (numerical) variables.
- Your primary goal is to determine if there is a relationship between them (e.g., positive, negative, linear, non-linear, or no correlation).
- You want to identify clusters, gaps, or outliers in the joint distribution of two variables.

## Exceptions

- **High data density:** When you have a very large number of data points, overplotting can obscure the underlying correlation. In this case, a standard scatterplot may be ineffective without modification (see "How to Improve").
- **One variable is time:** While you can use a scatterplot, a line chart is often the conventional and more effective choice for showing a trend over time, as it explicitly connects sequential points.

## Trade-offs

- **Limited variables:** A standard scatterplot can only show two variables clearly. Adding more through color, size, or shape can increase cognitive load and obscure the primary correlation pattern.
- **No explicit values:** It is difficult to look up the exact value of a single point without interaction (e.g., tooltips), as the focus is on the aggregate pattern.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Overplotting:** Points are plotted on top of each other, forming dense, unreadable "ink blots" that hide the true data density and correlation.
- **Incorrect data types:** One or both of your axes represent categorical data, resulting in points aligned in rigid rows or columns. A jittered dot plot, box plot, or bar chart would be more appropriate.

## How to Improve

When a basic scatterplot fails due to high data density (overplotting):

- **Quick Fix: Adjust Transparency.** Reduce the opacity (alpha) of the points. This allows overlapping points to create darker areas, giving a sense of density.

- **Moderate Approach: Use Data Subsampling.** Randomly sample a subset of your data. This can reveal the underlying pattern without the performance cost and visual complexity of plotting every point.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Aggregation.** Switch to a design that explicitly handles density:
    - **2D Binning (Hexbin):** Group points into hexagonal bins and use color to represent the number of points in each bin. This clearly shows density patterns.
    - **Contour Plot:** Show the estimated 2D data density using contour lines, similar to a topographical map.

## Guidance

When creating a scatterplot with a large number of data points, use techniques like transparency, subsampling, or aggregation to prevent overplotting and reveal the underlying data distribution.

## Why

Plotting every point in a dense dataset leads to overplotting, where points are drawn on top of each other. This creates saturated "ink blots" that obscure the true density and structure of the data, hide clusters, and can mislead viewers about the number of points in different regions.

### Core Principle

A visualization should be faithful to the underlying data. In dense scatterplots, a naive point-for-point rendering is not faithful to the data's distribution; techniques that reveal density are more truthful.

## When it applies

- You are creating a scatterplot with a "large" or "very large" number of points, where "large" is relative to the chart size and point size (e.g., more than a few thousand points for a typical screen-sized chart).
- Your goal is to understand data distribution, density, correlation, or clusters.
- You observe that individual points are no longer distinguishable in dense areas of the plot.

## Exceptions

- **Task is outlier detection:** If your primary and only goal is to find extreme outliers, and these outliers are far from the dense regions, a standard scatterplot might suffice as the outliers will be visually distinct. However, aggregation methods like Splatterplots are explicitly designed to show both density and outliers.
- **Interactive exploration with zoom:** If the visualization is interactive and allows for deep zooming, overplotting at the global level may be acceptable, as the user can zoom into dense regions to resolve individual points.

## Trade-offs

- **Loss of mark fidelity:** Aggregation techniques (like binning or density maps) replace individual data points with abstract representations (e.g., a colored hexagon). This makes it impossible to see or interact with specific points within the aggregated areas.
- **Parameter sensitivity:** Aggregation methods often require setting parameters (e.g., bin size, kernel bandwidth) which can significantly affect the final visual representation.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Ink Blots:** Large, solid-color areas form where individual points are completely indistinguishable.
- **Hidden Structures:** You suspect there are clusters or patterns within the dense regions, but you cannot see them.
- **Misleading Density:** The visual density appears uniform across a large area, even though the underlying data density may vary significantly. This happens when the plot is saturated with points.

## How to Improve

Choose a strategy based on the trade-off between implementation effort and the fidelity of the representation.

- **Quick Fix: Apply Transparency.** Set a low opacity (alpha value) for each point. Where points overlap, the color will be darker, giving a rough, qualitative sense of density. This is easy to implement but less precise than other methods.

- **Moderate Approach: Subsample the Data.** Instead of plotting all points, plot a random sample (e.g., 10% of the data). This reduces clutter and can often reveal the main trend or clusters, but you risk losing information about outliers or fine-grained structures.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use an Aggregation Strategy.** Replace individual points with a representation of their density. This is the most effective approach for large datasets.
    - **Binned Scatterplot (e.g., Hexbin):** Divide the plot area into a grid (often hexagons) and use color intensity to represent the number of points that fall into each cell. This is excellent for showing density.
    - **Contour Plot / Density Estimation:** Calculate a 2D kernel density estimate (KDE) over the data and display it as a heatmap or with contour lines. This provides a smooth representation of the data distribution.

## Guidance

For tasks that require comparing or ranking the values of different categories within a part-to-whole relationship, use a bar chart instead of a pie or donut chart.

## Why

The human visual system is much better at accurately comparing lengths from a common baseline (as in a bar chart) than it is at comparing angles, areas, or arc lengths (as in pie and donut charts). This makes bar charts more effective for precise comparisons, leading to faster and more accurate interpretation.

### Core Principle

Choose visual encodings that people perceive most accurately for the intended task. For quantitative comparison, position along a common scale is perceptually superior to angle or area.

## When it applies

- You are visualizing a part-to-whole relationship (i.e., categories that sum to 100%).
- The primary task for the user is to compare the sizes of the different categories or to rank them from largest to smallest.
- You have more than 2-3 categories. The more categories you have, the less effective a pie chart becomes.

## Exceptions

- **Showing a single part-to-whole value:** If you want to emphasize a single, striking proportion (e.g., "75% of users completed the task"), a pie chart can be an acceptable and familiar idiom. The goal is not comparison, but highlighting a single metric.
- **Audience familiarity:** In some contexts (e.g., business reports where pie charts are entrenched), using a pie chart may be necessary to meet audience expectations, even if it is perceptually suboptimal.
- **Only two categories:** With just two slices (e.g., 60% vs 40%), a pie chart is easy to read and comparison is trivial.

## Trade-offs

- **Space:** A bar chart typically requires more linear space (especially with long category labels) than a compact pie chart.
- **"Part-of-whole" emphasis:** While a bar chart can show parts of a whole, the "whole" is less visually explicit than in a pie chart, where the full circle represents 100%. A stacked bar chart can mitigate this, but introduces its own comparison challenges.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Clock-Watching" Test:** Viewers have to tilt their heads or treat the chart like a clock face ("this one is about 10 minutes, that one is 12 minutes") to compare slices.
- **Legend Reliance:** The chart is impossible to read without constantly referring back and forth between the legend and the slices.
- **Similar Slices:** The chart contains multiple slices of very similar size, making it nearly impossible to tell which is larger without reading data labels.
- **Too many slices:** The pie chart has more than 5-7 slices, creating a cluttered and unreadable graphic.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** If you must use a pie chart, add direct labels with the category name and percentage to each slice. This provides an "escape hatch" by allowing users to read the numbers directly instead of estimating angles.

- **Moderate Redesign: Sort the Slices.** If using a pie chart, sort the slices from largest to smallest, starting at the 12 o'clock position and going clockwise. This makes it slightly easier to do coarse-grained comparisons.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Switch to a Bar Chart.** The most effective solution is to convert the pie chart into a bar chart. Place category labels on one axis and values on the other. Sort the bars from largest to smallest to make comparison and ranking effortless.
