## Guidance

On trajectories with significant curves or turns, avoid using stroke width (size) as the primary visual channel for encoding speed.

## Why

The perception of line thickness is distorted by the geometry of a curved path. A thick line segment on a tight curve can appear visually "bunched up" or less prominent than the same thickness on a straight segment. This distortion interferes with the viewer's ability to accurately compare speeds along the trajectory, leading to misinterpretation and higher error rates, especially when trying to identify the fastest or slowest points.

### Core Principle

The effectiveness of a visual channel can be dependent on the context of other visual elements. A channel that works in a simple case (a straight line) may fail in a more complex one (a curved line) due to perceptual interference.

## When it applies

- When visualizing speed on a 2D path.
- When the path is curved, winding, or has sharp angles.
- When the user's task involves comparing speeds or finding the point of maximum/minimum speed.

## Exceptions

- This guideline is less critical for trajectories that are entirely or mostly straight. Perin et al. (2018) found that size was an adequate encoding for speed on straight paths.
- If speed variations are very large and dramatic, size might still be effective as the differences in thickness could overcome the perceptual distortion.

## Trade-offs

- By not using size to encode speed, you free up that channel for another variable (such as time).
- The alternative, using color, introduces its own constraints, such as the need to be mindful of color vision deficiencies and potential conflicts with background colors.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Curvature Bias:** Speed judgments are consistently inaccurate on the curved portions of the path.
- **Inconsistent Perception:** The same speed value (represented by the same stroke width) appears different on a straight section versus a curved section.
- **User Feedback:** Viewers report that it's "hard to tell how fast it's going around the corners."

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Combine with a Better Encoding.** If you must use size, add a redundant, more robust encoding like color to represent speed. The color will help viewers overcome the perceptual bias introduced by the size on curves.
- **Moderate Approach: Switch to Color.** Change the speed encoding from size to color value (e.g., a sequential color gradient). Perin et al. (2018) found that color was more robust to path curvature than size for speed-related tasks.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Switch to Segment Length.** Re-encode the trajectory using time ticks (segment length) to represent time. Speed can then be accurately inferred from the distance between ticks, an encoding that is highly robust for both straight and curved paths.
