## Guidance

For tasks that require viewers to compare values, rank categories, or sort items by value, use a bar chart (or column chart).

## Why

Bar charts encode quantitative values using the length of the bar and its position along a common scale (the axis). Humans are exceptionally good at perceiving and comparing linear distances from a shared baseline. This makes bar charts one of the most accurate and efficient chart types for comparison-based tasks, significantly outperforming charts that rely on angle (pie charts) or area (treemaps).

### Core Principle

Humans judge quantities more accurately by comparing positions along a common scale than by comparing angles, areas, or colors. A visualization should use the most accurate perceptual channel for its primary task.

## When it applies

- When the primary goal is to compare the magnitude of values across a set of discrete categories.
- When you need to show the rank order of categories.
- When you want to enable viewers to easily look up the value of a specific category and compare it to others.
- When visualizing data for a general audience that may not be familiar with more complex chart types.

## Exceptions

- **Showing part-to-whole composition:** If the primary task is to show how parts make up a whole, a stacked bar chart or treemap might be more appropriate, although they make it harder to compare the individual parts that are not on the baseline.
- **Visualizing disproportionate values:** When some values are orders of magnitude larger than others, a standard bar chart can make smaller values invisible. In such cases, a wrapped bar chart or a panel of charts with different scales might be considered, though they come with their own perceptual trade-offs.

## Trade-offs

- **Space:** Bar charts can take up more space than more compact forms like treemaps, especially with a high number of categories.
- **Composition:** While a simple bar chart is excellent for comparison, it does not inherently show the part-to-whole relationship as clearly as a pie chart or stacked bar chart.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Back-and-Forth:** Viewers have to repeatedly look at data labels to be sure of the rank order because the visual representations (e.g., pie slices) are too similar to judge by eye.
- **Incorrect Comparisons:** When asked, viewers frequently make errors when comparing the size of two or more categories.
- **Slow Interpretation:** It takes viewers a long time to answer simple comparison questions like "Which category is the second largest?"

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Sorted Labels.** If you must use a less effective chart (like a pie chart), add direct labels with the value and rank, and sort the categories by value. This provides a text-based escape hatch but doesn't fix the underlying perceptual issue.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Bar Chart.** Replace the existing chart (e.g., a pie chart or a set of circles) with a standard bar chart. This directly addresses the perceptual weakness.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Create a Sorted Bar Chart with Annotations.** Convert to a bar chart, sort the bars from largest to smallest (or vice versa), and use color and annotations to highlight the most important findings or categories. This not only enables accurate comparison but also guides the viewer's attention.

## Guidance

When designing a bar chart, adjust the chart dimensions and bar widths so that the bars have a moderate, roughly square-like aspect ratio. Avoid creating bars that are either extremely tall and thin or very short and wide.

## Why

The shape of a bar influences how its value is perceived. Research has shown that viewers tend to underestimate the value of tall, thin bars and overestimate the value of short, wide bars. This systematic bias can compromise the integrity of the data visualization, leading viewers to draw incorrect conclusions. Bars with an aspect ratio closer to 1:1 (a square) have been found to be free of this perceptual bias.

### Core Principle

Visual features that are not meant to encode data (like a bar's aspect ratio) should not distort the perception of the features that do encode data (the bar's length).

## When it applies

- When creating any bar or column chart where accurate value judgment is important.
- Particularly when viewers might be mentally averaging or comparing groups of bars, as the bias can accumulate.
- In responsive designs where chart dimensions change dynamically, as this can unintentionally introduce bias.

## Exceptions

- **Sparklines:** For very small, word-sized charts where the goal is to show overall shape and trend rather than precise values, this guideline is less critical. The extreme aspect ratio is a necessary trade-off for compactness.
- **Unit charts:** If each bar is composed of discrete, countable glyphs, the counting task may override the aspect ratio bias, but this is not well-studied.

## Trade-offs

- **Aesthetics vs. Accuracy:** A designer might prefer the look of very thin or thick bars for stylistic reasons, but this comes at the cost of perceptual accuracy.
- **Information Density:** To achieve a square-like ratio for bars representing large values, the chart may need to become very wide or the scale may need to be compressed, potentially affecting the ability to see small differences.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Extreme Proportions:** The bars in your chart look like skinny needles or flat rectangles.
- **Responsive Breakpoints:** The chart looks fine on a desktop but becomes a series of very tall, thin bars on a mobile screen.
- **Inconsistent Bar Widths:** The width of bars changes arbitrarily, which can compound the aspect ratio bias.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Adjust Bar/Gap Width.** Most charting tools allow you to adjust the width of bars and the spacing between them. Widen thin bars or narrow wide bars until they look more proportional. A common rule of thumb is to make the gap between bars about 50% of the bar width.
- **Moderate Redesign: Re-evaluate Chart Dimensions.** Instead of just changing bar width, adjust the overall height and width of your chart. If you have tall bars, make the chart wider. If you have wide bars, make it taller.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Use a Charting Library with Aspect Ratio Controls.** For responsive projects, use or configure a charting library that attempts to maintain a reasonable aspect ratio for elements as the viewport size changes, rather than simply squashing or stretching the entire image.

## Guidance

Choose your visual encoding based on the viewer's primary task. Use a color-based encoding (like a heatmap or "colorfield") for summary tasks like judging the average value or variance. Use a position-based encoding (like a line or bar chart) for identification tasks like finding the highest/lowest value or determining the range.

## Why

The human visual system appears to process color and position differently. Color seems to facilitate a more "global" or summative judgment, allowing for rapid estimation of the statistical properties of a collection (e.g., the average color of a region). Position, on the other hand, is better suited for identifying specific points, boundaries, and shapes, making it easy to locate peaks and troughs. Choosing the encoding that matches the task leads to more accurate and efficient interpretation.

### Core Principle

The effectiveness of a visual channel is not absolute; it is dependent on the task the viewer is trying to perform. Design requires matching the strengths of an encoding to the demands of the task.

## When it applies

- When visualizing dense data, such as time series or 2D spatial data.
- When you must decide between showing data as a line chart versus a heatmap.
- When designing interactive systems where a user might switch between wanting to see an overview (summary) and wanting to find specific details (identification).

## Exceptions

- This guideline is most relevant when there is a clear primary task. If summary and identification are equally important, a design that combines both (e.g., a line chart with a colored background indicating a range) or allows toggling between views may be necessary.
- For very sparse data, the benefits of a colorfield for summarization may be diminished as there are not enough data points to form a clear "ensemble."

## Trade-offs

- **Prioritizing one task over another:** By choosing color for a summary task, you make identification tasks (like finding the max value) harder. By choosing position for an identification task, you may make summary judgments less accurate.
- **Familiarity:** Line charts are generally more familiar to a general audience than heatmaps for time-series data, which might be a reason to prefer them even if the primary task is summarization.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Hunting for Peaks in a Heatmap:** Viewers are struggling to find the single highest or lowest value in a heatmap, scanning back and forth across the color legend.
- **"Eyeballing" Averages in a Line Chart:** Viewers are trying to estimate the average value of a highly volatile line chart, leading to inaccurate guesses.
- **Task Mismatch:** You have provided a line chart but the main question is "What was the average value in June?" or you have provided a heatmap and the main question is "On what day did we hit our peak?"

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Annotations.** If your chart's encoding is mismatched to the task, add annotations or labels that explicitly state the answer. For example, add a label for the "Peak Value" on a heatmap, or draw a "Monthly Average" line on a line chart.
- **Moderate Approach: Provide an Alternative View.** In an interactive context, provide a button or control that allows the user to switch between a position-based view (line chart) and a color-based view (heatmap) of the same data.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Choose the Encoding Matched to the Primary Task.** During the design phase, explicitly define the most critical task the viewer must accomplish. If it is summarization, choose a color-based encoding. If it is identification, choose a position-based encoding.

## Guidance

If viewers need to compare the number of items in different categories, do not use size or luminance (lightness/darkness of a color) to encode other data dimensions for those items.

## Why

The human visual system's ability to estimate "how many" things there are (a perceptual task called numerosity) is not independent of other visual properties. We have a built-in bias to perceive collections of larger items as more numerous, and collections of darker items as more numerous. When you map another variable (like GDP or importance) to size or luminance, you unintentionally distort the viewer's ability to compare the raw counts of the categories, which can be misleading.

### Core Principle

To enable fair visual comparison of a specific data dimension (like count), confounding perceptual cues from other visual channels (like size or luminance) should be minimized or eliminated.

## When it applies

- In scatterplots or bubble charts where you want the viewer to be able to compare the number of points in different colored groups.
- In any chart using discrete glyphs (icons, symbols, shapes) where the count of glyphs per category is a meaningful metric.
- In text visualizations where the number of words or phrases of a certain type is being compared.

## Exceptions

- If the primary task is *not* to compare counts, using size or luminance is acceptable. For example, in a bubble chart showing GDP (size) and life expectancy (position), comparing the number of countries is usually a secondary task, and the size encoding is critical for the primary task.
- If the variation in size or luminance is very small and unlikely to create a significant bias.

## Trade-offs

- **Encoding Channel Limitation:** This guideline "reserves" the size and luminance channels, preventing you from using them to encode other variables if count comparison is important. This may force you to use less effective channels (like shape) or create more charts.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Size-Count Confusion:** A bubble chart where one category has a few very large bubbles and another has many small bubbles. It's nearly impossible to tell which category has more items without manually counting.
- **Luminance-Count Confusion:** A chart where one category is represented by dark, prominent marks and another by light, faint marks, making the dark category appear more prevalent.
- **Misleading First Impression:** A viewer's initial glance gives them a wrong impression of the balance between categories (e.g., "There are way more blue dots," when in fact there are not).

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Use a Different Channel.** If you are using size or luminance to encode a variable, switch to a channel that does not bias numerosity, such as shape or a color hue with uniform luminance.
- **Moderate Approach: Separate the Encodings.** Instead of layering multiple encodings on one chart, create two separate charts (e.g., using small multiples). One chart can show the count comparison (e.g., a bar chart of counts per category), and the other can show the distribution of the other variable (e.g., a bubble chart showing size).
- **Comprehensive Approach: Normalize the Biasing Feature.** In some specific cases, you can computationally adjust the "biasing" feature to counteract the perceptual effect. For example, the study by Correll et al. (2013) on text visualization added spacing to shorter words to make their overall length similar to longer words, which improved numerosity estimation. This is an advanced technique that requires a deep understanding of the specific perceptual bias.

## Guidance

When encoding ordinal data (e.g., "low," "medium," "high") or binned quantitative data, use a sequential color scale that primarily varies in saturation or lightness. Avoid using a qualitative (multi-hue) scale like a rainbow.

## Why

Color saturation and lightness (e.g., light blue to dark blue) have a natural, intuitive perceptual order. Viewers immediately and correctly assume that "more" of a color (darker or more saturated) means a higher value. In contrast, color hue (e.g., red, green, blue, yellow) has no inherent perceptual order. There is no universal agreement on whether green comes "after" blue, which forces the viewer to constantly refer to the legend, increasing cognitive load and the chance of error.

### Core Principle

The structure of the visual encoding should match the structure of the data. Ordered data requires a visual channel with a clear perceptual order.

## When it applies

- When creating choropleth maps to show ranked regions (e.g., low to high risk).
- In heatmaps where the values represent ordered categories or binned numerical data.
- For any chart where the color of marks (points, bars, lines) represents an ordinal variable.

## Exceptions

- **Diverging data:** If your data has a meaningful midpoint (e.g., "below average," "average," "above average"), a diverging palette (which uses two different hues that meet at a neutral light color) is more appropriate than a simple sequential one.
- **Highlighting specific categories:** If the goal is not to show order but to make one specific category (e.g., "high risk") pop out, you may break this rule and use a single, highly salient accent color for that category and a neutral color for all others.

## Trade-offs

- **Discriminability:** A single-hue sequential scale with only a few steps (e.g., 3-5) might make the steps harder to tell apart than if you used distinct hues. However, this is usually a worthwhile trade-off for the gain in interpretability.
- **Aesthetics:** Some designers find multi-hue palettes more vibrant or visually appealing, but this typically comes at the cost of clarity and perceptual accuracy.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Legend Dependence:** Viewers cannot interpret the chart without constantly consulting the legend to understand the color order.
- **Rainbow Colors:** The chart uses a spectral "rainbow" palette (red-orange-yellow-green-blue-indigo-violet) for ordered data.
- **Incorrect Ordering:** When asked, viewers incorrectly state the order of the categories based on color.
- **Accessibility Issues:** The chosen hues are not distinguishable by users with color vision deficiencies. Sequential single-hue scales are often more robust to colorblindness.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Switch to Grayscale.** The simplest sequential scale is a grayscale ramp (white to black). This is universally ordered and accessible, though it may lack visual appeal.
- **Moderate Approach: Use a Single-Hue Sequential Palette.** Change your color scheme to a sequential scale that varies the saturation/lightness of a single hue (e.g., light blue to dark blue). Tools like ColorBrewer make these readily available.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Create a Perceptually Uniform Sequential Palette.** Use a modern color space (like HCL or L*C*h) to design a sequential color scale where the perceived steps in lightness are uniform. This ensures that a step from 1 to 2 feels visually equal to a step from 4 to 5. Tools like Viz Palette or the `colorspace` package in R can help create these.
