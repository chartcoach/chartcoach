## Guidance

When encoding quantitative data for comparison or ranking tasks, prefer using position along a common, aligned scale over other visual channels like length, angle, area, or color saturation.

## Why

The human visual system is most adept at accurately judging differences in position along a common baseline. Other visual variables are perceived less precisely and are more susceptible to bias. Using position-based encodings like bar charts or dot plots minimizes perceptual error and allows viewers to make faster, more accurate quantitative comparisons.

### Core Principle

Humans judge quantities more accurately by comparing positions along a common scale than by comparing lengths, angles, areas, or colors. Make the most important comparisons the easiest and most accurate to perform.

## When it applies

-   When the primary task is to compare, rank, or look up quantitative values.
-   When creating bar charts, dot plots, scatter plots, or line charts where values are mapped to the x or y-axis.
-   When data integrity and accuracy are critical.

## Exceptions

-   **Showing part-to-whole relationships:** While less accurate for comparison, a pie or donut chart can effectively emphasize that individual values are parts of a whole, especially with 2-3 categories. However, a stacked bar chart often achieves this better.
-   **Limited Space:** Area-based charts like treemaps can be more space-efficient for displaying hierarchical data than position-based charts, at the cost of comparison accuracy.
-   **Cyclical Data:** Angles in a radar chart can be appropriate for comparing multiple quantitative attributes across different items, though they are still less accurate than linear position.

## Trade-offs

-   **Space:** Position-based charts like bar charts can take up more space than area-based charts like treemaps, especially with many categories.
-   **Familiarity:** For some audiences, a pie chart might feel more familiar for part-to-whole relationships, even if it's less perceptually effective for precise comparisons. Switching to a bar chart might require a small cognitive adjustment for the viewer.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Chart Type:** The visualization is a pie chart, donut chart, or treemap, and the primary goal is to compare values between categories.
-   **Viewer Questions:** Viewers ask "Is this slice bigger than that one?" or express uncertainty when comparing values.
-   **Distortion:** In a stacked bar or area chart, the segments are not aligned to a common baseline, making it impossible to accurately compare their lengths/heights.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** If you must use a pie or area chart, add direct data labels (e.g., "42%") to each segment. This provides an "escape hatch" for the user, allowing them to read the exact values instead of relying on less accurate perceptual estimation.

-   **Moderate Redesign: Switch to Aligned Bars.** If using a stacked bar chart for comparison, switch to a grouped bar chart. This places all bars on the same baseline, enabling accurate length/position comparison.

-   **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Position on a Common Scale.** Replace the chart with a bar chart or dot plot. This directly leverages the most effective visual channel (position) for the task, ensuring the highest possible accuracy for the viewer, as recommended by decades of perception research.

## Guidance

Select the visualization type that is empirically best suited for the viewer's primary analytical task. Do not assume a single chart type is effective for all purposes.

## Why

Different chart designs are cognitively and perceptually optimized for different tasks. For example, judging correlation is a different mental process than identifying clusters or finding outliers. Using a chart type that is mismatched to the task can make the analysis slower, less accurate, and more mentally demanding for the viewer.

### Core Principle

The effectiveness of a visualization is a function of the data, the visual encoding, and the task. To create an effective visualization, all three components must be in alignment.

## When it applies

-   When designing any visualization, especially for exploratory data analysis.
-   When creating dashboards or reports that are intended to support multiple analytical questions.
-   When a visualization seems confusing or isn't yielding clear insights.

## Exceptions

-   **Audience Convention:** If an audience has a strong, established convention for using a specific (even if sub-optimal) chart type for a task, deviating from it may cause more confusion than the perceptual benefit is worth. In such cases, education or a gradual transition is needed.
-   **Multi-Task Visualizations:** Sometimes a single chart must support multiple tasks. In this case, you may need to choose a "good-enough" chart that performs reasonably well across tasks, or supplement it with interaction or additional views.

## Trade-offs

-   **Flexibility vs. Specificity:** A general-purpose chart like a table can support many tasks (lookup, compare, rank) but may be less effective at revealing high-level patterns (trends, correlations) compared to a more specific chart like a line chart or scatterplot.
-   **Learning Curve:** Using the optimal chart for a task (e.g., parallel coordinates for finding anomalies) might require a learning curve for audiences unfamiliar with that chart type.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Task Mismatch:** A line chart is being used to show correlation between two non-temporal variables.
-   **Ineffective Analysis:** Viewers struggle to see patterns, clusters, or outliers in the chart.
-   **Wrong Tool for the Job:** Using a pie chart (for part-to-whole) when the task is to see the trend over time (which requires a line chart).

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Clarify the Primary Task.** Add a title or caption that explicitly states the intended takeaway or the question the chart is meant to answer. This helps focus the viewer's attention on the intended task.

-   **Moderate Redesign: Switch to the Recommended Chart.** Based on empirical evidence (like that summarized by Zeng & Battle), swap the current chart for one better suited to the task.
    -   **For Distribution:** Use a bar chart (histogram) or box plot.
    -   **For Correlation:** Use a scatterplot.
    -   **For Finding Anomalies/Outliers:** Use a scatterplot, table, or parallel coordinates plot.
    -   **For Clustering:** Use a bar chart or scatterplot.

-   **Comprehensive Approach: Create Multiple Views.** If the data needs to support multiple distinct tasks, provide multiple, coordinated charts. For example, pair a scatterplot (for correlation) with histograms on the margins (for distribution) to allow the viewer to perform each task with the optimal chart type.

## Guidance

In multiclass scatterplots, assign colors from a chosen palette to data classes in a way that maximizes their perceptual separability. Prioritize giving high-contrast colors to classes that are spatially adjacent, overlapping, or dense.

## Why

Simply choosing a good color palette is not enough; how those colors are *assigned* to data classes is critical. A random or default assignment can place perceptually similar colors on spatially adjacent data clusters, causing them to blend together and be misread as a single group. An optimized assignment ensures that the most confusable data clusters are given the most distinguishable colors, clarifying boundaries and improving the accuracy of class identification.

### Core principle

The effectiveness of a color palette depends not only on the colors themselves but also on how they are assigned relative to the spatial features of the data. Visual separation is a product of both color distance and spatial distance.

## When it applies

-   When creating multiclass scatterplots where each point belongs to a category encoded by color.
-   When data classes have some degree of overlap or are spatially close to each other.
-   When the number of classes is high (e.g., > 5), making random assignment more likely to produce confusing adjacencies.
-   When the task is to identify, count, or compare the clusters.

## Exceptions

-   **Semantic Color Association:** If categories have strong, pre-existing color associations (e.g., "Democrats" are blue, "Republicans" are red), these semantic constraints should override a purely perceptual optimization. The meaning conveyed by the color is more important.
-   **Ordered Categories:** If the categories are ordered, a sequential color scheme should be used and assigned in order, rather than optimizing for separability.

## Trade-offs

-   **Aesthetics vs. Clarity:** An optimized assignment might not always be the most aesthetically harmonious. For example, it might place two high-contrast, non-complementary colors next to each other to maximize separability.
-   **Consistency:** If you are creating multiple charts, you may want to keep the color for a specific category consistent across all of them. This may conflict with finding the optimal assignment for each individual chart.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **The Squint Test:** When you squint at the scatterplot, do two or more distinct clusters blur into a single colored mass?
-   **Adjacent Ambiguity:** Are two clusters that are right next to each other colored with very similar hues (e.g., blue and purple, or orange and red)?
-   **Washed-Out Clusters:** Is a dense cluster of points assigned a color with very low contrast against the background (e.g., light yellow on a white background), making it hard to see?
-   **User Confusion:** Do viewers have trouble counting the number of classes or distinguishing between them?

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Manually Swap Confusable Colors.** Identify the two most confusable, adjacent clusters. Go to your color palette and find the most different color available and swap it in for one of the clusters. Repeat for the next most confusable pair.

-   **Moderate Approach: Prioritize High-Contrast Colors for Overlapping Classes.** Identify the classes with the most spatial overlap or density. Manually assign them colors from your palette that are most distinct from each other and have high contrast with the chart's background color. Assign the remaining colors to the more clearly separated classes.

-   **Comprehensive Approach: Use an Optimization Algorithm.** Use a tool like the one from the Wang et al. (2019) paper to automatically find a near-optimal assignment. These tools analyze both the color palette and the spatial distribution of your data to compute an assignment that maximizes perceptual separability.
