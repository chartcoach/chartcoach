## Guidance

Use colormaps that maximize hue variation, such as rainbow or spectral schemes, when the primary user task is to estimate specific quantitative values from a continuous map.

## Why

Colormaps with non-monotonic variations across perceptual channels (like the full hue spectrum in a rainbow map) are better at counteracting the "simultaneous contrast" illusion. This illusion causes surrounding colors to alter the perception of a central color, leading to significant errors in value lookups. Maximizing hue variation minimizes these lookup errors, improving perceptual accuracy.

### Core Principle

Task-specific encoding is critical. The optimal visual encoding for one task (e.g., reading a value) may be suboptimal for another (e.g., seeing a pattern). The visual properties that make rainbow maps poor for seeing form are precisely what make them good for accurate value lookups.

## When it applies

- When the primary task for a continuous quantitative map (like a weather map, elevation map, or scientific visualization) is for the user to look up or read the specific value at a given point (e.g., "What is the temperature in this city?").
- This guideline applies regardless of whether the underlying data is simple (low spatial frequency) or complex (high spatial frequency).

## Exceptions

- **Pattern recognition is primary:** If the main goal is for users to identify shapes, forms, or fine-grained geometric patterns, do not use a rainbow colormap. It performs poorly for this task.
- **Accessibility is paramount:** Standard rainbow colormaps are not accessible to users with common forms of color vision deficiency (e.g., Deuteranopia). If you cannot provide an alternative, avoid rainbow colormaps.

## Trade-offs

- **Accuracy vs. Form:** You gain accuracy in value lookups at the expense of clearly perceiving the shape and form of data features. Rainbow colormaps can introduce strong visual boundaries where none exist in the data, obscuring geometric patterns.
- **Accuracy vs. Accessibility:** You gain lookup accuracy for users with typical color vision but create significant barriers for users with color vision deficiencies.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Inaccurate Readings:** Users consistently misjudge or misread values in certain regions of a map that uses a luminance-based (greyscale, sequential, spiral) colormap.
- **Contrast Illusions:** Two points with clearly distinct values appear to have the same color because of the influence of their surrounding colors.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add a Tooltip.** If you must use a less-than-ideal colormap, add an interactive tooltip that reveals the exact data value on hover. This provides an "escape hatch" for users to get an accurate reading, bypassing the perceptual limitations.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Multi-Hue Scheme.** Replace a luminance-based colormap (like greyscale or a single-hue sequential scheme) with a multi-hue scheme like `spectral` from ColorBrewer.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Use a Full-Spectrum Colormap.** If the *only* critical task is quantity estimation, use a full-spectrum rainbow colormap, as it was found to be the most accurate in experiments. Clearly acknowledge and document its trade-offs regarding pattern perception and accessibility, and consider providing an alternative view for other tasks.

## Guidance

To help users perceive and match longitudinal patterns in complex (high spatial frequency) maps, use a diverging colormap that has distinct, equidistant luminance steps. Avoid rainbow, sequential, and spiral colormaps for this task.

## Why

The human visual system relies heavily on luminance (lightness/darkness) cues to infer shape, form, and structure. Diverging colormaps with controlled, stepped luminance provide "maximal perceptual resolution," which helps the visual system resolve and compare fine-grained features. Colormaps that lack this controlled luminance structure (like rainbow) or have smoothly changing luminance (sequential/spiral) tend to obscure or wash out these important patterns in complex data.

### Core Principle

Align the visual encoding channel with the perceptual task. For perceiving shape and form, luminance is the dominant perceptual channel, so it should be the primary variable in the colormap.

## When it applies

- When the task requires users to identify, compare, or match a specific shape, profile, or pattern within a continuous, complex dataset.
- Examples include a geologist identifying a landform profile, a doctor spotting an anomaly in a medical scan, or a climatologist tracking the shape of a weather front.
- This is most critical for data with high spatial frequency (many fine details).

## Exceptions

- **Quantity estimation is primary:** If the main goal is for users to look up specific values, these colormaps are less accurate than a rainbow-like scheme.
- **Simple data:** In maps with very simple, broad features (low spatial frequency), the choice of colormap has less impact on pattern perception.

## Trade-offs

- **Form vs. Value:** You gain clarity in perceiving geometric forms at the cost of accuracy for reading specific quantitative values.
- **Highlighting extremes:** Diverging maps are excellent for showing deviation from a central point, but may be less intuitive for data that does not have a meaningful midpoint.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Lost Details:** Fine details and structures in your data appear "washed out," blurry, or are completely invisible.
- **False Forms:** A rainbow colormap is creating strong visual boundaries (especially in the yellow/cyan regions) that are being misinterpreted as features in the data.
- **Task Failure:** Users are unable to reliably identify or match known patterns, shapes, or profiles in the visualization.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Convert to Greyscale.** As a quick diagnostic, convert your visualization to greyscale. If key patterns disappear, your colormap is likely relying too much on hue and not enough on luminance. This is a sign you need a better colormap for this task.
- **Moderate Redesign: Provide an Alternate View.** If you must support multiple tasks, provide a user control to switch between a hue-based map (for value lookups) and a diverging luminance-based map (for pattern analysis), labeling each for its intended purpose.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Select a Task-Appropriate Diverging Scheme.** Replace the existing colormap with a diverging scheme from a tool like ColorBrewer (e.g., `Spectral`, `RdYlBu`). Ensure the scheme has clear luminance steps and is appropriate for the data's midpoint.

## Guidance

For complex maps with many fine details (high spatial frequency), use colormaps with either a full range of saturated hues (like rainbow) or a diverging scheme with strong saturation/chroma variation (like cool-warm or blue-yellow) to help users compare local gradients or steepness.

## Why

When data is complex, judging how quickly values are changing in different areas requires strong perceptual cues. At high spatial frequencies, the visual system benefits from strong hue or saturation (chroma) contrasts to distinguish these changes in local variance. Colormaps that rely primarily on luminance or have low saturation do not provide enough perceptual "pop" to make these judgments reliably in complex data.

## When it applies

- When the user's task is to compare how quickly a value is changing in two or more different regions of a complex map.
- For example, answering questions like, "Is the terrain steeper here or there?" or "Is the pressure changing more rapidly in region A or region B?".
- This guideline is specifically for data with high spatial frequency.

## Exceptions

- **Simple Maps:** This guideline does not apply to simple maps with low spatial frequency (broad, smooth features). In that context, the study found no significant performance difference between any of the tested colormaps, including plain greyscale.
- **Other Tasks:** The colormaps that are best for this task may not be ideal for others. For instance, `rainbow` is good here but poor for perceiving geometric patterns.

## Trade-offs

- **Clarity vs. Other Tasks:** Choosing a colormap optimized for gradient perception may compromise performance on other important tasks like pattern recognition or even value lookup (if not using a rainbow-like scheme).
- **Interpretability:** While effective, a fully saturated rainbow map can be perceptually non-uniform and is not ideal for conveying magnitude in an ordered way.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Indistinguishable Slopes:** On a complex map, users cannot reliably tell which of two areas has a steeper gradient or higher local variance. All areas with high variance blend together.
- **Washed-Out Colors:** The colormap used is low in saturation (e.g., pastel colors), failing to provide enough contrast to highlight differences in steepness.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Increase Saturation.** If you are using a sequential or diverging colormap that feels "washed out," try increasing its overall saturation or chroma range to make the colors more vivid.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Chroma-Varying Diverging Scheme.** If using a luminance-based ramp (greyscale, sequential), switch to a diverging scheme that varies in saturation, such as `coolwarm` or a blue-to-yellow ramp.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Choose a Task-Appropriate Saturated Scheme.** For a map with high spatial frequency where gradient perception is the key task, choose a colormap that provides strong hue and/or saturation variation. This could be a chroma-varying diverging ramp or a fully saturated ramp like `rainbow`, depending on other task priorities.

## Guidance

Do not universally ban the rainbow colormap. Instead, evaluate it as a specialized tool that is highly effective for some tasks (quantitative lookups, gradient perception) but demonstrably ineffective for others (geometric pattern recognition).

## Why

The long-standing and valid critique of rainbow colormaps centers on their non-perceptual ordering and creation of false boundaries, which hinders the interpretation of shape and form. However, the very properties that make them bad for patterns (strong, non-monotonic hue variation) make them excellent at combating simultaneous contrast illusions. This leads to higher accuracy in value lookups. The colormap's effectiveness is a direct trade-off based on the user's primary task.

## When it applies

- When choosing a colormap for a continuous quantitative map where users may need to perform multiple, competing analytical tasks.
- When your team or organization has a blanket rule to "never use rainbow," this guideline provides the evidence for a more nuanced, task-based policy.

## Exceptions

- **Never use a rainbow colormap if the primary task is identifying fine-grained geometric shapes or patterns.** The false boundaries it creates will actively mislead the user.
- **Never use a standard rainbow colormap without mitigation if accessibility is a primary concern.** It is not perceivable by users with common red-green color vision deficiencies.

## Trade-offs

- The primary trade-off is stark: choosing rainbow **maximizes accuracy for value lookup** while simultaneously **degrading performance for pattern recognition** and **creating accessibility barriers**. This choice must be made consciously.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Dogmatic Rejection:** A team has a blanket policy to "never use rainbow" and applies it even in situations where the primary task is quantity estimation, for which it is the most accurate tool.
- **Task Mismatch:** A rainbow colormap is being used on a complex map where the primary goal is to understand the shape or structure of the data.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Document the Rationale.** If you choose to use a rainbow colormap for its lookup accuracy, add a note to the visualization explaining *why* it was chosen and acknowledging its known limitations for pattern perception.
- **Moderate Redesign: Provide Task-Based Alternatives.** Instead of a single, static map, provide a user control to switch between two views:
  1.  A **"Read Values"** view using a rainbow colormap.
  2.  A **"See Patterns"** view using a perceptually-uniform diverging colormap.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Implement a Task-Aware System.** Design the visualization system to automatically recommend or switch the colormap based on the analytical task the user initiates. For example, activating a "measure" tool could trigger a rainbow scheme, while activating a "profile" tool could trigger a diverging scheme.
