## Guidance

For tasks requiring comparison of parts to a whole with a small number of categories, use chart types other than treemaps, such as bar charts or pie charts.

## Why

Humans are less accurate and slower at judging and comparing the relative areas of nested rectangles (as in a squarified treemap) compared to angles in a pie chart or lengths in a bar chart for simple composition tasks. A 2019 study found no evidence that treemaps perform better than pie charts in this context; in fact, they performed significantly worse in both accuracy and speed.

### Core Principle

Choose visual encodings that are optimized for the primary analytical task. For accurate magnitude comparison, length and position along a common scale (bar charts) are superior to area (treemaps).

## When it applies

- When showing a part-to-whole relationship with fewer than 10-15 categories.
- The primary task is for viewers to compare or estimate the size of specific parts (e.g., "Which segment is largest?" or "How does segment A compare to segment B?").
- The data is not hierarchical.

## Exceptions

- **Hierarchical Data:** Treemaps are designed for and excel at visualizing hierarchical data (data with parent-child relationships). If you need to show both the part-to-whole relationship *and* the underlying hierarchy, a treemap is the appropriate choice. This guideline applies only when using a treemap as a direct alternative to a pie chart for non-hierarchical data.
- **Very Large Number of Categories:** For visualizing the composition of hundreds or thousands of items where individual comparison is not the goal, a treemap can be a space-efficient way to give a high-level impression of the distribution.

## Trade-offs

- While less perceptually effective for comparison, treemaps are more space-efficient than bar charts and avoid the negative stigma sometimes associated with pie charts. However, this aesthetic or spatial advantage comes at the direct cost of viewer accuracy and speed of interpretation.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Difficult Comparisons:** Viewers struggle to accurately rank the sizes of the rectangles, especially for non-adjacent rectangles or those with different aspect ratios.
- **Slow Reading:** It takes viewers a long time to find and estimate the value of a specific segment.
- **Misleading Insights:** Viewers draw incorrect conclusions about the relative proportions of the categories.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** Add large, clear labels with the percentage or value to each rectangle in the treemap. This provides a "cognitive escape hatch," allowing users to read the values directly instead of relying on flawed area perception.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Pie or Donut Chart.** For a small number of slices, a pie chart is more perceptually effective and faster to read than a treemap for part-to-whole tasks, according to the study.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use a Bar Chart.** The most effective solution is to switch to a simple bar chart, with bars sorted from largest to smallest. This uses length along a common baseline, the most accurate encoding for this type of comparison task.

## Guidance

When the primary goal is for a user to assess the relationship (correlation) between two quantitative variables, a scatterplot is the most effective choice.

## Why

Scatterplots encode two variables using position on the X and Y axes. The resulting visual pattern of the points (e.g., a tight line, a diffuse cloud) provides a direct, powerful, and relatively intuitive representation of the strength and direction of a correlation. Other chart types, like parallel coordinates, have been shown to cause viewers to underestimate the strength of the relationship.

### Core Principle

Visual encodings that map data attributes directly to emergent visual properties (like the shape of a point cloud) can make complex statistical relationships easier to perceive.

## When it applies

- The dataset contains two (or more) quantitative variables.
- The analytical task is to determine if, how, and to what extent the variables are related (e.g., "Is there a relationship between advertising spend and sales?").

## Exceptions

- **Overplotting:** If you have a very large number of data points (thousands or more), a standard scatterplot can become a solid blob, hiding the true density and correlation. In this case, specialized techniques are needed.
- **Time as a Variable:** If one of the variables is time, a line chart is the more conventional choice for showing a trend over time. For showing the correlation *between* two time series, a connected scatterplot can be effective.

## Trade-offs

- A scatterplot focuses on the aggregate pattern and makes it difficult to look up the exact value for a specific data point without interaction (like tooltips).
- They require a certain level of graphical literacy to interpret correlation from the point cloud.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Wrong Chart Type:** You are using a line chart, bar chart, or parallel coordinates plot and asking the user to judge a correlation.
- **Overplotting ("The Hairball"):** The chart is an unreadable, solid mass of overlapping points, obscuring any underlying pattern.
- **User Confusion:** Viewers are unable to describe the relationship between the two variables or misinterpret the strength of the correlation.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Change Chart Type.** If using another chart type like a bar or line chart for a correlation task, switch to a scatterplot.
- **Moderate Approach: Address Overplotting.** If the scatterplot is overplotted, reduce the opacity (alpha) of the points, use smaller marks, or randomly sample the data to reveal the underlying structure.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Use Density Visualization.** For severe overplotting, switch from a standard scatterplot to a 2D density plot (also known as a heatmap) or a contour plot. These explicitly visualize the number of points in each region, revealing both the correlation and the density of the data simultaneously.

## Guidance

When viewers need to accurately compare or rank the values of different categories, use a bar chart instead of a pie chart.

## Why

Bar charts encode data using length and position along a common baseline. Pie charts encode data using angle and area. Humans are demonstrably better at perceiving small differences in length from a common scale than they are at perceiving differences in angles or areas. This makes bar charts a more accurate and efficient tool for comparison tasks.

### Core Principle

Humans judge quantities more accurately by comparing positions along a common scale than by comparing angles or areas. The visualization should use encodings that humans can perceive most accurately for the intended task.

## When it applies

- The primary task is to compare categories to each other (e.g., "Which region had the highest sales? By how much?").
- You need to show rankings or relative differences between categories.
- You have more than 3-4 categories, or the values of the categories are relatively close, requiring precise comparison.

## Exceptions

- **Simple Part-to-Whole:** If the primary goal is simply to show a single part's relationship to the whole (e.g., "Yes votes were 75% of the total") and there are only 2-3 categories, a pie chart can be acceptable and is immediately recognizable to a general audience.
- **Clock Metaphor:** If the data is cyclical (e.g., hours in a day), the circular layout of a pie chart might reinforce a useful metaphor, though a bar chart would still be more accurate for comparisons.

## Trade-offs

- Pie charts are very familiar to general audiences for showing parts of a whole. Switching to a bar chart might feel less intuitive for this specific "composition" message, even if it's more perceptually accurate for the "comparison" sub-task.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Pac-Man" Chart:** A pie chart has more than 4-5 slices, making it look cluttered and difficult to read.
- **Difficult Comparisons:** It is hard to tell which of two similarly-sized slices is actually bigger without reading a label.
- **3D or Exploded Pies:** The chart uses 3D effects or has "exploded" slices, which further distort the angles and areas, making perception even less accurate and potentially misleading.
- **Multiple Pies:** You are trying to compare values across two or more separate pie charts. This is an extremely difficult perceptual task.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** If you must use a pie chart, add direct data labels (values or percentages) to each slice. This allows viewers to read and compare the numbers directly instead of relying on flawed perception of angles.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Bar Chart.** Convert the pie chart into a bar chart. This immediately makes comparisons easier and more accurate for the viewer.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Sort and Annotate the Bar Chart.** Convert the data to a bar chart, sort the bars in a meaningful order (e.g., descending value), and consider annotating key differences or totals to guide the viewer's attention to the most important insights.

## Guidance

When designing a visualization where the user needs to find all data points that meet a specific value-based condition (i.e., filter the data), a bar chart is an effective choice.

## Why

A bar chart, especially when sorted, allows for easy visual scanning of values against a threshold. The common baseline provides a consistent starting point, and the length encoding of values makes it straightforward to visually identify all bars that extend past a certain point on the axis.

## When it applies

- The core user task is to identify a subset of data that meets a quantitative or ordinal condition.
- Example tasks: "Find all countries with a population over 100 million," or "Identify all products with a customer rating of 4 stars or higher."

## Exceptions

- **Two-Dimensional Filtering:** If the filtering condition involves two quantitative variables simultaneously (e.g., "Find all products with sales > $10,000 AND profit > 20%"), a scatterplot is more effective as it shows both variables at once.
- **Geographic Filtering:** If the condition is spatial (e.g., "Find all stores within a 10-mile radius"), a map is the necessary chart type.
- **High Cardinality Data:** With thousands of categories, a bar chart becomes unscrollable and ineffective. A data table with filtering controls or more advanced visualizations would be better.

## Trade-offs

- A bar chart can take up significant vertical or horizontal space, especially as the number of categories grows. In a highly interactive dashboard, a simple filterable data table might be faster for users who already know what they are looking for.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Unsorted Bars:** The bars are in a random or alphabetical order, forcing the user to scan every single bar individually to check if it meets the condition, which is slow and error-prone.
- **Inappropriate Chart for Filtering:** A pie chart or treemap is being used for a filtering task. These charts lack a quantitative axis, making it impossible to visually check values against a threshold.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Sort the Bars.** If your bar chart is unsorted, sort it by the quantitative value. This groups all items meeting the condition together, making them easy to identify as a single block.
- **Moderate Redesign: Add a Reference Line.** In a static or interactive chart, draw a line on the chart representing the filter threshold (e.g., a line at the "passing grade" level). This makes the comparison even more explicit and reduces cognitive load.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Implement Interactive Filtering.** In a digital medium, allow users to directly select or "brush" the bars that meet their criteria. Alternatively, provide filter controls (like sliders or input boxes) that dynamically update the view to show only the relevant data points, fulfilling the filter task directly.
