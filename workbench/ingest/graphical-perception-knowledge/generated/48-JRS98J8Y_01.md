## Guidance

When representing ordered data (quantitative or ordinal), use a colormap that has a clear and intuitive perceptual order. A viewer should be able to correctly determine if one color represents a higher or lower value than another without relying on the legend.

## Why

If a colormap lacks a clear perceptual order, viewers cannot perform basic comparison tasks. They cannot judge the relative magnitude of values, identify trends, or determine direction (is it increasing or decreasing?). This forces them into a slow, error-prone cycle of looking at a color in the chart, finding it in the legend, reading the value, and repeating for every comparison. This dramatically increases cognitive load and undermines the purpose of the visualization.

### Core Principle

Visual encodings should match the structure of the data. Ordered data requires an ordered visual representation. The human visual system primarily uses luminance to infer order and shape.

## When it applies

- When using a continuous color scale to represent ordered data (quantitative or ordinal).
- For tasks that involve ranking, comparing, or identifying the direction of data.
- For any chart where color represents a continuous value, such as a heatmap or a choropleth map.

## Exceptions

- **Categorical Data:** Order is not a meaningful concept for unordered categories. Here, the goal is discriminability, not order.
- **Diverging Data:** A diverging colormap has two opposite orders moving away from a central, neutral color. It is effectively two ordered colormaps joined together. It has order, but it is not a single monotonic progression.

## Trade-offs

- **Constrained Color Choice:** Requiring a strict perceptual order, especially one based on monotonically increasing luminance, limits the available palette. You cannot, for example, have a colormap that goes from dark blue to bright yellow and back to dark red, as this would violate the principle of order.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Three-Color Test:** Pick three colors from different parts of the colormap. Can you (or a colleague) confidently place them in order from low to high? If not, the colormap lacks intuitive order.
- **The Squint Test:** Squint at the visualization. Does the form dissolve into a series of disconnected color patches instead of a coherent shape? This often indicates a lack of luminance order. The rainbow colormap fails this test, as the bright yellow band in the middle breaks the form.
- **Legend Dependence:** You find yourself constantly moving your eyes back and forth between the chart and the legend to understand basic relationships.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Contours or Labels.** If you must use a poorly-ordered colormap, overlaying explicit contour lines or data labels can provide the ordering information that the colormap fails to convey. This is a crutch, not a fix.

- **Moderate Approach: Use a Single-Hue or Two-Hue Progression.** A simple way to ensure order is to use a sequential colormap that varies primarily in lightness and saturation within a single hue (e.g., light blue to dark blue) or between two adjacent hues (e.g., yellow to green). ColorBrewer is an excellent source for these.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Use a Monotonic Luminance Ramp.** The most robust way to create perceptual order is to ensure the colormap's luminance is monotonically increasing (or decreasing). Colormaps like `Viridis` or a simple greyscale ramp are designed this way. This directly leverages the visual system's ability to perceive shape from shading.
