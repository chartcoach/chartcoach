## Guidance

When a user's task is to remember and compare a small set of quantitative values (e.g., 3-5 items), and the primary goal is to minimize systematic error (bias), consider using a length-based encoding (e.g., misaligned bars or a dot plot) instead of a standard position-based bar chart.

## Why

Classic experiments show position on a common scale (bar charts) is best for *perceptual* accuracy in direct, side-by-side comparisons. However, this study suggests that for *memory-based* tasks, the story changes. Storing and recalling multiple positions may be more susceptible to systemic bias (over/underestimation) than storing and recalling multiple lengths. Length encoding appeared to be more robust against this type of memory distortion.

### Core Principle

The optimal visual channel for a task depends on the cognitive processes involved (e.g., perception vs. memory), not just on a static perceptual hierarchy. Rankings derived from one type of task may not generalize to others.

## When it applies

-   When the main goal is to ensure that users' recalled judgments are, on average, accurate (low bias), even if they are less consistent (lower precision).
-   When designing charts where users must remember a few key values to compare them against each other, such as in a series of small multiples.
-   For tasks involving 4 to 8 data points, where the effect was observed.

## Exceptions

-   For simple two-value, non-memory-based comparisons, where position encoding is still likely superior.
-   When precision (consistency of judgment) is more important than accuracy (low bias). The same study found other channels, like area, produced more precise (though more biased) responses.
-   When a common baseline is essential for the task. By definition, length-based charts like misaligned bars or Gantt charts lack a common starting point.

## Trade-offs

-   Length judgments are generally less precise (more variable) than position judgments, even if they are less biased in this specific context. Users' responses may be more scattered around the true value.
-   Charts that rely on length encoding without a common baseline (like misaligned bars) can make part-to-whole comparisons impossible and other comparison tasks more difficult.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Systematic Bar Chart Bias:** Users consistently underestimate the average of a set of bars or show other predictable patterns of misestimation when recalling values from a bar chart.
-   **Context-Dependent Errors:** The errors users make in recalling bar heights change significantly depending on the heights of surrounding bars.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Approach: Switch to a Dot Plot.** Instead of a bar chart, use a dot plot. This still uses position but de-emphasizes the length of the bar, which may mitigate some biases.
-   **Moderate Approach: Use Misaligned Bars.** If a common baseline is not critical, switch from a standard bar chart to a misaligned bar chart (where bars start at different points). This forces a length judgment, which may be less biased for recall.
-   **Comprehensive Approach: Test and Validate.** If low bias is critical for your application, run a small user study comparing a standard bar chart with a length-based alternative (like a dot plot or misaligned bars) for your specific task to see which yields more accurate recalled judgments from your users.
