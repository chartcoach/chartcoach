## Guidance

To encode quantitative data, use visual channels that viewers perceive most accurately. The generally accepted hierarchy, from most to least effective, is:

1.  **Position** (e.g., scatter plots, dot plots)
2.  **Length** (e.g., bar charts)
3.  **Angle / Slope** (e.g., pie charts, slopegraphs)
4.  **Area** (e.g., bubble charts, treemaps)
5.  **Volume**
6.  **Color Saturation / Lightness** (e.g., sequential color in heatmaps)
7.  **Color Hue** (less effective for quantity)

## Why

Humans are physiologically better at judging differences in position along a common axis and differences in length. We are significantly less accurate when judging differences in angles, areas, or the saturation of colors. Using a less effective channel (like area in a bubble chart) when a more effective one (like position in a dot plot) is available increases the risk of misinterpretation and reduces the precision of the message.

### Core Principle

**Match the encoding to human perceptual strengths.** The most effective visualizations use visual channels that our brains can decode quickly and accurately for the specific task at hand.

## When it applies

-   When the primary goal is for the audience to make precise comparisons or rankings of numerical data.
-   When designing any chart that encodes quantitative values, including bar charts, scatter plots, bubble charts, and pie charts.
-   When choosing between different chart types to represent the same data.

## Exceptions

-   When the goal is to show a general overview or pattern rather than precise comparisons, less effective channels like area or color saturation can be acceptable (e.g., a heatmap for showing hotspots).
-   When space is extremely limited, area-based charts like treemaps can be a necessary compromise to show hierarchical data.
-   When the metaphor is very strong (e.g., a pie chart for a budget), but be aware of the perceptual trade-offs.

## Trade-offs

-   **Information Density vs. Perceptual Accuracy:** More accurate encodings like position (dot plots) sometimes feel less dense than less accurate ones like area (treemaps). You may trade some perceptual accuracy for the ability to display more data in a smaller space.
-   **Aesthetics vs. Accuracy:** Bubble charts (area) are often considered aesthetically pleasing but are perceptually inferior to bar charts (length) for comparisons.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **"Which bubble is bigger?":** Viewers struggle to determine the relative size of marks in a bubble chart or slices in a pie chart.
-   **Misleading Proportions:** A treemap gives a general sense of size, but viewers cannot accurately state that one rectangle is, for example, 2.5 times larger than another.
-   **Inaccurate Takeaways:** When asked to estimate values from a chart using area or angle, users' answers have high error rates.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** If you must use a chart with a less accurate encoding (like a bubble or pie chart), add direct data labels (e.g., "42%"). This provides an "escape hatch" for viewers to read the exact values instead of relying on flawed perceptual judgments.

-   **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Better Encoding.** Change the chart type to use a more effective channel. For example, convert a pie chart (angle/area) into a bar chart (length/position). This directly improves the viewer's ability to make accurate comparisons.

-   **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Position on a Common Scale.** For the highest precision, redesign the visualization to use position on a common, aligned scale. A dot plot is often the ideal choice, as it purely uses position to encode value, minimizing perceptual distortion.

## Guidance

When encoding nominal (unordered categorical) data, use visual channels that are easily distinguishable but do not imply a quantitative order. The most effective channels, from most to least effective, are:

1.  **Position** (e.g., separating categories on an axis)
2.  **Color Hue** (e.g., blue, green, orange)
3.  **Texture**
4.  **Shape**
5.  **Length / Area / Saturation** (Avoid these, as they imply order)

## Why

Nominal data consists of unordered categories (e.g., 'Apple', 'Orange', 'Banana'). An effective visualization should make it easy to tell these categories apart without accidentally suggesting that one is "more" or "higher" than another. Channels like color hue and shape are easily differentiable without creating a ranking. In contrast, channels like length (bar charts) or area (bubble charts) are strongly perceived as representing quantity, creating a misleading order where none exists.

### Core Principle

**The Expressiveness Principle:** A visualization should express all the information in the data, and *only* the information in the data. Using a visual channel that implies order (like length) to encode data that has no order (nominal categories) violates this principle.

## When it applies

-   When choosing colors, shapes, or other visual marks to distinguish between unordered categories.
-   When deciding between a bar chart and a dot plot for a categorical axis.
-   When creating a legend for a chart with nominal data.

## Exceptions

-   If the categories have a conventional, though not strictly numerical, order (e.g., alphabetical), using position is acceptable and often helpful for lookup tasks.
-   When there is a single category, length or area is fine as there are no other categories to create a misleading comparison with.

## Trade-offs

-   **Number of Categories:** Color hue is very effective, but the number of easily distinguishable hues is limited (typically 5-8). For categories beyond this number, you may need to combine hue with another channel like shape, or choose a different chart type.
-   **Legibility:** While a dot plot is more "correct" than a bar chart for a nominal axis, audiences may be more familiar with bar charts. You might trade theoretical correctness for audience familiarity.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Implicit Ranking:** A chart appears to rank categories that have no inherent order (e.g., a bar chart of "Sales by Department" seems to imply that the departments themselves are ranked).
-   **Ordered Colors for Unordered Data:** A sequential color palette (e.g., light green to dark green) is used to represent distinct categories like 'North', 'South', 'East', 'West'.
-   **Size Implies Importance:** Different categories are represented by circles of different sizes, suggesting some are more "important" or have a higher value that is not in the data.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Change the Channel.** If you are using a perceptually ordered channel like color saturation or size to represent nominal categories, switch to a qualitative channel like color hue or shape.

-   **Moderate Redesign: Switch from Bar Chart to Dot Plot.** If you are using a bar chart with a nominal category axis, convert it to a dot plot. This removes the misleading length encoding while retaining the accurate position encoding for the quantitative value.

-   **Comprehensive Redesign: Use a Faceted Display (Small Multiples).** If you have many categories, instead of trying to encode them all with color or shape in a single chart, break the data into a series of small, consistent charts, one for each category. This uses position (the most effective channel) to separate the categories.

## Guidance

When encoding ordinal data (ordered categories like "Low, Medium, High" or "Bad, Neutral, Good"), use visual channels that have a natural perceptual order. The most effective channels are:

1.  **Position** (e.g., ordering categories along an axis)
2.  **Color Saturation / Lightness** (e.g., a sequential palette from light to dark)
3.  **Color Hue** (only if the hues form a clear sequential progression)
4.  **Texture** (e.g., from sparse to dense)

Avoid using unordered channels like shape or a qualitative (rainbow) color palette.

## Why

Ordinal data has an inherent sequence, and the visualization should reflect that. The human visual system naturally perceives a progression in lightness or saturation (from light to dark) and density (from sparse to dense). However, a "rainbow" of different hues (e.g., red, green, blue) does not have a universally agreed-upon order, forcing viewers to memorize the legend and increasing cognitive load. Matching the data's ordered structure to a visually ordered encoding makes the chart intuitive and easier to interpret.

### Core Principle

**Match the visual structure to the data structure.** Ordered data should be represented by perceptually ordered visual variables.

## When it applies

-   Visualizing survey data with Likert scales (e.g., Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree).
-   Encoding any ordered categories, such as "Small, Medium, Large" or "Bronze, Silver, Gold".
-   Choosing a color palette for a choropleth map or heatmap that displays binned, ordered data.

## Exceptions

-   If the ordinal categories also have a very strong, conventional color association (e.g., "cold" to "hot" with a blue-to-red diverging scale), a multi-hue palette can be effective. However, this relies on cultural conventions and is not universally applicable.

## Trade-offs

-   **Discriminability vs. Order:** A single-hue sequential palette is great for showing order, but it can be harder to distinguish between adjacent steps than a palette with multiple hues. You are trading some discriminability for a clearer sense of order.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Rainbows for Ranks:** A "rainbow" or qualitative color palette is used to encode clearly ordered data.
-   **Legend Whiplash:** The viewer has to constantly look back and forth between the chart and the legend to understand the order of the categories.
-gaps in the logic.
-   **The "Fruit Salad" Effect:** The colors on the chart look like a random assortment of hues with no clear progression, despite the underlying data having a distinct order.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Switch to a Sequential Palette.** Change your color scheme from a qualitative (rainbow) palette to a sequential one. Most visualization tools have built-in "single-hue" or "sequential" color palettes (e.g., light blue to dark blue).

-   **Moderate Redesign: Use a Perceptually Uniform Palette.** Instead of a basic single-hue ramp, use a perceptually uniform sequential palette (like Viridis, Plasma, or a ColorBrewer palette). These are designed so that a given step in data value corresponds to an equal step in perceived color, making the representation more accurate.

-   **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Position.** If possible, redesign the chart to use position to represent the order. For example, instead of a colored scatterplot, you could use a small-multiples display where each chart represents one of the ordinal categories, arranged in the correct order. This leverages the most effective visual channel for showing order.
