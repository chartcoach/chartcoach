## Guidance

When viewers need to accurately compare or rank the values of different categories, use a bar chart instead of a pie chart.

## Why

Bar charts encode data using length and position along a common baseline. Pie charts encode data using angle and area. Humans are demonstrably better at perceiving small differences in length from a common scale than they are at perceiving differences in angles or areas. This makes bar charts a more accurate and efficient tool for comparison tasks.

### Core Principle

Humans judge quantities more accurately by comparing positions along a common scale than by comparing angles or areas. The visualization should use encodings that humans can perceive most accurately for the intended task.

## When it applies

- The primary task is to compare categories to each other (e.g., "Which region had the highest sales? By how much?").
- You need to show rankings or relative differences between categories.
- You have more than 3-4 categories, or the values of the categories are relatively close, requiring precise comparison.

## Exceptions

- **Simple Part-to-Whole:** If the primary goal is simply to show a single part's relationship to the whole (e.g., "Yes votes were 75% of the total") and there are only 2-3 categories, a pie chart can be acceptable and is immediately recognizable to a general audience.
- **Clock Metaphor:** If the data is cyclical (e.g., hours in a day), the circular layout of a pie chart might reinforce a useful metaphor, though a bar chart would still be more accurate for comparisons.

## Trade-offs

- Pie charts are very familiar to general audiences for showing parts of a whole. Switching to a bar chart might feel less intuitive for this specific "composition" message, even if it's more perceptually accurate for the "comparison" sub-task.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Pac-Man" Chart:** A pie chart has more than 4-5 slices, making it look cluttered and difficult to read.
- **Difficult Comparisons:** It is hard to tell which of two similarly-sized slices is actually bigger without reading a label.
- **3D or Exploded Pies:** The chart uses 3D effects or has "exploded" slices, which further distort the angles and areas, making perception even less accurate and potentially misleading.
- **Multiple Pies:** You are trying to compare values across two or more separate pie charts. This is an extremely difficult perceptual task.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** If you must use a pie chart, add direct data labels (values or percentages) to each slice. This allows viewers to read and compare the numbers directly instead of relying on flawed perception of angles.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Bar Chart.** Convert the pie chart into a bar chart. This immediately makes comparisons easier and more accurate for the viewer.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Sort and Annotate the Bar Chart.** Convert the data to a bar chart, sort the bars in a meaningful order (e.g., descending value), and consider annotating key differences or totals to guide the viewer's attention to the most important insights.
