## Guidance

Do not use horizon graphs when the user needs to find time series patterns with similar shapes, regardless of their height (amplitude) or vertical position (offset). In these scenarios, use a line chart or colorfield instead.

## Why

Horizon graphs work by slicing the y-axis into discrete colored bands. A small change in a signal's amplitude or offset can push it across a band's boundary, causing a dramatic change in its color. This visual discontinuity makes two algorithmically similar shapes appear very different to the human eye. This perceptual effect runs directly counter to similarity measures that use Z-normalization to ignore amplitude and offset.

### Core Principle

A visualization's encoding should not create large visual discontinuities for small, conceptually unimportant changes in the data. The hard boundaries of horizon graph bands violate this principle when amplitude is meant to be ignored.

## When it applies

- When the user's task is to find patterns of a similar shape, even if one is "taller" or "shifted up" compared to another.
- When the underlying similarity algorithm uses a form of normalization (like Z-normalization) to achieve amplitude and offset invariance. Using a horizon graph in this context will create a frustrating mismatch between what the user sees and what the algorithm returns.

## Exceptions

- If amplitude and offset are critically important features for the similarity comparison, then a horizon graph's sensitivity to them could be considered a feature, not a bug. However, a standard line chart on a fixed scale would likely communicate these differences more clearly.

## Trade-offs

- By avoiding horizon graphs, you may lose some of their space-efficiency. However, for this specific task, the gain in perceptual accuracy and alignment with the user's goal far outweighs the cost in screen real estate.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Color Dominates Shape:** A user dismisses a pattern as "completely different" because it's a different color (e.g., red vs. blue), even though its shape is identical to the query.
- **Algorithm Mismatch:** A Z-normalized search algorithm returns a result that the user, looking at a horizon graph, finds nonsensical. For example, the algorithm ranks two patterns as highly similar, but the user sees one as a single blue peak and the other as a two-banded blue-and-red peak.
- **Flat Signals Exaggerated:** Relatively flat, low-amplitude signals appear noisy or overly prominent because they hover near a band boundary, causing colors to flicker.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach: Switch to a Line Chart.** The simplest and most effective fix is to replace the horizon graph with a standard line chart. This removes the discrete color bands that cause the perceptual distortion.

- **Moderate approach: Use a Colorfield.** A colorfield (or heatmap) visualization can also support amplitude-invariant comparison. The study found it performed similarly to a line chart for this task. This may be a good option if you need to display many time series compactly.

- **Comprehensive approach: Normalize Data Before Visualizing.** If you must show multiple series and their absolute values are not important, you can Z-normalize the data *before* plotting it with line charts in small multiples. This puts every series on the same scale (mean=0, std=1), making their shapes directly comparable and removing amplitude/offset as a visual variable.
