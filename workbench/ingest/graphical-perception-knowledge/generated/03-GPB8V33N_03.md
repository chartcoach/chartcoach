## Guidance

For identifying which items in a multivariate dataset have changed the most over time or across ordered stages, prefer Parallel Coordinates Plots (PCPs).

## Why

When the parallel axes of a PCP are ordered chronologically (e.g., Q1, Q2, Q3, Q4), the line segments connecting the axes directly visualize change over time. The slope of a line segment is a powerful perceptual cue for the rate of change: steep slopes indicate large changes, while flat lines indicate stability. This allows the human eye to quickly scan the visualization and identify the polylines (i.e., data items) with the most volatile or dynamic behavior.

## When it applies

- When the primary task is to find which items have changed the most or least.
- When the data has multiple attributes that represent ordered stages, such as points in time (e.g., yearly sales) or experimental phases.
- When comparing the trajectories of many different items simultaneously.

## Exceptions

- **Comparing Only Two Time Points:** If you are only comparing two points in time (e.g., "before" and "after"), a simpler chart like a slope chart or a dumbbell plot can be more direct and less visually complex.
- **Very Few Items:** If you are tracking change for only one or two items, a standard line chart is often clearer and more familiar to the audience.

## Trade-offs

- **Clutter:** With many data items (lines), the plot can become a "spaghetti graph," making it difficult to trace individual trajectories without interactive highlighting.
- **Axis Scaling:** The perception of slope is dependent on the scaling of the axes. If axes have different scales, it can be misleading to visually compare the steepness of lines across different segments. Normalizing the data may be necessary.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Mental Integration:** Viewers are forced to compare values across multiple bar charts or a scatter plot matrix to manually calculate the differences over time.
- **Lost in the Spaghetti:** In a standard line chart with many overlapping lines, it's impossible to track a single item's trajectory from start to finish.
- **Table Subtraction:** Users are exporting data to a spreadsheet to manually subtract columns and find the largest differences.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Create Slope Charts.** If you have many items but only two time points, use a series of small multiple slope charts. This declutters the view while still using the powerful perceptual cue of slope.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Switch to a Parallel Coordinates Plot.** For three or more time points, represent the data using a PCP with the axes ordered chronologically. This will make the trajectories and relative changes of all items visible in a single view. Consider using color to highlight specific items of interest.
