## Guidance

For comparing quantitative values, encode data using position along a common, aligned scale (e.g., bar charts, dot plots) instead of using angle (pie charts) or area (bubble charts, treemaps).

## Why

Humans are perceptually more accurate and faster at judging differences in position along a single axis than they are at judging differences in angles or areas. Using a less accurate encoding like angle or area increases the risk of misinterpretation, where viewers may fail to see important differences or may perceive differences that aren't significant.

### Core Principle

Make the most important comparisons the easiest and most accurate to perform. The visual encoding should match the limits of human perception for the given task.

## When it applies

-   When the primary goal is for the audience to accurately compare or rank quantities.
-   When displaying values that are relatively close to each other, where small differences matter.
-   When working with a general audience that may not be trained in reading complex charts.

## Exceptions

-   **Composition Task:** If the primary goal is to show a part-to-whole relationship and the number of slices is very small (2-3), a pie chart can be effective at conveying a simple proportion (e.g., "about a quarter"). However, a bar chart is still better for precise comparison.
-   **Geographic Data:** For maps, area is inherently used to represent geographic regions (e.g., country size). Other channels like color (for choropleths) or the area of a separate symbol (proportional symbol map) must be used.
-   **Limited Space:** A treemap (using area) can be a space-efficient way to show a large, hierarchical dataset, even if it makes precise comparisons difficult. The goal shifts from comparison to seeing the overall structure.

## Trade-offs

-   **Familiarity:** Pie charts are widely recognized, and switching to a bar chart might feel less familiar to some audiences, even if it's more effective.
-   **Aesthetics:** Some designers may prefer the circular or organic look of pie charts or bubble charts for aesthetic reasons, potentially sacrificing perceptual accuracy.
-   **Space:** Bar charts and dot plots typically require more linear space (especially with many categories) compared to a single pie chart.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **"Which slice is bigger?"** Viewers are unsure which of two similarly sized slices in a pie chart is larger.
-   **3D or Exploded Slices:** The use of 3D perspective or exploding slices, which further distorts angles and areas, making comparison nearly impossible.
-   **Many Slices:** A pie chart has more than 5-7 slices, creating a cluttered "color wheel" where comparison is futile.
-   **Bubble Overlap:** In a bubble chart, bubbles overlap, obscuring their true size and making area judgments difficult.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** If you must use a pie or bubble chart, add direct data labels (e.g., "42%") to each element. This provides an "escape hatch" by allowing viewers to read the numbers directly instead of relying on their (inaccurate) perceptual judgment of angle or area.

-   **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Donut Chart.** If a part-to-whole circular form is desired, a donut chart can be slightly better than a pie chart. It de-emphasizes angle and encourages viewers to compare arc length, which is a marginally more accurate judgment (closer to length).

-   **Comprehensive Redesign: Use a Bar Chart.** The most effective solution is to replace the pie or area-based chart with a bar chart. This encodes the quantitative values using length/position along a common baseline, which aligns with how our visual system most accurately perceives and compares magnitudes.

## Guidance

When designing treemaps or other charts where viewers must compare rectangular areas, avoid algorithms that force all shapes to be perfectly square (i.e., have an aspect ratio of 1:1). A slight deviation from a perfect square can improve the accuracy of area comparisons.

## Why

Viewers often use a mental shortcut (heuristic) to judge 2D area: they compare 1D lengths of the sides. When comparing two perfect squares, this heuristic fails because all sides are equal, forcing a more difficult and less accurate pure area judgment. When rectangles have a slight aspect ratio, viewers can compare the different side lengths, which paradoxically helps them make a better overall judgment of the area difference. The common wisdom to make treemap rectangles "as square as possible" may be perceptually suboptimal.

### Core Principle

Design should account for the cognitive shortcuts and heuristics people use, even if those heuristics are not mathematically pure. Sometimes, a "less perfect" geometric form can lead to "more perfect" perception.

## When it applies

-   When using treemaps, squarified cartograms, or any other visualization that uses the area of rectangles to encode quantitative data.
-   When the primary task is for viewers to compare the sizes of different rectangles.

## Exceptions

-   **Overall Structure is Key:** If the goal of the treemap is not precise comparison but to show the hierarchical structure and relative weight of components in a large dataset, the accuracy of individual comparisons is less critical.
-   **Aesthetic or Space Constraints:** A squarified layout is very space-efficient and can be aesthetically pleasing. These goals might take priority over a small gain in perceptual accuracy, especially if direct value labels are also provided.

## Trade-offs

-   **Space Efficiency:** Allowing for non-square rectangles might lead to layouts with long, thin "slivers" that are hard to label and visually unappealing. Squarified algorithms were invented to prevent this.
-   **Aesthetics:** A layout of perfect squares can look more orderly and clean than one with varied aspect ratios. Deviating from this may be seen as less polished.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Perfectly Tiled Squares:** Your treemap consists of a grid of rectangles that are all nearly perfect squares.
-   **User Inaccuracy:** In user testing, people struggle to accurately identify the larger of two similarly-sized rectangles in your treemap.
-   **Over-reliance on Labels:** Viewers report that they cannot judge the sizes at all and rely exclusively on reading the text labels to make comparisons.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Add Data Labels.** Always include direct value labels on treemap rectangles. This gives users a way to compare values precisely, bypassing the difficult perceptual task of area comparison.

-   **Moderate Adjustment: Tweak Algorithm Parameters.** If using a layout algorithm like `d3.treemapSquarify`, be aware of its behavior. While it's difficult to force it to be *less* square, you can provide an alternative algorithm to the layout generator if you want to experiment with different aspect ratios.

-   **Comprehensive Re-evaluation: Consider a Different Chart Type.** If precise comparison is the most critical task, a treemap may be the wrong choice. A bar chart (or a sorted series of bar charts for hierarchical data) will enable far more accurate comparisons by using position and length instead of area.
