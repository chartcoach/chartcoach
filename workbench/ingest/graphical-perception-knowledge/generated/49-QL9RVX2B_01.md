## Guidance

When the primary goal is for a user to assess the relationship (correlation) between two quantitative variables, a scatterplot is the most effective choice.

## Why

Scatterplots encode two variables using position on the X and Y axes. The resulting visual pattern of the points (e.g., a tight line, a diffuse cloud) provides a direct, powerful, and relatively intuitive representation of the strength and direction of a correlation. Other chart types, like parallel coordinates, have been shown to cause viewers to underestimate the strength of the relationship.

### Core Principle

Visual encodings that map data attributes directly to emergent visual properties (like the shape of a point cloud) can make complex statistical relationships easier to perceive.

## When it applies

- The dataset contains two (or more) quantitative variables.
- The analytical task is to determine if, how, and to what extent the variables are related (e.g., "Is there a relationship between advertising spend and sales?").

## Exceptions

- **Overplotting:** If you have a very large number of data points (thousands or more), a standard scatterplot can become a solid blob, hiding the true density and correlation. In this case, specialized techniques are needed.
- **Time as a Variable:** If one of the variables is time, a line chart is the more conventional choice for showing a trend over time. For showing the correlation *between* two time series, a connected scatterplot can be effective.

## Trade-offs

- A scatterplot focuses on the aggregate pattern and makes it difficult to look up the exact value for a specific data point without interaction (like tooltips).
- They require a certain level of graphical literacy to interpret correlation from the point cloud.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Wrong Chart Type:** You are using a line chart, bar chart, or parallel coordinates plot and asking the user to judge a correlation.
- **Overplotting ("The Hairball"):** The chart is an unreadable, solid mass of overlapping points, obscuring any underlying pattern.
- **User Confusion:** Viewers are unable to describe the relationship between the two variables or misinterpret the strength of the correlation.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Change Chart Type.** If using another chart type like a bar or line chart for a correlation task, switch to a scatterplot.
- **Moderate Approach: Address Overplotting.** If the scatterplot is overplotted, reduce the opacity (alpha) of the points, use smaller marks, or randomly sample the data to reveal the underlying structure.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Use Density Visualization.** For severe overplotting, switch from a standard scatterplot to a 2D density plot (also known as a heatmap) or a contour plot. These explicitly visualize the number of points in each region, revealing both the correlation and the density of the data simultaneously.
