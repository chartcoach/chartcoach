## Guidance

For tasks that require comparing or ranking the magnitude of different categories, use a bar chart instead of a pie or donut chart.

## Why

Humans are significantly better at accurately judging and comparing lengths from a common baseline (the core mechanism of a bar chart) than they are at judging angles, arcs, or areas (the mechanisms of pie and donut charts). Using a bar chart reduces perceptual error and cognitive load, leading to faster, more accurate, and more confident interpretations of the data.

### Core Principle

Make the most important comparisons the easiest and most accurate to perceive. Visual encodings that map data to perceptually "weaker" channels like angle or area hinder comparison, while "stronger" channels like position and length support it.

## When it applies

- When the primary goal is to compare the values of different parts of a whole.
- When you need the audience to be able to rank categories accurately from largest to smallest.
- When you have more than 2-3 categories, as perceptual difficulty with pie charts increases rapidly with the number of slices.

## Exceptions

- **Retrieving a single value:** If the *only* task is for a user to look up the approximate value of a single, specific category (e.g., "What percentage is Category A?"), some research (Redmond, 2017) has found pie charts can perform as well as or slightly better than bar charts. However, this is a very narrow use case.
- **Binary (two-part) composition:** For showing a simple part-to-whole relationship with only two categories (e.g., 75% vs 25%), a pie chart can be effective as the comparison is trivial and focuses attention on the part-to-whole nature of the data.

## Trade-offs

- **Part-to-whole emphasis:** A pie chart more obviously signals that the data represents parts of a whole. A bar chart can also represent this but may require an explicit title or context to make it clear.
- **Space:** A pie chart can sometimes be more compact, but this often comes at the cost of clarity and the need for a separate legend.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Impossible to Rank" Test:** Look at the smaller slices of your pie chart. Can you confidently, without reading the labels, rank them from smallest to largest? If not, the chart is failing at its comparison task.
- **Back-and-Forth:** Viewers have to repeatedly look back and forth between the legend and the slices to understand the chart.
- **Too Many Slices:** The pie chart has more than 4-5 slices, resembling a colorful wagon wheel where no meaningful comparison is possible.
- **Similar Slices:** Two or more slices are very close in value (e.g., 22% and 24%), making them impossible to distinguish by angle alone.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** If you must use a pie chart, add direct, clear percentage or value labels to every slice. This provides a "cognitive escape hatch," allowing users to compare the numbers directly instead of relying on flawed angle perception.
- **Moderate Redesign: Sort the Slices.** If using a pie chart, sort the slices from largest to smallest, starting at the 12 o'clock position and going clockwise. This makes it slightly easier to follow but does not fix the core perceptual issue.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Switch to a Bar Chart.** The most effective solution is to change the chart type. A simple bar chart, with categories on the y-axis and values on the x-axis, sorted by value, provides the clearest and most perceptually accurate representation for comparison tasks.

## Guidance

When using the area of shapes (e.g., in bubble charts, treemaps, or cartograms) to represent quantitative data, be aware that viewers will systematically underestimate the differences between values. To ensure accurate interpretation, either choose a different visual encoding or apply a perceptual correction to the scale.

## Why

Human perception of area is non-linear and compressive. We do not perceive a 2x increase in a shape's area as a 2x increase in its value. This phenomenon, described by Stevens' Power Law, means that as the difference between two areas grows, our underestimation of that difference also grows. Encoding data directly to area without accounting for this bias can lead viewers to misjudge the magnitude of differences, a form of unintentional deception.

### Core Principle

The relationship between the physical properties of a visualization and the perceived quantities they represent should be as direct and linear as possible. If a perceptual channel (like area) is inherently non-linear, it risks misrepresenting the data unless corrected.

## When it applies

- Any chart that uses the 2D area of a shape to encode a quantitative value.
- **Bubble charts:** where the area of circles represents data.
- **Treemaps:** where the area of rectangles represents part-of-a-whole data.
- **Proportional symbol maps:** where glyphs (often circles) are scaled by area.

## Exceptions

- **When only rank order matters:** If the primary task is simply to determine which shape is largest, second largest, etc., and the precise magnitude of the differences is unimportant, the underestimation bias is less critical (though still present).
- **When values are directly labeled:** If every shape has its value clearly labeled, viewers can rely on reading the text to make precise comparisons, using the area encoding as a secondary, impressionistic guide. This provides a "cognitive escape hatch."

## Trade-offs

- **Using a more accurate encoding (like length):** A bar chart is more perceptually accurate than a bubble chart but can take up more space and may not handle a very large number of categories as elegantly.
- **Applying a perceptual correction:** You can adjust the area scale to counteract the bias (e.g., using a power scale). This makes the chart more perceptually accurate but adds a layer of abstraction and complexity to the design process. A common practice is to scale the *radius* of a circle linearly with the data, not the area, which results in an exponent of 0.5 and can partially mitigate the underestimation.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Large dynamic range:** The chart displays values with a wide range (e.g., the largest bubble is 100x the area of the smallest). The underestimation bias will be most severe in this case, making the largest bubble seem far less dominant than it truly is.
- **Reliance on visual comparison:** The chart lacks direct labels, forcing the viewer to rely entirely on comparing areas to understand the data.
- **Critical comparisons:** The purpose of the chart is to allow for precise comparisons of magnitude (e.g., comparing budget allocations), a task for which area is poorly suited.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** The simplest mitigation is to add clear data labels to each shape. This allows users to read the exact values and bypass their flawed perceptual system.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to Radius Scaling.** When using circles, scale the **radius** linearly with your data, not the area. This is mathematically equivalent to `area = data^2` and over-corrects in the other direction, but it is a common convention that can be easier for viewers to interpret than correctly-scaled areas. Be sure to note this choice in the chart's legend.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Switch to a Length/Position Encoding.** The most effective solution is to change the visual encoding to a more perceptually accurate one. Replace the bubble chart with a bar chart or a dot plot. This maps the data to length or position along a common scale, which humans perceive far more accurately and linearly.

## Guidance

When the primary task is to assess the relationship (correlation) between two quantitative variables, use a scatter plot.

## Why

A scatter plot maps the two variables to the x- and y-positions of points on a 2D Cartesian plane. The human visual system is exceptionally good at detecting patterns in the resulting cloud of points. We can intuitively and quickly assess the direction (positive or negative), strength (tightly clustered or widely dispersed), and shape (linear, curved) of the relationship. Other chart types, like parallel coordinates or table-based visualizations, obscure these patterns, making correlation much harder to perceive accurately.

### Core Principle

Choose the visualization that best matches the structure of the task. The task of assessing correlation is fundamentally about identifying a 2D pattern, and the scatter plot is the most direct graphical representation of that pattern.

## When it applies

- When you have two quantitative (numerical) variables.
- When your main question is "How are these two variables related?"
- When you want to see if the relationship is strong or weak, positive or negative, linear or non-linear.

## Exceptions

- **Time-series data:** If one of the variables is time and the data is ordered sequentially, a line chart is often a better choice. The lines in a line chart also effectively show correlation over time (i.e., auto-correlation or correlation between two series). Zeng & Battle (2023) note that line charts are also recommended for correlation tasks.
- **More than two variables:** For exploring correlations across many variables, a scatter plot matrix (SPLOM) or a correlation heatmap are more suitable, though each individual plot in a SPLOM is still a scatter plot.

## Trade-offs

- **Overplotting:** With very large datasets, a standard scatter plot can suffer from overplotting, where points are drawn on top of each other, obscuring the density of the data. This can be a significant problem for assessing correlation.
- **Read-out of exact values:** While excellent for showing the overall pattern, a dense scatter plot can make it difficult to look up the exact values for a specific point.

## Signs of Trouble

- **No discernible pattern:** You have used a chart type other than a scatter plot (e.g., parallel coordinates, two separate bar charts) and are trying to mentally connect the values to infer a relationship.
- **Overplotting:** Your scatter plot is a dense, solid blob of color, making it impossible to see variations in data density. The correlation may be obscured.
- **Incorrect Scales:** The axes do not cover the full range of the data, or non-linear scales are used without clear justification, potentially distorting the perceived correlation.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Check for Overplotting.** If your scatter plot is too dense, reduce the opacity of the points (alpha blending) to reveal areas of higher density. This can make the underlying correlation much clearer.
- **Moderate Approach: Bin the Data.** For very large datasets, use 2D binning to create a heatmap-style scatter plot (a "density plot"). This explicitly shows the number of points in different regions of the plot, solving overplotting and clearly revealing the shape of the correlation.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Use the Right Chart.** If you are not using a scatter plot for a two-variable correlation task, switch to one. It is the most perceptually effective tool for the job. If you have many variables, consider a scatter plot matrix or a heatmap.

## Guidance

When using shape to encode categorical data, select shapes from different perceptual families (e.g., filled vs. open, geometric vs. line-based, round vs. angular) to maximize their discriminability. Avoid using shapes that are too similar, such as a circle and a square, or a plus and an 'x'.

## Why

Not all shapes are created equal in terms of perceptual distance. Viewers naturally group shapes based on shared properties like "pointiness," "roundness," "filled-ness," or line-based structure. Shapes from within the same group are perceptually closer and therefore easier to confuse. By selecting a diverse set of shapes from different groups, you maximize the perceptual distance between your categories, making the chart easier and faster to read and reducing the risk of misinterpretation.

### Core Principle

Maximize the perceptual discriminability of visual encodings used for different categories. The harder it is to tell two categories apart, the higher the cognitive load and the greater the chance of error.

## When it applies

- In scatter plots or strip plots where shape is used to distinguish different categories of points.
- In maps where different types of locations are marked with different glyphs (proportional symbol maps).
- Any chart using a discrete shape legend, especially when there are 3-7 categories. (Beyond ~7 categories, shape becomes very difficult to use effectively).

## Exceptions

- **When shapes have semantic meaning:** If you are using universally recognized symbols (e.g., a male and female icon, an airplane and a car icon), their semantic meaning will override purely perceptual grouping. In this case, using the semantically correct shape is more important than maximizing perceptual distance.
- **Redundant encoding:** If shape is used as a secondary, redundant encoding alongside a stronger channel like color, the discriminability of the shapes is less critical. However, easily distinguishable shapes are still preferable.

## Trade-offs

- **Aesthetics vs. Clarity:** A set of highly uniform shapes (e.g., all filled circles of different colors) might appear cleaner or more aesthetically pleasing than a motley collection of different shapes. The latter, however, will be more robust and accessible, especially for users with color vision deficiency who may rely on shape.
- **Number of categories:** It is difficult to find more than about 5-7 shapes that are both highly discriminable and not overly complex. This limits the utility of shape as a primary encoding for data with high cardinality.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Squint and Confuse" Test:** Squint at your chart. Do the different categories of shapes blur into a single, indistinguishable group?
- **Similar Silhouettes:** The shapes you've chosen have very similar overall envelopes (e.g., a circle and a square of the same size, or a plus and an x).
- **Frequent Legend Checks:** You or your users have to constantly refer back to the legend to remember which shape corresponds to which category.
- **Small Multiples Inconsistency:** You have a small multiple plot where a specific shape seems to change its appearance or becomes hard to identify in some of the panels.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Diversify One Shape.** If you are using two similar shapes (e.g., circle and square), change one to be from a different perceptual group. For example, change the square to a triangle (different angles) or a plus sign (line-based).
- **Moderate Redesign: Create a Perceptually Diverse Palette.** Audit your shape palette. Ensure you have a mix of:
    - **Filled vs. Open:** (● vs. ○)
    - **Angular vs. Round:** (▲ vs. ●)
    - **Symmetrical vs. Asymmetrical/Oriented:** (+ vs. ►)
    - **Line-based vs. Solid:** (x vs. ■)
- **Comprehensive Approach: Re-order and Prioritize.** Based on the re-ordered palette from Demiralp et al.'s findings (Figure 16), prioritize using the most distinct shapes first for your most important categories. Their recommended order for high discriminability starts with: `+, ▷, □, ×, ▽, <, *`. This provides a research-backed starting point for a highly effective shape palette.
