## Guidance

If animation is not an option, use an overlaid (superposed) layout to help viewers find the largest change between two datasets. This is especially effective for slope charts and line charts.

## Why

Placing data from two series in the same coordinate space (co-location) dramatically reduces the cognitive and perceptual load of comparison. Viewers do not need to shift their gaze back and forth between charts or hold values in working memory. Instead, they can directly perceive the difference between marks (e.g., the distance between two lines or the ends of two bars) within their focal area, a much faster and more accurate perceptual task.

### Core Principle

Reduce the cognitive work required to decode your visualization. By minimizing eye movements and memory load, you make comparisons more direct, efficient, and accurate.

## When it applies

-   When the task is to find the single data point with the **maximum change** between two datasets.
-   When using **static media** (print, images) or when animation is otherwise not feasible.
-   This technique is highly effective for **slope charts** and **line charts**, and is the best static option for **bar charts**.

## Exceptions

-   **Chart Clutter:** Overlaying can lead to a cluttered and illegible chart if the two series frequently cross over each other (occlusion) or if you are comparing more than two or three series.
-   **For judging correlation:** Mirrored small multiples were found to be more effective for judging correlation in bar charts. The benefit of overlaying is specific to discrete value comparison.
-   **When animation is possible:** For bar charts and donut charts, animation was found to be even more effective than overlaying for the max delta task.

## Trade-offs

-   **Potential Occlusion:** One data series can obscure another, especially in dense charts. This can be mitigated with transparency or by using different mark types (e.g., line vs. dots).
-   **Color Dependency:** This layout relies on color or another visual channel to distinguish the two series, making it vulnerable to issues of color vision deficiency if colors are not chosen carefully.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Chart Sprawl:** You are using two separate charts (side-by-side or stacked) where one overlaid chart would suffice, forcing users to do extra work.
-   **"Chart Spaghetti":** An overlaid line chart is so cluttered with crossing lines that it's impossible to follow any single series.
-   **Memory Strain:** Viewers complain they "lose their place" when looking back and forth between two separate charts to compare them.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Use Guiding Lines.** If you must keep charts separate, add subtle guiding lines or hover effects that connect corresponding points across the charts to aid comparison.

-   **Moderate Redesign: Combine into an Overlaid Chart.** Redesign your visualization to combine the two separate charts into one. Use distinct, perceptually separated colors (e.g., blue and orange) to represent the two series. For bar charts, you can place the bars for each category next to each other (grouped bar chart) or overlay them with transparency. For line charts, simply draw both lines on the same axes.

-   **Comprehensive Approach: Interactive Toggling.** In an interactive context, start with separate charts (small multiples) to give an overview of each, but allow the user to click a button to merge them into a temporary overlaid view for focused comparison.
