## Guidance

Be cautious using line charts for tasks that require users to accurately remember and recall the absolute values of individual data points. In memory-based tasks, bar charts or dot plots are significantly more effective for this purpose.

## Why

While both line charts and bar charts use the visual channel of position, they are perceived differently. A line chart connects points, encouraging the perception of a continuous shape, trend, or slopes between points. In memory-based tasks, users tend to encode this overall shape rather than the absolute vertical position of each individual point. This leads to high error and systematic bias (e.g., underestimation) when they try to reproduce the absolute values. In contrast, discrete marks like bars or dots encourage perception of individual magnitudes.

## When it applies

- The primary task for the user is to look at the chart, remember the value of one or more specific data points, and then use that information for comparison or decision-making.
- When accuracy of absolute magnitudes is more important than the perception of overall trend or rate of change.
- When designing static charts or dashboards where users cannot hover to see exact values.

## Exceptions

- When the primary task is to communicate the overall trend, shape, or pattern of change over a continuous variable (like time). Line charts are exceptionally effective for this.
- In highly interactive charts where tooltips provide precise values on demand, thus offloading the memory requirement.
- When comparing the slopes or rates of change between multiple series, for which line charts are ideal.

## Trade-offs

- **Trend vs. Magnitude:** Line charts excel at showing trends but are poor for recalling discrete magnitudes. Bar charts excel at discrete magnitudes but can be less effective for showing the continuous shape of a trend. The choice depends on which aspect of the data is more important to communicate.
- **Connectedness vs. Discreteness:** The connecting line in a line chart implies a relationship between adjacent points, which is useful for continuous data but misleading for discrete categories.

## Signs of Trouble

- **"Shape" Recall, "Value" Error:** Users can accurately describe the overall pattern ("it went up, then down sharply") but are highly inaccurate when asked to estimate the value at the peak or trough.
- **Systematic Underestimation:** Users consistently recall the values of points on a line chart as being lower than they actually were.
- **Comparison Failures:** Users struggle to accurately compare the value of a point at the beginning of a line chart with a point at the end, because they are primarily encoding the local slopes, not the absolute positions.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Point Markers.** Add visible dots or markers to each data point on the line chart. This helps to visually emphasize the individual points and slightly improves the perception of their discrete values.

- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Dot Plot or Bar Chart.** If recalling absolute values is the primary goal, the most effective change is to switch the chart type. Use a bar chart (for categorical data) or a dot plot (for either categorical or continuous data) to encode values using position on a common baseline without a connecting line.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Provide Both Views.** If both trend and absolute magnitude are important, consider providing both a line chart and a bar chart. This can be done via small multiples or an interactive control that allows the user to toggle between the two views, letting them use the best chart for their specific task at any given moment.
