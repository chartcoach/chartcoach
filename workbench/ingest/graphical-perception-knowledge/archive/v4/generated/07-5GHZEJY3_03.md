## Guidance

Use color hue (from a qualitative palette) or shape as primary visual channels for encoding nominal (unordered categorical) data.

## Why

Color hue and shape are effective because they are "identity" channels; they help distinguish items without imposing a perceptual order. This aligns perfectly with the nature of nominal data, where categories are distinct but have no inherent ranking. According to Mackinlay's (1986) model, color hue is one of the most effective retinal (non-positional) encodings for nominal data.

## When it applies

- When distinguishing categories in a chart, such as different groups in a scatter plot or multiple lines in a line chart.
- When the data variable is purely categorical (e.g., Country, Product Type, Gender).

## Exceptions

- **High Cardinality:** If you have too many categories (typically > 7-10), it becomes difficult to find enough distinct hues or shapes that are easily discriminable. In such cases, other solutions like faceting (small multiples) or grouping "other" categories may be necessary.
- **Small Marks:** Shape can become ineffective if the marks on the chart (e.g., points in a scatter plot) are very small or overlap, as the shapes become difficult to identify. In this case, color hue is a better choice.
- **Accessibility:** Color choice must be managed carefully to be accessible to users with color vision deficiencies. Shape is a good alternative or supplement to color for this reason.

## Trade-offs

- **Color:** Requires careful palette selection to ensure colors are discriminable, accessible, and do not have unintended semantic meanings (e.g., red for "bad").
- **Shape:** Can add visual complexity and may be less effective than color for quick, pre-attentive grouping of marks.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Indistinguishable Categories:** Viewers cannot easily tell the difference between categories because the colors are too similar or the shapes are not distinct.
- **Accessibility Failures:** A colorblindness simulator reveals that two or more categories become visually identical.
- **Color Overload:** So many colors are used that the chart becomes a "fruit salad" of visual noise, and the legend is impossible to memorize.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Use a Color Palette Tool.** Use a tool like ColorBrewer or Viz Palette to generate a qualitative, colorblind-safe palette for your chosen number of categories.
- **Moderate Redesign: Combine Color and Shape.** For improved accessibility and clarity, use both color and shape to encode categories. This provides a redundant signal that helps all users.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Small Multiples.** If you have too many categories to encode with color or shape, break the chart into a series of smaller charts (small multiples or facets), one for each category. This uses the powerful "position" channel to separate the groups.
