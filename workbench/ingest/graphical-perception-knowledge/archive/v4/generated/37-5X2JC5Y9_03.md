## Guidance

When the task is to judge and compare the average value of groups in a scatterplot, do not assume that showing more data points will hurt performance. Within reasonable bounds, viewers can effectively process larger numbers of points for this task.

## Why

The visual system's ability to perform aggregate judgments, such as finding the "center of gravity" of a cloud of points, is highly efficient and largely independent of the number of items in the set. Performance does not degrade with more points because the brain is not serially counting or averaging each point; it is performing a parallel, holistic assessment of the group's properties. More points can even provide a more stable and reliable visual estimate of the group's center.

## When it applies

- In scatterplots where users are expected to visually judge and compare summary statistics (like the mean or distribution) of different groups.
- When deciding whether to show a full dataset or a sample. This guideline suggests that showing the full dataset is perceptually feasible.

## Exceptions

- **Severe Overplotting:** If the data points become so dense that they overlap and form an indistinguishable mass, it becomes impossible to perceive the underlying distribution or density. The guideline applies when points are generally distinct.
- **Technical Performance:** Extremely large datasets (millions of points) can cause slow rendering or make interaction sluggish. In these cases, limiting points through aggregation or sampling is a technical necessity.

## Trade-offs

- **Completeness vs. Clarity:** Showing all data provides a complete and honest picture of the distribution but can lead to overplotting.
- **Detail vs. Performance:** Rendering every single point in a massive dataset may be perceptually fine but technically impractical.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Overplotting:** Points are so dense they obscure each other, forming solid blobs. It's impossible to discern the shape of the distribution or areas of high density.
- **Performance Lag:** The chart is slow to render, pan, or zoom, leading to a frustrating user experience.
- **Arbitrary Sampling:** A small, arbitrary sample of the data is shown, which may not be representative of the full dataset, potentially misleading the viewer.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Adjust Opacity.** If you have moderate overplotting, reduce the opacity of the points (e.g., set alpha to 0.2). This allows areas of high density to appear darker, creating a makeshift heatmap effect that reveals the underlying distribution.

- **Moderate Approach: Change Mark Type.** If overplotting is severe, switch from solid filled circles to smaller points or open outlines. This allows more points to be visible in dense areas.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Density Visualizations.** For very large datasets where overplotting is unavoidable, switch from plotting individual points to visualizing density. Use a 2D histogram (heatmap), a contour plot, or bin the data (hexbinning) to show the number of points in each region of the chart.
