## Guidance

Do not assume a single, universal ranking of visual channels (e.g., position is always better than length, which is always better than angle) is applicable to all visualization tasks. The effectiveness of a visual channel is highly dependent on the user's task, the number of data points, and the specific values being shown.

## Why

Classic rankings of visual channels were derived from simple tasks, like judging the ratio between two marks. Research shows these rankings do not hold for more complex, memory-intensive tasks, such as recalling and reproducing a series of values. For these tasks, other factors like the number of items to remember (cognitive load) and their distribution have a much larger impact on performance than the choice of visual channel. For example, `angle` can be less biased than `position (line)` in a memory task, reversing the traditional ranking.

## When it applies

- When the user's task involves more than just a simple, immediate comparison of two values.
- When the task requires memory, such as comparing a chart to one seen previously or recalling values after looking away.
- When displaying more than 2-4 data points, as the influence of working memory limitations becomes dominant.
- When deciding between chart types and optimizing for a specific perceptual metric (e.g., minimizing bias vs. maximizing precision).

## Exceptions

- For simple, immediate perceptual tasks, such as judging which of two adjacent bars in a bar chart is taller, the classic rankings (position > length) are generally reliable and serve as a strong starting point.
- When a specific organizational or branding style guide mandates a certain chart type, though the perceptual limitations should still be acknowledged.

## Trade-offs

- **Clarity vs. Simplicity:** Relying on a simple universal ranking is easy but can lead to suboptimal or misleading charts. Considering the specific context requires more effort but results in more effective visualizations.
- **Optimizing for Bias vs. Precision:** The "best" channel may differ depending on what you optimize for. For example, bubble charts (`area`) can be surprisingly precise (consistent responses) but also highly biased (consistently wrong). Bar charts (`position`) may be less biased but also less precise under certain conditions.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Dogmatic Design:** A design process that defaults to a specific chart type (e.g., "always use a bar chart because position is best") without considering the task or data complexity.
- **Mismatched Performance:** Users can easily perform simple judgments (e.g., "which is biggest?") but struggle to recall values, summarize distributions, or compare multiple points from memory.
- **Surprising Errors:** Observing that users make systematic errors on a chart type that is theoretically "good" (e.g., consistently underestimating values in a line chart).

## How to Improve

- **Quick Approach: Acknowledge the Task.** Before choosing a chart, clearly define the primary question you want the user to answer. If it involves memory or comparing more than two values, be skeptical of simple ranking heuristics.

- **Moderate Approach: Choose Based on Error Tolerance.** Decide if your application is more sensitive to bias (systematic error, e.g., always underestimating) or imprecision (random error). The research shows that `angle` and `length` can be less biased, while `area` can be more precise. Choose the channel that aligns with your tolerance for different error types.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Prototype and Test.** For critical applications, create versions of the visualization using different visual channels and test them with representative users on the specific task they need to perform. This is the most reliable way to determine the optimal encoding for your unique context.
