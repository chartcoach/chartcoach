## Guidance

When designing a colormap for a dark background that appears to fade into the background (simulating opacity), map the largest quantities to the lightest, highest-contrast color, not the darkest one.

## Why

On dark backgrounds, two perceptual biases conflict: the general "dark-is-more" bias and a more powerful "opaque-is-more" bias. Lighter colors have higher contrast against a dark background, making them appear more "opaque" or solid. Viewers will intuitively map these high-contrast colors to larger quantities. Encoding "more" as a dark color that blends in with the background is perceptually confusing and requires more effort to interpret.

## When it applies

- You are designing a visualization (e.g., heatmap) that will be displayed on a **dark or black background**.
- The colormap is a gradient that interpolates between a saturated color and the dark background color (e.g., a gradient from bright blue to black on a black background), which creates the illusion of varying opacity.

## Exceptions

- This guidance is less relevant if the colormap does not appear to vary in opacity. For example, if you use a multi-hue colormap (like 'Hot' or 'Viridis') that is designed to be robust against background changes, the standard "dark-is-more" rule may still be effective, as the "opaque-is-more" bias is not triggered.
- If you are required by a strict style guide to use a "dark-is-more" encoding regardless of background. Be aware that this will likely decrease usability on dark themes.

## Trade-offs

- This "light-is-more" approach on dark backgrounds directly contradicts the "dark-is-more" rule that works best on light backgrounds. This means a single colormap may not be intuitively understood if it's used across both light and dark themes without modification.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Disappearing Data:** The areas with the highest values on your chart seem to fade away or are hard to see against the dark background.
- **Perceptual Inversion:** The chart "feels" backwards, as if the low values are being highlighted and the high values are being de-emphasized.
- **Low-Contrast Hotspots:** The most "important" part of the heatmap (the highest value) has very low visual salience.
- **The Squint Test:** When you squint at the chart, the brightest spots (which should represent "more") actually correspond to low or mid-range values.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Reverse the Scale for Dark Mode.** If you have a theme-switcher, programmatically reverse your colormap when the user selects the dark theme. This ensures "more" is always mapped to the highest-contrast color for the current background.

- **Moderate Approach: Use a "Value-by-Alpha" Inspired Scale.** Re-design your colormap for dark backgrounds to be a linear interpolation from the background color (e.g., black) to a single, bright, saturated color (e.g., yellow). This intentionally leverages the "opaque-is-more" bias.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Choose a Robust, Theme-Agnostic Colormap.** Instead of a simple gradient that fades to the background, use a perceptually-uniform, multi-hue colormap (like Viridis, Plasma, or Magma) that is designed to have monotonic lightness and work on both light and dark backgrounds. With these, you can often consistently use the "dark-is-more" rule, as they don't strongly trigger the "opaque-is-more" bias.
