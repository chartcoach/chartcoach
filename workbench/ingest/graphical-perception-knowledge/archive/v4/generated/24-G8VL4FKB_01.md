## Guidance

Avoid stacked bar charts and stacked area charts when the primary task is to compare the size of individual components across different groups. Instead, use a chart type that places all components on a common baseline, such as a grouped bar chart, a dot plot, or small multiples.

## Why

According to Cleveland & McGill's hierarchy of perceptual tasks, judging position along a common scale is far more accurate than judging length without one. In a stacked bar chart, only the bottom-most series rests on the common baseline (y=0). All other segments float on top of the ones below them, requiring viewers to perform difficult and error-prone length judgments to compare their sizes across bars. This leads to inaccurate interpretations and can obscure important trends in the inner components.

## When it applies

- When a key user task is to compare the values of a *specific* component across multiple groups (e.g., "Did revenue from 'Product A' grow more than from 'Product B' between 2022 and 2023?").
- When you need to accurately rank the size of components within different groups.
- When visualizing trends of individual components over time in a stacked area chart. The fluctuating baseline makes it nearly impossible to judge the true trend of the upper layers.

## Exceptions

- When the primary task is to compare the **total** value of the stacks and see the approximate part-to-whole composition *within* each stack.
- If there are only two components in each stack. In this case, the top component's value can be inferred by its distance from the top of the bar, which is a slightly easier (though still not ideal) task.

## Trade-offs

- **Space:** Grouped bar charts and small multiples require more horizontal or total space than a stacked bar chart.
- **Total Comparison:** Grouped charts make it harder to see and compare the total sum of the components in each group, a task at which stacked bar charts excel.
- **Cognitive Load:** For categories with many components, a grouped bar chart can become visually cluttered.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Floating Segments:** You find it difficult to tell if a middle or top segment in one bar is larger than the corresponding segment in another bar.
- **Part-to-Part Questions:** Viewers are asking questions about the relative size or trend of inner components (e.g., "Is the blue part getting bigger or smaller?"), not about the totals.
- **Hidden Trends:** The trend of an individual component is obscured because the baseline below it is fluctuating, masking its true change over time.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Reorder the Stacks.** If you must use a stacked chart, place the component that is most important for comparison at the bottom, so it rests on the common baseline.

- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Grouped Bar Chart.** Convert the stacked bar chart to a grouped bar chart. This gives every single bar a common baseline, making all part-to-part comparisons accurate and easy. This directly addresses the perceptual problem.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Small Multiples.** Create a separate, small chart (e.g., a simple bar or line chart) for each component. This approach is especially effective for time-series data, as it gives each component its own clear chart to reveal its individual trend, free from any distortion from other series.
