## Guidance

To make an outlier or an important data point visually salient (i.e., "pop out"), ensure its visual features (e.g., color, shape, size) are unique across the entire display, not just different from its immediate neighbors.

## Why

The human visual system automatically computes summary statistics (or "ensembles") of features across a whole scene. An item is perceived as an outlier when it deviates significantly from these global, scene-wide statistics. An object that is only locally different but globally common will not create a strong pop-out effect. Therefore, global uniqueness is a more powerful driver of pre-attentive outlier detection than local contrast.

## When it applies

- You want to draw the user's attention to a specific data point or a small group of anomalous points.
- The task is anomaly detection, highlighting, or finding specific items in a dense visualization.
- Designing scatterplots, symbol maps, or any chart with a large number of discrete marks.

## Exceptions

- **When all items are unique:** If every item in a visualization is already unique (e.g., a photograph), this principle doesn't apply in the same way. Salience is then driven by other factors like semantic meaning or low-level image features.
- **When local comparison is the task:** If the task is explicitly to compare an item to its immediate neighbors (e.g., "Is this house bigger than the ones next to it?"), local contrast is what matters most.

## Trade-offs

- **Visual Dominance:** Making an item globally unique gives it strong visual weight, which can be distracting if it is not the primary point of interest. This technique should be used sparingly and only for genuinely important data.
- **Aesthetic Disruption:** A single, starkly different element can sometimes disrupt the aesthetic harmony of a visualization. This is often a necessary price for drawing attention effectively.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Where's Waldo?" Effect:** The supposedly important outlier is lost in the crowd, and users have to actively search for it.
- **Local Camouflage:** The outlier is visually distinct from its immediate neighbors, but it blends in with other items elsewhere in the display.
- **False Alarms:** Items that are not actually outliers are popping out more than the true outliers because they happen to have higher local contrast.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Use a Unique Color.** Change the color of the outlier to a hue that is not used anywhere else in the visualization. Make it bright and saturated to maximize its salience.

- **Moderate Redesign: Use Redundant, Unique Encodings.** Make the outlier unique across multiple visual channels simultaneously. For example, if the other points are small blue circles, make the outlier a large red star. This combination of unique color, size, and shape makes it maximally salient.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Design a "Quiet" Background.** To make outliers stand out, make the non-outlier items as homogeneous as possible. Use a muted, single color for all background points and uniform size/shape. This lowers the global variance of the "distractors," making any deviation from that norm (the outlier) much more conspicuous.
