## Guidance

Sort the bars in a bar chart by their value (ascending or descending) when a primary goal is for the viewer to understand the rank or relative position of items.

## Why

Sorting bars by value provides a strong, monotonic visual pattern that significantly improves the accuracy and speed of rank estimation tasks. Unsorted charts (e.g., ordered alphabetically) force the viewer to scan and mentally compare all bars, a process that is slow, cognitively demanding, and prone to large errors. Research shows that viewers of unsorted charts tend to underestimate the rank of high-value items and overestimate the rank of low-value items.

## When it applies

- When the primary task is to judge where an item falls within the overall distribution (e.g., "Is this product in the top 10% of sales?").
- When the categorical order of the bars is arbitrary or not inherently meaningful (e.g., a list of countries, products, or people).

## Exceptions

- If the categories have a natural, intrinsic order that is more important than the value, that order should be preserved. Examples include time-based categories (days of the week, months), age groups, or Likert scale responses (e.g., "Strongly Disagree" to "Strongly Agree").

## Trade-offs

- Sorting by value makes it more difficult to look up a specific category by name. Finding "Switzerland" is easy in an alphabetized list but can be difficult in a list sorted by population.
- If lookup is a critical co-primary task, an interactive chart with a sort control or a searchable table may be a better solution.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Alphabetical Order for Performance Data:** The bars are sorted alphabetically, but the goal is to understand performance, rank, or magnitude.
- **Chaotic Appearance:** The bar heights jump up and down randomly, forcing viewers to scan the entire chart to find the highest, lowest, or median values.
- **Difficult Comparisons:** Viewers struggle to answer questions like "Which items are in the top quartile?" or "How does this item's performance compare to the average?".

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** Re-sort the chart either ascending or descending by the bar's value. This is the single most effective change to support rank estimation.

- **Moderate approach:** If item lookup is also important, provide an interactive feature (e.g., a button or dropdown) that allows the user to toggle between a value-based sort and an alphabetical sort.

- **Comprehensive approach:** For complex scenarios requiring both easy lookup and rank comparison, consider alternatives to a static bar chart, such as a searchable and sortable table that includes data bars (spark-bars) for each row.

## Guidance

Be aware that the height of bars immediately adjacent to a target bar can introduce a small perceptual bias. A bar surrounded by taller neighbors may be perceived as slightly shorter or lower in rank than it actually is.

## Why

This phenomenon, known as the "neighborhood effect," is a form of visual illusion called "length contrast." The human brain's perceptual system does not judge visual elements in isolation; the immediate visual context influences perception. While research confirms this effect exists in bar charts, its impact is small compared to other biases related to the overall data distribution.

## When it applies

- This subtle bias is present in any bar chart, but is most variable in unsorted charts where bars of very different heights can be placed next to each other.
- It becomes a relevant (though minor) consideration in high-stakes visualizations where extreme precision in judgment is required.

## Exceptions

- For the vast majority of general-purpose visualizations, this effect is small enough to be considered negligible. The paper's authors conclude that "designers do not need to worry too much about neighborhood effects" because they are overshadowed by much larger data-inherent effects.

## Trade-offs

- Actively trying to mitigate this small effect could add unnecessary complexity. For example, increasing the spacing between all bars might slightly reduce the illusion but would also decrease data density and make the chart take up more space. Prioritizing larger issues like overall sorting is more impactful.

## Signs of Trouble

- **High-Stakes Precision:** The chart is used for a critical decision where a perceptual error of 1-2% in rank or value could lead to a wrong conclusion.
- **Surprising Adjacency:** In an unsorted chart, an important bar happens to fall between the two tallest bars, creating a context that maximizes the potential for underestimation.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** In most cases, no action is needed. Simply be aware that this minor bias exists. Prioritize addressing more significant issues like sorting the chart by value.

- **Moderate approach:** If the chart must remain unsorted, consider if grouping the bars by a secondary categorical variable could create more visually homogeneous "neighborhoods," thereby reducing the variability of the effect.

- **Comprehensive approach:** The most effective way to manage neighborhood context is to sort the bar chart by value. This creates a smooth and predictable context for every bar, minimizing the random influence of adjacent bar heights.

## Guidance

When presenting bar charts for rank estimation, add explicit visual cues like reference lines (e.g., for the mean or median) or annotations to help viewers overcome strong cognitive biases caused by the data's distribution.

## Why

Research shows that viewers' judgment of a bar's rank is strongly and systematically biased by the overall shape of the data. Viewers tend to exhibit a "regression to the mean," underestimating the rank of high-value items and overestimating the rank of low-value items. This effect is powerful, especially in skewed datasets, and can lead to significant misinterpretation. Providing explicit anchors gives viewers a stable reference point, reducing their reliance on flawed mental heuristics.

## When it applies

- In bar charts used for rank or distribution tasks.
- When the chart contains a moderate-to-high number of bars (e.g., >20), making it difficult for viewers to scan and count.
- When the underlying data distribution is skewed (has a long tail).

## Exceptions

- For simple lookup tasks where the viewer only needs to find the value of a specific, known bar, these additional cues may not be necessary.
- In charts with very few bars (e.g., <10), viewers can often make accurate judgments without explicit anchors.

## Trade-offs

- Adding reference lines, shading, or annotations increases the visual complexity of the chart, adding more ink to the page. However, this trade-off is often worthwhile, as the gain in interpretive accuracy outweighs the cost of added visual elements.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Skewed Data:** The data distribution has a long tail, with many low-value items and a few high-value items (or vice-versa).
- **High Cardinality:** The chart is dense with many bars, making it nearly impossible to accurately place a single bar within the overall distribution without assistance.
- **Misjudged Extremes:** Viewers consistently misjudge the rank of top and bottom performers, perceiving them as "less extreme" or "closer to the middle" than they actually are.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** Add a single reference line to mark a key statistical measure. The **median** is often a robust choice as it marks the 50th percentile, providing a clear halfway point for ranking. The **mean** can also be useful but is sensitive to skewed data.

- **Moderate approach:** Enhance the chart with quartile information. This can be done by adding reference lines for the 25th, 50th, and 75th percentiles, or by using shaded regions to visually delineate the different quartiles.

- **Comprehensive approach:** For critical analysis, supplement the bar chart with an explicit statistical plot, such as a box plot or violin plot, placed alongside it. This directly visualizes the data's distribution, skewness, and key statistical markers, providing a rich context for interpreting the individual bars. Additionally, use annotations to call out specific items of interest (e.g., "Top 10%").
