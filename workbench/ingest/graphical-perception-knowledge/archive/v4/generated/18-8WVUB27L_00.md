## Guidance

For tasks requiring the comparison or judgment of gradient magnitudes (e.g., "steepness" or "rate of change") in scalar fields like heatmaps, use colormaps with some hue discretization. Diverging (e.g., cool-warm) or spectral (e.g., rainbow) schemes can make it easier for viewers to perceive subtle structural differences.

## Why

Discontinuities in the color ramp, such as the bands in a rainbow or cool-warm colormap, act as perceptual proxies. Viewers can quickly estimate the size and frequency of these color patches to judge the overall "activity" or steepness of the field. A perfectly smooth, continuous colormap (like viridis) lacks these salient features, making this specific task more difficult.

## When it applies

- When the primary task is to compare the overall "steepness," "volatility," or average rate of change between two or more scalar fields.
- When visualizing scientific data where identifying regions of high activity (e.g., turbulence, geological stress) is more important than reading precise values at a point.

## Exceptions

- **Prioritizing Value Lookups:** If the primary task is for users to accurately read specific data values at points on the map, a perceptually uniform colormap (like viridis) is generally superior.
- **Accessibility is Paramount:** Rainbow colormaps are not colorblind-safe and can introduce interpretation issues. If your audience includes color-vision deficient users or if you cannot risk misinterpretation, avoid rainbow schemes. A diverging scheme is a safer compromise.

## Trade-offs

- **Clarity vs. Artifacts:** You are intentionally using visual "artifacts" (color bands) to aid one specific task (gradient perception) at the potential cost of introducing misleading boundaries for other tasks (like identifying continuous regions).
- **Task Specificity:** A colormap optimized for gradient perception may perform poorly for other common tasks like value estimation or identifying specific data ranges.

## Signs of Trouble

- **"Everything looks flat":** Users report difficulty seeing differences in steepness or activity between two maps, even when you know a numerical difference exists.
- **Over-reliance on tooltips:** Viewers must hover over many points to understand the structure of the data field, as the visual representation is not providing enough information about its form.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach: Switch to a Diverging Scheme.** Change from a monotonic-luminance colormap (like viridis or a single-hue sequential scheme) to a principled diverging colormap (like cool-warm). This introduces a central discontinuity that can help anchor perception without the full chaos of a rainbow.
- **Moderate approach: Use a Binned (Quantized) Colormap.** Instead of a continuous gradient, explicitly bin the data into 5-7 distinct, ordered color steps. This forces discretization, making it easy to see contours and judge their density, which is a proxy for gradient.
- **Comprehensive approach: Provide Multiple Views.** If tasks are mixed, don't force one colormap to do everything. Provide the primary view with a perceptually uniform map (like viridis) for general use. Offer a user-controlled option to switch to a different scheme for analyzing gradients, or provide a separate, explicit gradient-field visualization.
