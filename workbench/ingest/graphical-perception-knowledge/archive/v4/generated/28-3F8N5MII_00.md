## Guidance

For sequential data represented by a colormap (like in a heatmap or choropleth map), map larger quantities to darker colors and smaller quantities to lighter colors.

## Why

People have a strong, pre-existing cognitive bias to associate darker colors with more "stuff" or larger quantities (a "dark-is-more" bias). Aligning your visual encoding with this intuition reduces cognitive load and allows viewers to interpret the visualization faster and more accurately. Violating this expectation forces users to constantly consult the legend and can lead to misinterpretation.

## When it applies

- When using a sequential colormap to represent quantitative data that progresses from low to high.
- This rule is especially strong and reliable when your visualization has a **light or white background**.
- Applies to chart types like heatmaps, choropleth maps, and other surfaces filled with color to represent magnitude.

## Exceptions

- **On dark backgrounds:** The "dark-is-more" bias can weaken or even reverse if the colormap appears to vary in opacity. On a dark background, lighter colors have higher contrast and can seem more "opaque" or prominent. In these cases, a "light-is-more" encoding might be more effective.
- **Diverging data:** This guideline does not apply to diverging data, which has a meaningful midpoint (like zero). Diverging data should use a diverging colormap with two distinct hues that meet at a neutral, light color.

## Trade-offs

- Following this guideline may conflict with some brand color palettes that use light colors as the primary "accent" color.
- It prioritizes perceptual speed and intuitiveness over other aesthetic or brand considerations.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Delayed Interpretation:** Viewers pause for a long time or have to carefully study the legend to understand what the colors mean.
- **Reversed Readings:** Users mistakenly interpret high-value areas as low-value, or vice-versa.
- **User Feedback:** Viewers comment that the chart feels "backwards" or "counter-intuitive."
- **Inconsistent Performance:** When testing, users are significantly slower or make more errors with a "light-is-more" encoding compared to a "dark-is-more" one on a light background.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Invert the Colormap.** If your current map uses a "light-is-more" scheme, simply reverse the color scale so that darker colors correspond to higher values. Most visualization tools have a "reverse" or "invert" option for the color scale.

- **Moderate Approach: Choose a Standard Sequential Palette.** Switch to a well-tested, perceptually uniform sequential colormap (like Viridis, Plasma, or the single-hue schemes from ColorBrewer). These are explicitly designed to vary from light to dark to encode quantity.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Test Your Encodings.** If you are creating a visualization for a broad or critical application, run a simple user test. Present two versions of the chart (one "dark-is-more," one "light-is-more") and measure which one people can interpret more quickly and accurately for a simple aggregation task (e.g., "Which region has more?").
