## Guidance

Use colormaps where equal steps in the data correspond to equal steps in the perceived color change. This property is known as perceptual uniformity.

## Why

Non-uniform colormaps distort the perception of your data. They can artificially create or hide features by making some data ranges appear more prominent (with rapid color changes) and others less detailed (with slow color changes). Perceptual uniformity ensures that the visual representation faithfully reflects the underlying structure of the data, preventing misinterpretation.

## When it applies

- When visualizing continuous quantitative or ordinal data.
- When the goal is to accurately represent the magnitude of change or the distribution of values across a range.

## Exceptions

- When the goal is intentionally to segment data into discrete perceptual bins for classification, a non-uniform, binned colormap might be used.
- For purely categorical data with no inherent order, a uniform gradient is not required; instead, a palette of distinct, non-ordered colors should be used.

## Trade-offs

- A strictly uniform colormap might have slightly less "discriminative power" (total number of distinguishable steps) than a non-uniform one that travels a longer but more crooked path through color space. However, the gain in data fidelity and prevention of artifacts almost always outweighs this.

## Signs of Trouble

- **False Contours:** The visualization shows distinct bands or "contours" in areas of smooth data. These are often called "Mach bands" and are a classic sign of non-uniformity (e.g., the bright yellow and cyan bands in a standard rainbow colormap).
- **Uneven Detail:** Some parts of the color scale seem to have much more visual detail and variation than others, even when the underlying data is evenly distributed.
- **The Squint Test:** If you squint at the visualization, do sharp edges or bands of color pop out? A uniform gradient should appear as a smooth, continuous blur.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** Replace your current colormap with a well-known perceptually uniform one. Most modern plotting libraries offer these as defaults or options (e.g., Viridis, Cividis, Plasma, Inferno).
- **Moderate Redesign:** Use a tool like Viz Palette to inspect the lightness (L*) profile of your chosen colormap. A perceptually uniform colormap will typically have a smooth, linear or monotonically increasing/decreasing lightness profile.
- **Comprehensive Redesign:** If designing a custom colormap, create it within a perceptually uniform color space (like CIELAB or CIECAM02-UCS). A straight line in one of these spaces will produce a perceptually uniform colormap according to that space's metric.

## Guidance

For data with a natural sequence (e.g., low to high), use colormaps that progress steadily and consistently from light to dark or from dark to light.

## Why

The human visual system has a strong, pre-attentive ability to perceive order in lightness. A monotonic lightness ramp provides an intuitive sense of direction ("more" vs. "less") without requiring the viewer to memorize a complex color legend. This reduces cognitive load, makes the chart's structure immediately apparent, and ensures the visualization remains interpretable when printed in grayscale or viewed by people with color vision deficiencies.

## When it applies

- When mapping any ordered data (ordinal, quantitative, sequential) to a continuous or sequential colormap.

## Exceptions

- **Diverging Data:** For data with a meaningful midpoint (like zero or an average), use a diverging colormap. A diverging map should have two different monotonic lightness ramps that meet at a neutral, often light, central color (e.g., dark blue -> light gray -> dark red).
- **Cyclical Data:** For data that wraps around (e.g., angles from 0-360°, time of day), a cyclical colormap without a monotonic lightness trend is appropriate, as there is no "start" or "end".

## Trade-offs

- A pure single-hue colormap (e.g., light blue to dark blue) has a perfect monotonic lightness ramp but may have fewer discriminable steps than a multi-hue map. A well-designed multi-hue map (like Viridis) can offer more discriminable steps while still maintaining a monotonic lightness profile.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Grayscale Test Failure:** When you convert the visualization to grayscale, the order is lost, becomes ambiguous, or reverses in some sections.
- **Bright Middle:** The colormap has a band of high lightness in the middle of the data range (e.g., the bright yellow in a standard rainbow/jet colormap), which breaks the perceptual order.
- **Ambiguous Ends:** It's not immediately obvious which end of the color scale represents a higher or lower value without carefully consulting the legend.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: The Grayscale Test.** Convert your chart to grayscale. If it no longer shows a smooth, ordered gradient from dark to light (or vice versa), your colormap lacks a monotonic lightness ramp.
- **Moderate Redesign:** Replace the existing colormap with a sequential colormap known to have a monotonic lightness ramp. Single-hue sequential palettes (e.g., 'Blues', 'Greens') are a safe choice. Perceptually uniform multi-hue palettes (e.g., 'Viridis', 'Cividis') are also excellent.
- **Comprehensive Redesign:** Use a tool that allows you to design or inspect the lightness (L*) profile of your colormap. Ensure the L* value increases or decreases consistently and smoothly across the entire data range.

## Guidance

Do not use the full-spectrum rainbow (often called `jet`) colormap to represent continuous or sequential data.

## Why

Rainbow colormaps are harmful to data interpretation for several critical reasons:
1.  **No Perceptual Order:** They lack an intuitive ordering of values (is yellow "more" or "less" than green?), forcing high cognitive load and frequent legend lookups.
2.  **False Boundaries:** Their non-uniform nature creates sharp, artificial boundaries (especially between yellow and green) that can be mistaken for features in the data where none exist.
3.  **Uneven Salience:** The bright, highly saturated colors (yellow, cyan) draw the viewer's attention disproportionately, creating a misleading emphasis on certain data values regardless of their importance.
4.  **Poor Accessibility:** They are not robust to common forms of color vision deficiency, making them unreadable for a significant portion of the audience.

## When it applies

- When visualizing any data with a natural order (quantitative, ordinal). This includes most scientific visualizations, heatmaps, and choropleth maps.

## Exceptions

- **None for ordered data.** While sometimes defended for historical reasons in certain scientific fields, there is always a better, more perceptually sound alternative. For purely categorical data with no inherent order, a palette of distinct colors is needed, but a continuous rainbow is still not the right choice.

## Trade-offs

- You may be breaking with a historical convention in some scientific domains. The trade-off is choosing clarity, accuracy, and accessibility over tradition.

## Signs of Trouble

- The presence of a `jet`, `rainbow`, or similar full-spectrum colormap is itself the sign of trouble.
- Bright, seemingly random bands of color (red, yellow, green, cyan, blue) appear on the visualization.
- When viewed through a color blindness simulator, large portions of the map become indistinguishable fields of muddy yellow and brown.
- Viewers have difficulty determining the order of values or identifying which areas are high or low without constant reference to the legend.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** In your plotting software, change the default colormap away from `jet` or `rainbow`. This is often a single line of code.
- **Moderate Redesign:**
    - For **sequential data** (e.g., 0 to 100), replace the rainbow map with a perceptually uniform, single- or multi-hue sequential colormap (e.g., Viridis, Cividis, Blues).
    - For **diverging data** (e.g., -50 to +50 with a center at 0), replace it with a perceptually uniform diverging colormap (e.g., coolwarm, Blue-White-Red).
- **Comprehensive Redesign:** Educate your team or community on the perceptual issues with rainbow colormaps. Advocate for changing the default colormaps in shared tools and style guides to perceptually sound alternatives.

## Guidance

When fine-grained detail is important, choose colormaps that maximize the number of just-noticeable differences across the data range. This is known as having high "discriminative power."

## Why

A colormap with high discriminative power allows viewers to perceive more distinct steps or levels within the data. This increases the effective "resolution" of the visualization, making it easier to spot subtle variations, identify specific values, and compare differences between points. Colormaps that travel a longer path through a perceptual color space (e.g., by varying both lightness and hue) generally have higher discriminative power than those that only vary lightness.

## When it applies

- When visualizing continuous data with a wide dynamic range.
- When it is important for the viewer to distinguish between small changes in the data.
- In tasks that require looking up or comparing specific values.

## Exceptions

- For categorical data, the goal is not a continuous gradient but a set of a few, maximally distinct colors.
- When simplicity and intuitive order are more important than resolving fine detail, a simple, single-hue sequential colormap may be preferable even if it has lower discriminative power.

## Trade-offs

- **Uniformity and Order:** Maximizing discriminative power can sometimes conflict with maintaining perceptual uniformity and order. The rainbow colormap is a classic example: it has very high discriminative power but is non-uniform and lacks intuitive order. Modern colormaps like Viridis are designed to balance these properties.
- **Cognitive Load:** A colormap with extremely high variation can feel noisy or overwhelming if the extra detail is not relevant to the task.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Banding:** The visualization appears to be "banded" or "posterized," with large ranges of data mapping to colors that look identical.
- **Lost Detail:** You know there are subtle but important variations in the data, but the visualization shows them as a single, flat-colored region.
- **The Squint Test:** When you squint, large areas of the chart merge into a single color, indicating low local contrast and discriminative power in those regions.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** Switch from a single-hue colormap (e.g., 'Blues') to a perceptually uniform multi-hue colormap (e.g., 'Viridis', 'Plasma'). The variation in hue adds to the perceptual path length, increasing discriminative power.
- **Moderate Redesign:** Use a tool like `colormeasures.org` to compare the "average speed" (a proxy for discriminative power) of different colormaps. Choose one with a higher average speed that still meets your other criteria (like uniformity and order).
- **Comprehensive Redesign:** If designing a custom colormap, aim for a long, smooth path through a perceptual color space like CIELAB. Avoid "flat spots" where the color does not change much over a range of data values.

## Guidance

Choose continuous colormaps that have a smooth path through perceptual color space, avoiding sharp "kinks" or abrupt transitions.

## Why

Abrupt changes or sharp "bends" in a colormap's path can create the illusion of boundaries or contours in the data that do not actually exist. This phenomenon, often related to "Mach bands," is a form of visual artifact that leads to misinterpretation by suggesting the data has a sharp feature where it is actually smooth. A smooth colormap ensures a faithful representation of continuous data.

## When it applies

- When visualizing continuous scalar fields (e.g., heatmaps, scientific data).
- When it is important to avoid introducing visual artifacts that could be mistaken for data features.

## Exceptions

- For binned or categorical data, sharp boundaries between colors are intentional and desirable to distinguish the categories. This guidance applies only to continuous colormaps for continuous data.

## Trade-offs

- Creating a perfectly smooth colormap might involve desaturating colors near the "bends," which could slightly reduce the colormap's overall discriminative power. However, this is generally a worthwhile trade-off to ensure data fidelity.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Visible "Kinks":** You can see a distinct line or edge appearing in a region of what should be a smooth color gradient.
- **Mach Bands:** Illusory bands of lightness appear at the boundaries of different color regions, making the transition seem sharp and uneven.
- **Path Visualization:** When viewing the colormap's path in a 3D color space (like CIELAB), the line has sharp corners instead of being a smooth curve.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** Avoid colormaps created by simply joining highly saturated primary or secondary colors (e.g., a gradient from pure red to pure blue). Instead, choose established, well-designed colormaps like Viridis or Moreland's cool-to-warm.
- **Moderate Redesign:** Use a tool like Viz Palette to inspect the colormap's path in 3D color space. Look for any sharp corners or "kinks" and choose an alternative palette with a smoother trajectory.
- **Comprehensive Redesign:** When designing a custom colormap, use methods that ensure smoothness, such as spline-based interpolation in a perceptual color space rather than simple linear interpolation between key colors. Aim for low curvature in the colormap's path.
