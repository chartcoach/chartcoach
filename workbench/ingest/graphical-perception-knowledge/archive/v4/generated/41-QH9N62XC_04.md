## Guidance

When using a line chart to show the correlation between two variables (and the x-axis does not represent time), sort the data points along the x-axis according to one of the variables. This creates a smoother, more monotonic line that makes it easier to perceive the trend of the second variable.

## Why

In a standard line chart with an arbitrary or alphabetical x-axis order, the lines connecting the points can be jagged and noisy, obscuring the underlying relationship between the two variables. By sorting the data based on one variable, you create a smooth, continuous line for that variable (a straight diagonal line if it's plotted, or just the sorted order). The line for the second variable will then show its trend relative to the first in a much clearer, less noisy way. This reduces the perceptual and cognitive load required to discern the correlation.

## When it applies
- When using a line chart to show a relationship between two quantitative variables.
- When the x-axis does not have a natural, intrinsic order (like time).
- When the user's primary task is to judge the correlation between the two variables.

## Exceptions
- **Time-Series Data:** Never re-order a time-series axis. The chronological sequence is the most critical piece of information.
- **Categorical Data:** If the x-axis represents categories with a conventional order (e.g., "Small, Medium, Large" or days of the week), re-sorting may violate user expectations and cause confusion.

## Trade-offs
- Sorting by a variable imposes an order that might not be intuitive to the viewer if they are used to an alphabetical or other default sort order. The choice of which variable to sort by also influences the final appearance.

## Signs of Trouble
- **Jagged Mess:** The line chart is a noisy, jagged mess of up-and-down movements, making it impossible to see an overall trend or relationship.
- **Order-Dependence:** Changing the sort order of the x-axis dramatically changes the perceived relationship, indicating the visual representation is not stable.
- **No Clear Pattern:** Viewers cannot determine if the relationship between the variables is positive, negative, or non-existent.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Add a Trend Line.** Overlay a statistical trend line (e.g., a linear regression line) on top of the noisy line chart. This explicitly shows the underlying trend that is difficult to perceive.
- **Moderate Redesign: Sort the Data.** Re-order the data points based on their value for one of the variables. For example, if plotting "Height vs. Weight," sort the data by "Height" from smallest to largest before plotting. This will smooth the visual pattern.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Switch to a Scatterplot.** For showing correlation between two quantitative variables, a scatterplot is almost always the superior choice. It directly represents the relationship without being dependent on the sort order of a third, categorical variable or index.
