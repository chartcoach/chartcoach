## Guidance

When encoding ordinal data (e.g., "Low, Medium, High"; "Agree, Neutral, Disagree"), use variations in color saturation or lightness (a sequential color scheme) rather than variations in color hue (a rainbow scheme).

## Why

Color saturation and lightness (e.g., moving from light blue to dark blue) have a natural perceptual order that viewers intuitively understand as representing less to more. This aligns with the inherent order of the data. In contrast, a full spectrum of hues (like a rainbow) does not have a universally agreed-upon perceptual order, making it a poor choice for representing ranked data. Mackinlay's effectiveness ranking for ordinal data places saturation above hue for this reason.

## When it applies

- When encoding data with a clear, non-numeric order.
- When creating heatmaps or choropleth maps where cells or regions are colored according to an ordered-categorical or binned-quantitative scale.

## Exceptions

- **Diverging Data:** If your ordinal data has a meaningful midpoint (e.g., "Negative, Neutral, Positive"), a diverging palette (which uses two hues that diverge from a neutral color) is more appropriate than a sequential one.
- **Strong Semantic Associations:** If specific hues have a powerful, learned meaning for the categories (e.g., red for "Hot", blue for "Cold"), using hue may be more intuitive, even if it's not perceptually linear.

## Trade-offs

- It can be difficult to distinguish many different steps (typically more than 5-7) in a single-hue sequential scheme.
- A purely saturation-based scale may have accessibility issues if the steps do not also have sufficient lightness contrast.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Rainbow Palette:** A rainbow (or spectral) color palette is used to encode ordered data. Viewers cannot reliably say whether yellow is "more" or "less" than green.
- **Ambiguous Order:** The visualization uses a collection of distinct hues (e.g., blue, purple, orange), and the order of the categories is not immediately obvious from the colors alone.
- **Low Discriminability:** The steps in your sequential color ramp are too close together, making it hard to tell a "Low" value from a "Medium-Low" value.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Switch to Grayscale.** A simple grayscale ramp is a robust, universally understood sequential scheme.
- **Moderate Redesign: Use a Sequential Color Scheme.** Replace the multi-hue palette with a single-hue sequential palette (e.g., from a tool like ColorBrewer). This ensures the colors have a clear perceptual order.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use a Perceptually Uniform Palette.** For the highest accuracy, use a color space like CIELAB or HCL to generate your color ramp. This ensures that a given step in the data corresponds to an equally-sized perceptual step in color, preventing distortion. Tools like `d3-scale-chromatic` can generate these palettes.
