## Guidance

When visualizing elapsed time on a 2D path or trajectory, encode time using segment length, created by placing ticks at equal time intervals along the path.

## Why

This method leads to the most accurate perception of time compared to other common encodings like color value or size (stroke width). Experiments show that segment length (`TLENGTH`) clearly resulted in the lowest errors for time-estimation tasks on both straight and curved paths. While it may require slightly more time to decode than other methods, its superior accuracy makes it the most reliable choice.

## When it applies

- When visualizing movement or trajectory data where both the path and the passage of time are important (e.g., GPS tracks, movement analytics, user navigation paths).
- When the primary task is to accurately look up or compare the time elapsed at various points along the trajectory.

## Exceptions

- If the context of the visualization already contains conflicting linear marks (e.g., latitude/longitude lines on a map, grid lines on a chart) that would make the time ticks difficult to distinguish.
- If the path is extremely cluttered or crosses over itself many times, which may make the ticks and segments ambiguous.

## Trade-offs

- **Cognitive Load:** Inferring time and speed requires some cognitive effort—viewers must assess the frequency and spacing of ticks, which can be slower than perceiving a direct encoding like color.
- **Visual Clutter:** Adding ticks introduces visual elements that can clutter the display, especially with multiple or complex trajectories.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Indecipherable Ticks:** The ticks are too dense on slow-moving parts of the trajectory, creating a solid black line and obscuring the path.
- **Conflicting Lines:** The time ticks are visually confused with background grid lines or other chart markings.
- **Ambiguous Direction:** On a complex path, it's unclear in which direction time flows, making the sequence of ticks difficult to follow.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Adjust Tick Density.** If ticks are too cluttered, reduce their frequency (e.g., one tick every 5 seconds instead of every 1 second). This reduces clutter but also reduces the granularity of the time information.
- **Moderate Redesign: Add a Directional Cue.** If the path direction is ambiguous, add an arrowhead or use a subtle gradient to indicate the flow of time, complementing the segment length encoding.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use a Combined Encoding.** Consider combining segment length for time with color value for speed (`SVALUE TLENGTH`). Research shows this combination performs well for both time and speed perception tasks, although the added color may slightly interfere with speed perception compared to using segment length alone.

## Guidance

Avoid using a color gradient (e.g., changing brightness or hue) to represent the continuous passage of time along a 2D trajectory.

## Why

Humans are not adept at accurately perceiving and discriminating small variations in color value or hue. When time is encoded as a gradient, it becomes very difficult for viewers to map a specific shade back to a precise moment in time, leading to significantly larger errors in judgment compared to other encodings like segment length or even size (stroke width). Although it is a common technique, experiments show it is perceptually ineffective for accurate lookup tasks.

## When it applies

- When visualizing movement or trajectory data on a 2D plane (e.g., a map or an abstract plot).
- When a key task for the viewer is to accurately estimate the time elapsed at a specific point on the path (e.g., "At what time did the vehicle reach this location?").

## Exceptions

- **General Overview Only:** If the goal is only to provide a general, non-precise visual sense of direction or flow (e.g., "the path generally went from light to dark"), and accuracy is not important, color may be acceptable. However, it should not be relied upon for any analytical task.
- **Categorical Time:** If time is broken into a few, very distinct categorical chunks (e.g., "Morning," "Afternoon," "Night") and represented with highly discriminable colors, this can be effective. This guideline applies specifically to encoding continuous time with a smooth gradient.

## Trade-offs

- **Aesthetics vs. Accuracy:** A color gradient might look visually appealing and clean, but this aesthetic quality comes at the direct cost of perceptual accuracy. This guideline prioritizes accuracy over a clean look.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Is it Lighter or Darker?" Problem:** Viewers are unable to confidently determine if one point on the trajectory is "earlier" or "later" than another if their colors are similar.
- **Gradient Ambiguity:** A single continuous color gradient (e.g., light blue to dark blue) is used. Viewers struggle to map a specific shade to a percentage of time elapsed (e.g., is this shade 60% or 70% of the way through?).
- **Fast Guessing:** Users give up on accurately decoding the color and just make a quick, often inaccurate, guess. This can be observed in user testing as very fast but highly erroneous completion times.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Explicit Labels.** If you must use a color gradient, add explicit time labels or markers at key intervals (e.g., 25%, 50%, 75%) along the path to provide viewers with reference points.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to Size.** Change the encoding from color value to size (stroke width). While not the best method, it is perceptually more accurate for reading time than color value. The path would get progressively thicker over time.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Switch to Segment Length.** The most effective solution is to replace the color gradient with time ticks that create segments of equal time duration (`TLENGTH`). This encoding is demonstrably more accurate for time-perception tasks.

---

## Guidance

To represent the speed of an object along a 2D trajectory, prefer using color value (e.g., a brightness or saturation gradient) over size (stroke width).

## Why

Experiments show that viewers can perceive speed more accurately and quickly when it is encoded with color value (`SVALUE`) compared to stroke width (`SSIZE`). This is particularly true for curved trajectories, where the perception of an object's size can be distorted by the path's curvature. Using a color ramp (e.g., lighter for slow, darker for fast) provides a more robust and direct visual cue for speed.

## When it applies

- When visualizing movement data on a 2D path, such as a GPS track on a map or a path in an abstract space.
- When a key task is for the user to find points of maximum or minimum speed, or to compare the speed at different points along the trajectory.

## Exceptions

- **Color is Reserved:** If color is already being used to encode another, more important variable (e.g., a categorical identifier).
- **Accessibility Constraints:** When designing for users with color vision deficiencies, a color-based encoding may be less effective or inaccessible. In this case, an alternative like segment length (`TLENGTH`) is a better choice.
- **Background Conflict:** If the trajectory is overlaid on a complex, colorful background (like a satellite map), a size-based encoding might be more salient and less prone to being lost in the background noise.

## Trade-offs

- **Consumes Color Channel:** This approach uses the powerful visual channel of color, making it unavailable for other variables like category.
- **Accessibility:** Relies on the user's ability to perceive color differences, which can be a barrier for colorblind users. A single-hue saturation ramp is better than a multi-hue ramp in this regard.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Distorted Width on Curves:** When using size (stroke width) on a curved path, the thickness appears inconsistent or distorted, making it difficult to judge speed accurately.
- **Low Salience:** The color ramp used for speed has poor contrast with the background, making it hard to discern variations.
- **Non-Intuitive Color Ramp:** The color mapping is counter-intuitive (e.g., a bright, high-contrast color for low speed), requiring users to consciously override their initial perception.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Check Your Color Ramp.** Ensure the color ramp is perceptually uniform and semantically intuitive (e.g., increasing saturation or brightness maps to increasing speed). Use a tool like a colorblindness simulator to check for accessibility.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to Color.** If you are currently using size (stroke width) to encode speed, especially on a curved path, switch to using color value. This will likely improve perceptual accuracy.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Segment Length.** If color is not an option, the next best alternative for conveying speed is to encode *time* using segment length (`TLENGTH`). From this, users can accurately infer speed (longer segment = faster speed). This method was found to be highly effective for both straight and curved paths.

---

## Guidance

Whenever possible, use position along a common scale as the primary visual channel to encode your data, regardless of whether the data is quantitative, ordinal, or nominal.

## Why

Position is the most accurately perceived visual variable by humans. For quantitative data, this means viewers can make the most precise comparisons and judgments of value when data is encoded by position (e.g., the length of a bar in a bar chart, the position of a point in a scatterplot). For categorical and ordinal data, position is also highly effective for grouping and ordering items, making it a universally powerful encoding channel.

## When it applies

- In almost all visualization design scenarios. This is a foundational principle of graphical perception.
- **Quantitative data:** When comparing magnitudes (e.g., sales figures, temperatures). Use bar charts, dot plots, or scatterplots.
- **Ordinal data:** When showing ranked data (e.g., small, medium, large). Arrange items in order along an axis.
- **Nominal data:** When grouping categories. Use spatial grouping or a trellis/small multiples layout.

## Exceptions

- **Spatial Data:** When position is already being used to encode geographic location on a map, other channels like color or size must be used to encode additional data attributes. This is a common and necessary exception.
- **High-Dimensional Data:** For datasets with many variables, techniques like parallel coordinate plots or glyphs must use other channels (angle, size, color) in addition to position to represent all dimensions. Position is still used within the chart, but it can't encode every variable.

## Trade-offs

- **Space:** Position-based encodings, especially bar charts and small multiples, can require significant screen or page space compared to more compact forms like treemaps or packed bubble charts (which use area). The trade-off is often between spatial efficiency and perceptual accuracy.

## Signs of Trouble

- **"Which slice is bigger?":** Viewers are struggling to compare the sizes of pie chart slices or the areas of bubbles in a bubble chart. This indicates a task (comparison) that is poorly supported by the visual channel (angle/area) and would be better served by position.
- **Color-based Ranking:** A multi-hue color ramp (like a rainbow) is used to show quantitative or ordinal data. Viewers cannot reliably order the values based on color.
- **Size Overload:** A chart relies on many different shapes or sizes to encode quantitative values, and the differences are not easily discernible.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Annotations.** If you cannot change the chart type, add direct data labels to elements. This provides a "perceptual escape hatch" by allowing users to read the values directly instead of relying on a less accurate visual channel.
- **Moderate Redesign: Change the Encoding.** If a chart uses color or area to encode the most important quantitative variable, switch to a chart type that uses position. For example, replace a pie chart or bubble chart with a bar chart.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Restructure the Layout.** If you have multiple categories, consider switching from a single, complex chart to a series of simpler charts (small multiples). This uses position to separate categories, simplifying the comparison task within each chart.

    url: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581349
    note: "Citing Smart & Szafir [92], the paper notes that 'CH [color hue] are generally more discriminable with filled shapes than with unfilled ones' (Section 5.1.2)."
---

## Guidance

In multi-class scatterplots where color is used to distinguish categories, use filled shapes (e.g., solid circles) instead of unfilled shapes (e.g., hollow circles, plus signs, or 'x's).

## Why

Color is perceived as more distinct and discriminable when it is applied to a larger, filled-in area. Unfilled shapes have a much smaller area of color, making it harder for the human eye to distinguish between different hues, especially for small marks. Using filled shapes increases the salience of the color encoding, making it easier and faster for viewers to identify and group points belonging to the same category.

## When it applies

- When designing scatterplots with multiple categorical groups distinguished by color.
- When the marks on the chart are small.
- When the number of categories is high, making color differentiation more challenging.

## Exceptions

- **Overlapping Data:** In extremely dense scatterplots where many points overlap, using semi-transparent unfilled shapes can help reveal the underlying density structure, which might be obscured by opaque, filled shapes.
- **Redundant Encoding:** If another, stronger visual channel (like shape itself) is used to redundantly encode the categories, the weakness of color on unfilled shapes may be less of an issue.

## Trade-offs

- **Occlusion:** Opaque, filled shapes can obscure other data points in dense regions of the plot. This can be mitigated by reducing mark opacity or size, but that in turn reduces color salience.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Legend-to-Chart Difficulty:** Viewers have to look back and forth between the legend and the plot multiple times because they cannot easily distinguish the colors of the small, unfilled marks.
- **Category Confusion:** Viewers frequently misidentify the category of a data point because its color on an unfilled mark looks too similar to another category's color.
- **The "Is that blue or purple?" Problem:** The small area of an 'x' or hollow circle makes it perceptually difficult to determine its exact hue.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Fill the Shapes.** Change the mark property from unfilled to filled. This is a simple change that provides an immediate and significant perceptual benefit.
- **Moderate Redesign: Reduce Opacity.** If filling the shapes causes occlusion in dense areas, make the filled shapes semi-transparent (e.g., 70% opacity). This maintains the benefit of a larger colored area while mitigating the occlusion problem.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Small Multiples.** If you have many categories and significant overlap, consider breaking the single scatterplot into small multiples, with one plot per category. This completely eliminates the need to discriminate colors, using spatial position to separate the groups instead.

id: prefer-saturation-over-hue-for-ordinal-data
title: "Prefer color saturation over hue for ordinal data"
tags:
  - impact:perceptual
  - impact:accessibility
  - chart:any
  - task:rank
  - task:compare
  - data:ordinal
  - visual:color
  - access:color-vision-risk
sources:
  - type: research
    ref: Zeng & Battle, 2023
    url: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581349
    note: "The paper summarizes that both theory [61] and empirical work [29] confirm that color saturation (CS) is more expressive and performs better than color hue (CH) for representing ordinal data (Section 5.1.2)."
---

## Guidance

When using color to encode ordinal data (data with an inherent order, like "low, medium, high"), use a sequential color palette that varies in saturation or brightness, not a qualitative palette that varies in hue.

## Why

Color saturation (the intensity of a color) and brightness have a natural, perceived order. Viewers intuitively understand that a darker or more saturated color represents a "higher" or "more" value. In contrast, color hue (e.g., red, green, blue) does not have an inherent perceptual order. A rainbow palette, for example, is perceptually non-uniform and can lead to errors when viewers try to rank the data. Studies confirm that users are more accurate and faster at summary tasks with saturation-based palettes than with hue-based palettes for ordinal data.

## When it applies

- When encoding any data that has a logical order but is not strictly quantitative (e.g., survey responses from "Strongly Disagree" to "Strongly Agree," or ratings from "Poor" to "Excellent").
- When creating choropleth maps showing ranked categories (e.g., low, medium, high risk).
- When color-coding table cells to reflect ranked performance.

## Exceptions

- **Nominal Data:** For categorical data with no inherent order (e.g., "Apple," "Banana," "Orange"), a qualitative palette that varies hue is the correct choice. This guideline is specific to *ordinal* data.
- **Branding Constraints:** If a brand's style guide dictates a specific multi-hue palette, this guideline may be difficult to follow. In this case, the trade-off is between brand consistency and perceptual effectiveness.

## Trade-offs

- **Fewer Distinguishable Steps:** It is harder to create many easily distinguishable steps with a single-hue sequential palette compared to a multi-hue palette. Sequential palettes are best for a small number of ordered categories (typically 3-7).

## Signs of Trouble

- **Rainbow Palette for Ordered Data:** The visualization uses a rainbow-like (spectral) color scheme to represent ordered data like "low, medium, high." This is a classic red flag.
- **Inability to Rank:** A user looking at the visualization cannot easily tell if "green" is higher or lower than "yellow" without constantly referring to the legend.
- **False Groupings:** The non-uniform nature of a rainbow palette can cause viewers to perceive groupings in the data that do not actually exist, simply because certain colors appear more similar than others (e.g., yellow and light green).

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Switch to Grayscale.** A simple grayscale palette is a perfectly effective sequential scheme (light gray to dark gray). It has a clear perceptual order and is accessible by default to colorblind users.
- **Moderate Redesign: Use a Single-Hue Sequential Palette.** Choose a single hue (like blue) and create a palette that varies from a very light, desaturated version of that hue to a dark, saturated version. Tools like ColorBrewer provide pre-built, tested palettes for this purpose.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use a Multi-Hue Sequential Palette.** For a greater number of steps, use a carefully designed multi-hue sequential palette (e.g., one that transitions from light yellow to dark blue). These palettes vary brightness and saturation smoothly while gently shifting hue, providing more perceptual range than a single-hue palette while maintaining a clear order. Avoid spectral (rainbow) schemes.
