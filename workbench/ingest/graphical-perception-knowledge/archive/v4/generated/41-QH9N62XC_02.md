## Guidance

Do not use stacked area charts or stacked line charts when the primary goal is for a user to judge the strength of a correlation. Performance on these charts is often no better than random guessing.

## Why

In stacked charts, only the bottom-most series is anchored to a stable baseline (y=0). All subsequent series are plotted on top of the one below it, meaning their baseline is irregular and constantly changing. This makes it perceptually difficult to judge the values or trends of any series other than the first one. When trying to judge correlation, the viewer must compare two series, at least one of which has a distorted, floating baseline. This introduces significant cognitive load and perceptual error, making accurate judgment of correlation nearly impossible.

## When it applies
- When the user's main task is to determine the relationship (correlation) between two or more series.
- This applies to stacked area charts, stacked line charts, and to a lesser extent, stacked bar charts.

## Exceptions
- **Composition Over Time:** The primary goal of a stacked area chart is to show the change in a whole and its composition over time. If the user's task is to see the total and roughly how it is divided, it can be appropriate, but it is not suitable for comparing the individual components to each other.

## Trade-offs
- While stacked charts are good at showing the total sum of the components, they achieve this by sacrificing the ability to accurately compare the individual components (except for the one on the baseline). The choice of chart depends on which task is more important: seeing the total or comparing the parts.

## Signs of Trouble
- **Can't See the Trend:** It's impossible to tell if two non-baseline series are moving together or in opposition.
- **Incorrect Conclusions:** Users incorrectly believe two series are correlated because they appear to have similar shapes, when this is just an artifact of the changing baseline.
- **Focus on the Total:** Viewers can only comment on the total value, not the relationship between the individual parts.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Unstack the chart.** Convert the stacked area or stacked bar chart into a regular (unstacked) line chart or a grouped bar chart. This gives each series its own stable baseline.
- **Moderate Redesign: Use Small Multiples.** Separate each series into its own small chart (a panel). This gives each series a dedicated space and baseline, making its individual trend clear and enabling comparison across panels.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use a Scatterplot.** If the data is not a time-series, the most direct way to show correlation is to use a scatterplot, plotting one series on the X-axis and the other on the Y-axis. This is the most perceptually effective visualization for the correlation task.
