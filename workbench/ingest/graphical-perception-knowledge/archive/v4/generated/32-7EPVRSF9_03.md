## Guidance

When the main goal is to show the relationship or correlation between two quantitative variables, a scatterplot is the most effective and conventional choice.

## Why

Scatterplots are perceptually effective for this task because they directly map the two variables to the most accurate visual channels: horizontal and vertical position. The resulting cloud of points allows the human visual system to easily and intuitively detect patterns, such as linear or curvilinear trends, clusters, and the strength of a correlation. Studies comparing multiple chart types confirm that scatterplots are superior to other visualizations for judging correlation.

## When it applies

- You have two columns of continuous or discrete quantitative data.
- You want to see if the variables are related, and if so, what the nature of that relationship is (e.g., positive, negative, linear, non-linear).

## Exceptions

- **Very Large Datasets:** With hundreds of thousands or millions of data points, a standard scatterplot can suffer from severe overplotting, where points are drawn on top of each other, obscuring the underlying density.
- **Categorical vs. Quantitative:** If one variable is categorical, a scatterplot is not appropriate. A box plot or violin plot would be a better choice.

## Trade-offs

- Scatterplots can be difficult to read if the axes are not scaled properly or if there are extreme outliers that compress the majority of the data into a small area.
- They primarily show correlation, not causation. Viewers may incorrectly infer a causal link without additional context.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Dual-Axis Deception:** Trying to show correlation by plotting two line charts on a dual-axis chart. This is notoriously misleading, as the perceived correlation can be manufactured by manipulating the axis scales.
- **Separate Charts:** Presenting two separate bar or line charts and asking the audience to infer a correlation between them, which is a difficult and error-prone cognitive task.
- **No Visual Pattern:** A table of numbers is used to imply a correlation, but the viewer is never shown the underlying visual pattern, which might reveal it to be weak, non-linear, or driven by outliers.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Switch to a Scatterplot.** If you have two columns of quantitative data, plot them on the x and y axes of a scatterplot. This is the most direct and effective solution.

- **Moderate Approach (for Overplotting): Use Transparency or Binning.** If the scatterplot is too dense due to overplotting, either make the points semi-transparent (so dense areas appear darker) or switch to a 2D histogram (heatmap), which bins the points and uses color to show the number of points in each bin.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Add a Trend Line and Confidence Interval.** To make the correlation more explicit and provide statistical context, add a regression line (e.g., a line of best fit) to the scatterplot. Showing a confidence interval band around the line also helps communicate the uncertainty of the model fit.
