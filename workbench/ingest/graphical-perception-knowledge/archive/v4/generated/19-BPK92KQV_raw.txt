## Guidance

Use an animated transition that morphs one dataset into another to help viewers quickly and accurately identify the data point with the largest change.

## Why

The human visual system is highly attuned to motion. During an animated transition where all items move for the same duration, the velocity of a mark becomes a direct and powerful perceptual cue for the magnitude of its change. The fastest-moving item is the one that changed the most, allowing for rapid, pre-attentive identification.

## When it applies

- When comparing exactly two datasets in an interactive medium (e.g., a web browser).
- When the primary user task is to find the single value that has changed the most (the "biggest mover" or maximum delta).
- This was shown to be highly effective for bar charts and donut charts.

## Exceptions

- **Task Mismatch:** This technique is ineffective and can hinder performance when the task is to judge overall similarity or correlation between the two datasets. The focus on individual changes distracts from the global pattern.
- **Static Media:** Animation is not possible in static formats like print or a PNG image.
- **Chart Type:** The performance benefit is not universal. In the source study, animation was not the most effective method for slope charts.
- **Visual Instability:** In charts where positions are relative (like sunbursts or treemaps), a large change in one element can cause many other elements to shift, creating disorienting motion that is not related to their own data values.

## Trade-offs

- **Task Specificity:** This design is highly optimized for one task (finding the maximum delta) at the expense of others (like judging correlation).
- **Medium Constraint:** It strictly requires an interactive, screen-based medium, limiting its applicability.
- **Scalability:** The benefit may diminish when comparing more than two datasets simultaneously, as tracking multiple moving objects is cognitively demanding.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Task is Correlation:** The user's goal is to see how "similar" or "different" the two datasets are overall, not to find one specific change.
- **Static Output:** The final visualization needs to be exported as a static image or printed.
- **User Disorientation:** Viewers report that the animation is "too busy" or that it's hard to follow what's happening, which can occur if many items are changing at once.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Slow Down the Animation.** If the motion is too fast or jarring, increase the duration of the transition to make it easier to follow. Use easing (e.g., cubic-in-out) to create smoother acceleration and deceleration.

- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Static Alternative.** If animation proves unsuitable or the medium is static, switch to an overlaid chart, which is the next best-performing design for this specific task.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Provide User-Selectable Layouts.** Implement a control that allows the user to switch between an animated view (for finding the biggest mover) and a static view like mirrored small multiples (for judging correlation). This empowers the user to choose the best representation for their current analytical task.

## Guidance

When using small multiples to compare two datasets, arrange them as mirror images of each other (e.g., center-aligned bar charts with axes pointing outwards) rather than in a standard side-by-side layout.

## Why

The human visual system has a specialized, efficient mechanism for detecting and processing mirror symmetry. Arranging charts this way leverages this perceptual strength, making it easier for viewers to spot both differences between corresponding points and assess the overall similarity of the two datasets. This layout minimizes the cognitive load of comparison compared to standard juxtaposed charts.

## When it applies

- When comparing exactly two datasets using small multiples in a static or interactive format.
- The task is to judge the overall similarity or correlation between the two datasets (this was the top-performing layout for this task).
- The task is to find the largest change between the two datasets, and a small-multiple layout is preferred over animation or overlaying (this layout performs better than standard adjacent or stacked multiples).

## Exceptions

- **More Than Two Datasets:** The concept of a simple mirrored layout does not scale directly to comparing three or more datasets.
- **Convention is Critical:** This is an unconventional layout. If the audience is unfamiliar with it or if strict adherence to a "consistent axis" rule is required, a standard adjacent layout may be safer, albeit less perceptually efficient.
- **Chart Asymmetry:** The benefit may be reduced for chart types that lack a strong symmetrical structure.

## Trade-offs

- **Breaks Convention:** It violates the common design principle of maintaining a consistent axis direction across all small multiples. This may require a brief learning period for the viewer.
- **Limited Scalability:** The simple mirroring approach is fundamentally designed for a pair-wise comparison and does not extend well to a larger number of charts.

## Signs of Trouble

- **User Confusion:** Viewers are unsure how to read the chart with the reversed axis.
- **Multiple Comparisons:** You need to compare three or more datasets, and a mirrored arrangement becomes awkward or impossible.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Axis Cues.** If mirroring is confusing, add clear arrows or labels to the axes to explicitly indicate their direction, helping to orient the viewer.

- **Moderate Redesign: Revert to Adjacent Multiples.** If the mirrored layout proves too unconventional for the audience, fall back to a standard adjacent (side-by-side) small multiple layout. This is less performant but more familiar.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Test with Users.** For a specific chart type and audience, conduct a simple A/B test comparing a mirrored layout to an adjacent layout to see if the perceptual benefit outweighs the cost of unconventionality for your use case.

## Guidance

Do not use animated transitions between two datasets when the viewer's primary task is to assess the overall similarity or correlation between them.

## Why

Animation is effective at highlighting large, individual changes (deltas) because motion velocity is a salient perceptual cue. However, this same feature becomes a distraction when the task is to evaluate a global property like correlation. The viewer's attention is drawn to the "biggest movers," which interferes with their ability to integrate information across all data points and perceive the holistic pattern of similarity.

## When it applies

- When the primary analytical goal is to understand the global relationship between two datasets.
- When asking questions like "How similar are these two series?" or "Which pair of charts is more correlated?".
- This has been shown to be particularly true for bar charts.

## Exceptions

- If the animation's purpose is not analytical comparison but rather to maintain object constancy during a filtering or data update, it can still be useful. However, be aware that it will not aid in judging correlation.
- If the animation is part of a narrative or presentation designed to draw attention to a specific point of change, its use is justified for that rhetorical purpose.

## Trade-offs

- This finding demonstrates that a single design is rarely optimal for all tasks. A layout that is excellent for one type of comparison (finding the max delta) can be detrimental to another (judging correlation).
- Opting for a static design (like mirrored small multiples) improves correlation judgment but loses the powerful cue that animation provides for finding the largest individual change.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Task Mismatch:** The interface uses animation, but the guiding question for the user is about overall similarity, patterns, or correlation.
- **Poor Performance:** Users are inaccurate or slow when asked to pick the "most similar" pair of charts from an animated visualization.
- **User Frustration:** Users report that the animation is "distracting" or that they can't "get a feel for the whole picture" while things are moving.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Allow Pausing.** Provide a control to pause the animation, allowing the user to inspect the start and end states statically. This provides an escape hatch from the distracting motion.

- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Static Layout.** Replace the animation with a static comparison layout. For judging correlation in bar charts, mirrored small multiples are the most effective choice.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Provide Task-Specific Views.** If the interface must support both finding the biggest change and judging correlation, provide separate, optimized views for each task. For example, allow the user to toggle between an "animated (find changes)" mode and a "mirrored (see similarity)" mode.

---

## Guidance

When using a static visualization to help users find the largest change between two datasets, overlaying the charts in the same coordinate space is more effective than placing them side-by-side in small multiples.

## Why

Overlaying (or "superposition") co-locates the corresponding data points from each series. This eliminates the need for the viewer to make eye movements between separate charts and hold values in working memory to perform a comparison. The difference (delta) between the two series can be perceived directly and locally for each point, making it much easier to scan for the largest one.

## When it applies

- The visualization is static (e.g., print, PDF, or a non-animated screen graphic).
- The primary user task is to identify the data point with the maximum difference between two series.
- This applies to bar charts, line charts (slope charts), and donut charts.

## Exceptions

- **Visual Clutter:** If the two data series are very different, or if the chart is dense with data points, overlaying can lead to a cluttered and illegible "spaghetti" effect.
- **More Than Two Series:** Overlaying more than two or three series can quickly become visually overwhelming and difficult to decode.
- **Individual Readability is Key:** If it's more important for the user to be able to clearly read the values from each series individually, a small multiple layout may be better, as overlaying can cause occlusion.

## Trade-offs

- **Clutter vs. Comparison:** Overlaying prioritizes direct comparison at the potential cost of visual clarity and the readability of individual series.
- **Color Dependency:** This technique often relies on color to distinguish the series, which can be problematic for colorblind users if not designed carefully.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Spaghetti Chart:** The overlaid series are heavily intertwined, making it impossible to follow individual lines or distinguish bars.
- **Color Ambiguity:** The chosen colors are not easily distinguishable, especially for color-vision deficient users.
- **Occlusion:** Marks from one series are hiding marks from the other series, making comparison impossible for those points.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Adjust Visual Properties.** Use a colorblind-safe palette. Differentiate the series using another visual channel in addition to color, such as stroke style (solid vs. dashed lines) or fill opacity.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Different Layout.** If overlaying is too cluttered, switch to the next-best static layout for comparison: mirrored small multiples. This separates the charts, reducing clutter while still being more efficient than standard adjacent layouts.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Use Interactive Highlighting.** In an interactive context, allow the user to hover over a data point or legend item to highlight the corresponding series and dim the other. This reduces clutter on demand and facilitates focused comparison.

    note: "Experiments 1A and 2 consistently showed that a stacked (vertical juxtaposition) layout for small multiples was the least effective arrangement for both finding the maximum delta and judging correlation, often resulting in performance so poor it hit the experiment's difficulty ceiling ('floor effect')."
  - type: research
    ref: Cleveland & McGill, 1984
    url: https://doi.org/10.2307/2288400
    note: "This finding aligns with Cleveland & McGill's foundational work emphasizing that comparisons are most accurate along a common, aligned scale, and that increased distance between marks hinders judgment."

---

## Guidance

For detailed comparison tasks, do not arrange small multiples in a stacked (one above the other) layout. Prefer a side-by-side (adjacent) or mirrored layout instead.

## Why

Comparing values between two charts requires the viewer to mentally map a point in one chart to its corresponding point in the other. Stacking the charts significantly increases the physical distance the eye must travel between these corresponding points. This makes direct visual alignment impossible and places a heavy burden on working memory, leading to slow, error-prone comparisons. Performance is dramatically worse than in side-by-side layouts where the distance is minimized.

## When it applies

- When a user needs to perform a detailed comparison between two or more charts presented as small multiples.
- This applies to tasks like finding the largest difference, finding the smallest difference, or judging the overall similarity between the charts.

## Exceptions

- **Severe Space Constraints:** A stacked layout might be the only option in a very narrow column (e.g., a mobile view). In this case, acknowledge that you are sacrificing comparative accuracy for layout feasibility.
- **High-Level Impression Only:** If the goal is just to give a general impression of the charts and detailed comparison is not intended or necessary, stacking may be acceptable.

## Trade-offs

- **Space vs. Accuracy:** A stacked layout is more space-efficient vertically, while adjacent and mirrored layouts are more space-efficient horizontally. Choosing a layout involves a trade-off between perceptual accuracy and fitting the chart into the available space. By stacking, you are prioritizing vertical space savings over the user's ability to make accurate comparisons.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Up-and-Down Scanning:** You observe users repeatedly scanning their eyes up and down between the two charts to compare a single pair of values.
- **Difficulty with Alignment:** It is difficult to tell if two bars in different charts are the same height because they are not aligned and are far apart.
- **Poor Performance:** In user testing, participants are slow or inaccurate when asked questions that require comparing values between the stacked charts. The source study found performance was so low it was often not reliably quantifiable.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Guidelines.** If you must use a stacked layout, add faint horizontal guidelines across all charts that correspond to major axis ticks. This provides a visual aid to help align values across the vertical gap, mitigating the issue slightly.

- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to an Adjacent Layout.** Change the layout from stacked (vertical) to adjacent (horizontal). This brings corresponding values much closer together and aligns them vertically, making comparison significantly easier and more accurate.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use a Mirrored Layout.** For pairwise comparison, switch to a mirrored small multiple layout. This is the most perceptually efficient static arrangement, as it leverages the visual system's sensitivity to symmetry and minimizes comparison distance.
