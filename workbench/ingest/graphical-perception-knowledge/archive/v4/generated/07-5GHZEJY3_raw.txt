## Guidance

To enable the most accurate perception of quantitative data, encode values using position along a common, aligned scale. Position is perceptually superior to length, angle, area, and color.

## Why

Humans are far more accurate at judging and comparing positions on a shared scale than they are at judging other visual attributes. Jock Mackinlay's foundational 1986 model ranks visual encodings by their perceptual accuracy for different data types. For quantitative data, the ranking is:
1.  **Position** (most accurate)
2.  Length
3.  Angle / Slope
4.  Area
5.  Color Saturation / Lightness
6.  Color Hue (least accurate)

Choosing a more effective encoding, like position, reduces interpretation error and helps the audience understand the data more truthfully.

## When it applies

- When the primary goal is for users to accurately compare, rank, or look up quantitative values.
- When designing common charts like bar charts, line charts, dot plots, and scatter plots, which rely on the position channel.

## Exceptions

- **Geographic Data:** When visualizing data on a map, position is already used to encode location. Other channels like color (for choropleths) or size (for bubble maps) must be used for the quantitative values.
- **High-Level Overview:** When the goal is to show a general "sense" of scale or composition rather than precise comparison (e.g., in a treemap), using a less accurate channel like area may be acceptable.

## Trade-offs

- Using position to encode multiple variables often requires more screen space (e.g., faceting into small multiples) compared to encoding secondary variables with color or size on a single chart.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Inaccurate Comparisons:** Viewers struggle to state with confidence which value is larger when the data is encoded using bubble size or pie slice angle.
- **Reliance on Tooltips:** The chart is nearly unreadable without hovering over every mark to see the exact value in a tooltip, indicating the visual encoding itself is failing.
- **Encoding Mismatch:** The most important quantitative comparison in a chart relies on a less effective channel (like color), while a less important variable occupies the primary x- or y-axis.

## How to Improve

- **Moderate Redesign: Switch Chart Type.** If you are using a pie chart, bubble chart, or treemap for precise comparisons, switch to a bar chart or dot plot. This remaps the quantitative data from a weaker channel (angle, area) to the strongest one (position).
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Re-evaluate Encodings.** Review all variables in your chart. Ensure the variable that requires the most accurate interpretation is encoded using position. Demote less critical variables to other channels like color or shape.

## Guidance

For comparing quantitative values, encode data using length on a common baseline (as in a bar chart) rather than angle (as in a pie chart).

## Why

According to the perceptual hierarchy popularized by Mackinlay (1986), humans can judge and compare lengths with much greater accuracy than they can judge angles. While a pie chart's primary encoding is angle, a bar chart uses length, which is a more perceptually effective channel. This means viewers are less likely to make errors when reading a bar chart.

## When it applies

- When comparing the magnitude of different categories.
- When ranking categories from largest to smallest.
- When showing part-to-whole relationships where comparison between parts is important.

## Exceptions

- **Single, Simple Proportion:** If the goal is to highlight a single, simple part-to-whole relationship (e.g., "25% of users completed the survey") and high precision is not required, a pie chart can be an acceptable and highly familiar choice.
- **Familiarity:** If an audience is extremely familiar with pie charts for a specific reporting context, changing to a bar chart might cause temporary confusion, even if the bar chart is more effective.

## Trade-offs

- **Familiarity:** Pie charts, despite their perceptual flaws, are very common. Using a bar chart for part-to-whole relationships might feel less intuitive to some general audiences.
- **Focus:** A pie chart forces a part-to-whole interpretation, whereas a bar chart is more flexible. If the part-to-whole context is the single most important message, a pie chart makes that context unavoidable.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Which is bigger?" Test:** You or your audience cannot easily tell which of two slices in a pie chart is larger.
- **Legend Crutch:** Viewers must constantly look back and forth between the legend and the chart, or rely on data labels, to understand the values.
- **Multiple Pies:** You are using multiple pie charts side-by-side to show change over time or comparison across groups. This is a task for which pie charts are particularly ill-suited.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** If you must keep the pie chart, add direct percentage and/or value labels to each slice. This provides an "escape hatch" for the user, allowing them to read the values instead of trying to interpret the angles.
- **Moderate Redesign: Sort the Slices.** Sorting the pie chart's slices from largest to smallest can make comparisons between adjacent slices slightly easier.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Switch to a Bar Chart.** The most effective fix is to replace the pie chart with a bar chart, sorted by value. This leverages the more accurate "length" channel and makes all comparisons trivial.

sources:
  - type: research
    ref: Mackinlay, 1986
    url: https://doi.org/10.1145/22949.22950
    note: "Establishes the principle of Expressiveness, which states a visualization should not imply information that is not in the data. Using an ordered visual channel for unordered data violates this principle."

examples:
  - type: bad
    description: "Using different-sized bubbles to represent different, unordered categories (e.g., continents). This incorrectly implies that one continent is 'larger' or more important than another in the data."
  - type: bad
    description: "Using a single-color gradient (light blue to dark blue) to represent different product lines. This suggests a ranking or order that does not exist."
  - type: good
    description: "Using distinct shapes (circle, square, triangle) or distinct color hues (blue, orange, green) to represent different categories. These channels do not imply an inherent order."
---

## Guidance

Avoid using visual channels with a natural perceptual order—such as size, length, or color saturation/lightness—to encode nominal (unordered categorical) data.

## Why

This violates the principle of **Expressiveness**. Visual encodings that are naturally perceived as ordered (e.g., small to large, light to dark) create an unintended and false ranking in the viewer's mind. For nominal data where no such order exists (e.g., "Apple", "Banana", "Orange"), this misleads the audience and communicates information that is not in the data.

## When it applies

- When choosing a visual encoding for a nominal or categorical variable.
- When designing legends for categorical data.

## Exceptions

- There are no known exceptions. Applying an ordered visual cue to unordered data is fundamentally misleading.

## Trade-offs

- You sacrifice the use of some powerful visual channels (like size) for your categorical variable, but in return, you maintain the integrity and truthfulness of your visualization.

## Signs of Trouble

- **False Ranking:** The visualization seems to suggest that one category is "more" or "better" than another, simply because of its visual representation (e.g., a darker color or larger size).
- **Confusing Legend:** The legend uses a continuous color ramp or a series of increasing-sized shapes for categories that have no inherent order.
- **Audience Misinterpretation:** Viewers ask questions like, "Why is Europe a lighter color than Asia?" assuming the color difference carries meaning about a quantitative or ordinal value.

## How to Improve

- **Moderate Redesign: Change the Visual Channel.** Replace the ordered visual channel with one that is not perceptually ordered.
  - If using **size/length**, switch to **shape** or **color hue**.
  - If using **color saturation/lightness** (a single-color gradient), switch to **color hue** (a qualitative palette of distinct colors).
  - If using **texture** with varying density, switch to different **texture patterns** (e.g., dots vs. stripes).

    description: "A scatter plot uses distinct shapes (circles, squares, triangles) to show different groups of data points."
  - type: good
    description: "A line chart uses different color hues (e.g., blue, orange, green) to distinguish between multiple time series lines."
  - type: bad
    description: "A line chart uses shades of a single color (light gray to dark gray) to distinguish between three different, unordered product lines. This incorrectly implies a ranking."
---

## Guidance

Use color hue (from a qualitative palette) or shape as primary visual channels for encoding nominal (unordered categorical) data.

## Why

Color hue and shape are effective because they are "identity" channels; they help distinguish items without imposing a perceptual order. This aligns perfectly with the nature of nominal data, where categories are distinct but have no inherent ranking. According to Mackinlay's (1986) model, color hue is one of the most effective retinal (non-positional) encodings for nominal data.

## When it applies

- When distinguishing categories in a chart, such as different groups in a scatter plot or multiple lines in a line chart.
- When the data variable is purely categorical (e.g., Country, Product Type, Gender).

## Exceptions

- **High Cardinality:** If you have too many categories (typically > 7-10), it becomes difficult to find enough distinct hues or shapes that are easily discriminable. In such cases, other solutions like faceting (small multiples) or grouping "other" categories may be necessary.
- **Small Marks:** Shape can become ineffective if the marks on the chart (e.g., points in a scatter plot) are very small or overlap, as the shapes become difficult to identify. In this case, color hue is a better choice.
- **Accessibility:** Color choice must be managed carefully to be accessible to users with color vision deficiencies. Shape is a good alternative or supplement to color for this reason.

## Trade-offs

- **Color:** Requires careful palette selection to ensure colors are discriminable, accessible, and do not have unintended semantic meanings (e.g., red for "bad").
- **Shape:** Can add visual complexity and may be less effective than color for quick, pre-attentive grouping of marks.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Indistinguishable Categories:** Viewers cannot easily tell the difference between categories because the colors are too similar or the shapes are not distinct.
- **Accessibility Failures:** A colorblindness simulator reveals that two or more categories become visually identical.
- **Color Overload:** So many colors are used that the chart becomes a "fruit salad" of visual noise, and the legend is impossible to memorize.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Use a Color Palette Tool.** Use a tool like ColorBrewer or Viz Palette to generate a qualitative, colorblind-safe palette for your chosen number of categories.
- **Moderate Redesign: Combine Color and Shape.** For improved accessibility and clarity, use both color and shape to encode categories. This provides a redundant signal that helps all users.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Small Multiples.** If you have too many categories to encode with color or shape, break the chart into a series of smaller charts (small multiples or facets), one for each category. This uses the powerful "position" channel to separate the groups.

examples:
  - type: good
    description: "A choropleth map uses a single hue that varies from light to dark (e.g., light blue to dark blue) to show levels of a variable, such as low, medium, and high population density."
  - type: bad
    description: "A chart uses a rainbow color scheme (red, yellow, green, blue) to represent an ordered variable like customer satisfaction ratings (Poor, Fair, Good, Excellent). The order of the colors is not intuitive."
---

## Guidance

When encoding ordinal data (e.g., "Low, Medium, High"; "Agree, Neutral, Disagree"), use variations in color saturation or lightness (a sequential color scheme) rather than variations in color hue (a rainbow scheme).

## Why

Color saturation and lightness (e.g., moving from light blue to dark blue) have a natural perceptual order that viewers intuitively understand as representing less to more. This aligns with the inherent order of the data. In contrast, a full spectrum of hues (like a rainbow) does not have a universally agreed-upon perceptual order, making it a poor choice for representing ranked data. Mackinlay's effectiveness ranking for ordinal data places saturation above hue for this reason.

## When it applies

- When encoding data with a clear, non-numeric order.
- When creating heatmaps or choropleth maps where cells or regions are colored according to an ordered-categorical or binned-quantitative scale.

## Exceptions

- **Diverging Data:** If your ordinal data has a meaningful midpoint (e.g., "Negative, Neutral, Positive"), a diverging palette (which uses two hues that diverge from a neutral color) is more appropriate than a sequential one.
- **Strong Semantic Associations:** If specific hues have a powerful, learned meaning for the categories (e.g., red for "Hot", blue for "Cold"), using hue may be more intuitive, even if it's not perceptually linear.

## Trade-offs

- It can be difficult to distinguish many different steps (typically more than 5-7) in a single-hue sequential scheme.
- A purely saturation-based scale may have accessibility issues if the steps do not also have sufficient lightness contrast.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Rainbow Palette:** A rainbow (or spectral) color palette is used to encode ordered data. Viewers cannot reliably say whether yellow is "more" or "less" than green.
- **Ambiguous Order:** The visualization uses a collection of distinct hues (e.g., blue, purple, orange), and the order of the categories is not immediately obvious from the colors alone.
- **Low Discriminability:** The steps in your sequential color ramp are too close together, making it hard to tell a "Low" value from a "Medium-Low" value.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Switch to Grayscale.** A simple grayscale ramp is a robust, universally understood sequential scheme.
- **Moderate Redesign: Use a Sequential Color Scheme.** Replace the multi-hue palette with a single-hue sequential palette (e.g., from a tool like ColorBrewer). This ensures the colors have a clear perceptual order.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use a Perceptually Uniform Palette.** For the highest accuracy, use a color space like CIELAB or HCL to generate your color ramp. This ensures that a given step in the data corresponds to an equally-sized perceptual step in color, preventing distortion. Tools like `d3-scale-chromatic` can generate these palettes.
