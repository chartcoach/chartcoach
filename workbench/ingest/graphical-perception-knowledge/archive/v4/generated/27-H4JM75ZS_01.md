## Guidance

Re-order categorical palettes (for color, shape, etc.) to maximize the perceptual distance between items. The ordering should start with the two most dissimilar items, then iteratively add the item that is most perceptually distant from the already-selected set.

## Why

Default palettes are often ordered arbitrarily (e.g., alphabetically for shapes) or based on simple perceptual dimensions (e.g., by hue for colors). This can result in perceptually similar items being used for adjacent categories, making them difficult to distinguish. A perceptually optimized order ensures that as you add more categories to a chart, you are always using the most distinct and discriminable visual encodings available.

## When it applies

- When assigning visual properties to nominal (unordered) categories.
- When you need to ensure that viewers can clearly and quickly distinguish between different categories in a chart, especially with a high number of categories.
- Applicable to any categorical encoding channel, including color hue, shape, and even size (when used categorically).

## Exceptions

- When a pre-existing order is meaningful (e.g., a specific sequence for brand colors, or a logical progression of shapes).
- For ordinal or quantitative data, where the palette order must reflect the data order (e.g., a sequential color scheme).

## Trade-offs

- A perceptually re-ordered palette may appear less "tidy" or organized than one sorted by hue or shape type, potentially trading aesthetic simplicity for perceptual performance.
- The optimal ordering is dependent on the specific perceptual model (kernel) used.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Squint Test:** When you squint at the chart, do two or more different categories blend together into what looks like a single group?
- **Subtle Neighbors:** The palette contains two very similar items used for different categories (e.g., a light green and a pale teal, or a circle and a slightly rounded square).
- **Frequent Legend Lookups:** Viewers have to repeatedly look back and forth between the chart and the legend because the visual markers in the chart are not easily distinguishable.
- **High Error Rate:** When asked to identify points belonging to a specific category, users make frequent mistakes.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach: Manual Re-ordering.** Look at your chosen palette. Identify pairs of colors or shapes that are too similar and manually swap one of them with a more distinct option from later in the palette. Try to alternate between visually different families (e.g., a warm color then a cool color; a rounded shape then an angular shape).

- **Moderate approach: Use a Perceptually-Informed Palette Generator.** Use tools like Colorgorical or iWantHue that are designed to generate maximally distinct color palettes. These tools often have options to optimize for perceptual distance and color vision deficiency.

- **Comprehensive approach: Use a Perceptual Kernel.** For the highest fidelity, use a pre-computed perceptual kernel (distance matrix) for your chosen visual variable. Implement an ordering algorithm (as described in the paper: start with the most distant pair, then greedily add the next most distant item) to generate a new, optimized palette order.
