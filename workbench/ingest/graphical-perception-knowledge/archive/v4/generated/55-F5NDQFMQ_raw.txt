## Guidance

Avoid using extreme aspect ratios for bar chart marks. Bars that are very tall and thin tend to be recalled as shorter than they are, while bars that are very wide and short are recalled as taller. To improve recall accuracy, prefer bars with a more "squarish" aspect ratio.

## Why

This bias occurs because our memory for shapes is pulled toward a cognitive prototype—in this case, a square. When we recall the height of a non-square bar from memory, our brain adjusts the remembered height in the direction that would make the shape more like a square. This cognitive shortcut leads to systematic overestimation for wide bars and underestimation for tall bars, distorting comparisons made across time or space.

## When it applies

- When using bar charts, especially if the bars have extreme aspect ratios (very tall and thin, or very wide and short).
- When users need to compare values from memory, such as across different charts in a dashboard, in sequential views of an interactive visualization, or between a chart and a non-adjacent legend.
- The principle may also apply to other charts that use rectangular marks where aspect ratio is an incidental property, such as Mekko charts or treemaps.

## Exceptions

- **Direct, simultaneous comparison:** The bias is primarily a memory effect. If the user can see the bars being compared at the same time (e.g., adjacent bars in the same chart) without relying on memory, the effect is minimal.
- **Direct data labels:** When bars are directly labeled with their values, users have a cognitive "escape hatch" to read the precise value. This doesn't fix the biased visual impression but mitigates the risk of misinterpretation.

## Trade-offs

- **Space Efficiency:** Forcing bars to have a squarish aspect ratio can be inefficient. A chart with many categories will naturally have thin bars, and making them wider might require an excessively wide chart area.
- **Aesthetics vs. Accuracy:** Extreme aspect ratios are sometimes used for stylistic effect. This guideline prioritizes perceptual accuracy over those stylistic choices.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Extreme Proportions:** The bars in your chart are either very tall and skinny ("skyscraper" look) or very short and wide ("pancake" look).
- **Inaccurate Comparisons:** Users struggle to accurately judge the relative size of bars, especially when comparing values between two separate charts or from memory.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Data Labels.** Directly label bars with their exact values. This allows users to read the precise numbers, compensating for the visual recall bias.

- **Moderate Redesign: Adjust Chart Dimensions.** Change the overall width and height of your chart to make the aspect ratio of the bars closer to 1:1. This could mean making the chart area wider to give bars more width, or adjusting the y-axis range if appropriate.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Dot Plots for Cross-Chart Comparisons.** If the primary task is comparing values across different charts (where memory is required), consider using dot plots instead of bar charts. A dot plot encodes a value using only a single point for its position, which has no aspect ratio and is therefore not subject to this specific bias.
