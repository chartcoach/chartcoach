## Guidance

When creating charts for comparing or ranking quantitative values, encode the data using position along a common, aligned scale. Bar charts, dot plots, and line charts do this well. Avoid using less accurate channels like angle (pie charts) or area (bubble charts, treemaps) for this task.

## Why

Decades of graphical perception research have established a clear hierarchy of how accurately humans perceive different visual encodings. We are most accurate when judging position along a common scale. Other channels are progressively less accurate in roughly the following order: position (unaligned scales), length, angle, area, and finally color saturation/hue. Using a more accurate channel leads to fewer interpretation errors, higher precision, and faster understanding.

## When it applies

- When the primary task for the viewer is to accurately compare, rank, or look up quantitative values.
- When small but meaningful differences in the data need to be clearly visible.

## Exceptions

- **Showing Composition:** When the primary task is to show a part-to-whole relationship, area-based charts like pie charts or treemaps can be acceptable, though they remain poor for comparing the parts to each other.
- **Showing High-Level Patterns:** When the goal is to see a broad overview or pattern rather than precise values, such as identifying hotspots in a heatmap (using color) or understanding hierarchical structure in a treemap (using area).

## Trade-offs

- **Space:** Charts that rely on position (like bar charts) can take up more linear space than area-proportional charts (like treemaps) that can be packed more densely.
- **Engagement:** For some general audiences, area-based charts like pie charts may feel more familiar or aesthetically engaging, even if they are perceptually less effective. Choosing a bar chart over a pie chart may be a trade-off between perceptual accuracy and audience familiarity.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Pie Chart for Comparison:** A pie chart, donut chart, or bubble chart is used when the main goal is to compare categories to each other.
- **Ranking Errors:** Viewers struggle to accurately rank the slices of a pie chart or the bubbles in a bubble chart, especially when values are close.
- **Hidden Differences:** Small but important differences between data values are not visible in the chart because the chosen visual channel (e.g., area) is not precise enough to represent them.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Direct Data Labels.** If you must use a less accurate chart (like a pie chart), add direct data labels to each element. This provides a numerical "escape hatch," allowing users to read the precise values instead of relying on flawed visual estimation.

- **Moderate Approach: Switch to a Better Encoding.** Convert the chart to use a more effective visual channel. For example:
  - Replace a pie chart (angle/area) with a bar chart (length/position).
  - Replace a bubble chart (area) with a dot plot (position).

- **Comprehensive Approach: Re-evaluate the Core Task.** Step back and clarify the primary question the visualization is meant to answer. Choose a chart type that is fundamentally aligned with that task and uses the most effective visual channel according to the perceptual hierarchy. For example, if the task is "ranking," a sorted bar chart is almost always a better choice than a pie chart.
