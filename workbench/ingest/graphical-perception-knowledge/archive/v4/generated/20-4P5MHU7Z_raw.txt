## Guidance
When the primary task is to compare the lengths or magnitudes of different categories, place the graphical marks (bars, dots, etc.) on a common, shared baseline. Avoid using chart types like stacked bars for precise component-to-component comparison.

## Why
Humans perceive and compare position along a common scale more accurately than they compare unaligned lengths. Placing bars on a shared baseline (like in a standard bar chart) allows viewers to use this superior perceptual skill. Unaligned comparisons, which are necessary to compare segments across different stacked bars, are a fundamentally harder perceptual task and lead to higher error rates.

## When it applies
- When comparing quantitative values across different categories.
- When ranking categories based on a quantitative value is a key task.
- When using bar charts, grouped bar charts, or dot plots.

## Exceptions
- When showing the part-to-whole composition of a total is the primary goal, and precise component-to-component comparison is secondary. In this case, a stacked bar chart might be acceptable, but with the knowledge that comparisons will be less accurate.

## Trade-offs
- Using a grouped bar chart (which has a common baseline for all bars) typically requires more horizontal space than a stacked bar chart.
- Switching from a stacked to a grouped chart makes it harder to see the total sum of the segments for each main category.

## Signs of Trouble
- **The Stacked Comparison:** The chart is a stacked bar chart, and the viewer's primary task is to answer questions like, "Which main category has the largest value for the blue segment?" or "How much bigger is the red segment in Category A than in Category B?"
- **Viewer Error:** Users consistently misjudge the relative sizes of segments across different stacks.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Add Data Labels.** If you must use a stacked bar chart, add direct data labels to the key segments. This provides an escape hatch, allowing viewers to read and compare numbers instead of relying on flawed length perception.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Grouped Bar Chart.** Change the stacked bar chart to a grouped bar chart. This gives every segment its own bar starting from the same zero baseline, making comparisons direct and accurate.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use a Dot Plot.** For each main category, create a separate dot plot or a set of aligned dots on a common scale. This removes the bar's area, focusing the viewer entirely on the position, which is the most accurate visual cue for this task.

  - type: research

## Guidance
To help viewers accurately compare the heights of two or more bars, place them directly next to each other whenever possible. Avoid placing other "distractor" bars between them.

## Why
Visual separation between bars increases the difficulty and error rate of height comparison. This "Separation Effect" forces viewers to hold one bar's height in working memory while scanning across the gap to the other, a process that is prone to error. The effect is a significant source of perceptual difficulty, especially when comparing short bars.

## When it applies
- When a key task for the viewer is to compare the values of two or more specific categories in a bar chart.
- When designing static charts where the order of categories can be manipulated.

## Exceptions
- When the categorical order is inherently meaningful and fixed, such as chronological time (e.g., months of the year) or alphabetical order (e.g., country names), and the items to be compared are not naturally adjacent.

## Trade-offs
- Sorting bars by value to bring important items together can be very effective for comparison, but it disrupts any pre-existing meaningful order (like alphabetical or regional).

## Signs of Trouble
- **The Long Scan:** Viewers have to scan their eyes back and forth across several other bars to make a single comparison.
- **Important Pairs are Separated:** The two bars that are most critical for the chart's message are on opposite ends of the chart.
- **High Error on Short Bars:** Viewers have particular trouble comparing short bars that are far apart.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Use Interactive Highlighting.** In an interactive chart, allow users to click or hover on bars to highlight them and fade the distractors. This visually shortens the distance and reduces interference. You can also show the exact values in a tooltip.
- **Moderate Redesign: Sort the Data.** If the bar order is not fixed, sort the chart by the quantitative value. This often brings bars of similar height close together, facilitating comparison. It also makes ranking effortless.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use a Direct Comparison Chart.** If comparing specific pairs is the primary goal, consider creating a chart that visualizes the comparison directly, such as a dumbbell plot (showing the two values and the difference) or a slope chart.

---
## Guidance
Avoid designing a visualization where the primary task requires viewers to compare the lengths of two segments that are stacked directly on top of each other.

## Why
When two segments are stacked, viewers are susceptible to a "part-to-whole" cognitive bias. Instead of comparing the top segment to the bottom segment, their perception is unconsciously influenced by comparing the top segment to the *total length* of the combined stack. This leads to a systematic and significant underestimation of the true ratio between the two segments.

## When it applies
- In stacked bar charts or divided bar charts where you need to compare the size of the component parts.
- When asking questions like, "What percentage of Segment B is Segment A?" where A is stacked on B.

## Exceptions
- If only a very rough "is it bigger or smaller?" judgment is needed and precision is not a goal.
- If the segments are so disproportionate (e.g., one is 10x the other) that the bias is negligible compared to the obvious difference.

## Trade-offs
- While the solutions fix the comparison bias, they can make it harder to see the total sum of the segments or require more chart space.

## Signs of Trouble
- **The Task:** The chart's primary purpose is to compare the size of stacked components (e.g., comparing Q1 sales to Q2 sales within a single yearly bar).
- **The Bias:** When tested, viewers consistently underestimate the ratio. For example, when comparing a 50-unit segment to another 50-unit segment (a 100% ratio), their answers might cluster around 50% (the part-to-whole ratio of 50/100).

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Add a Gap.** Introduce a small, visible gap between the segments. This visually breaks the "whole" bar, disrupting the part-to-whole bias and encouraging a more direct segment-to-segment comparison.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Grouped Bar Chart.** Instead of stacking the segments, place them side-by-side as individual bars on a common baseline. This allows for accurate, unbiased comparison.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Plot the Comparison Directly.** Create a chart that directly visualizes the comparison of interest. For example, if you want to show the ratio of Segment A to Segment B, calculate that ratio and plot it directly on a bar or dot plot.

title: "Minimize tall distractor bars between items being compared"
tags:
  - impact:perceptual
  - chart:bar
  - chart:bar.stacked
  - task:compare
  - data:quantitative
  - visual:position
sources:
  - type: research
    ref: Talbot et al., 2014
    url: https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2014.2346320
    note: "Experiments 1 and 2 showed that tall distractor bars increase comparison error more than short ones, especially in unaligned (stacked) charts."
---
## Guidance
When two bars to be compared are not adjacent, be aware that tall bars located between them ("distractors") will interfere with the comparison and increase perceptual error.

## Why
Tall intervening bars act as visual obstacles, making the mental task of comparing the heights of the two target bars more difficult. This interference is more significant than that caused by short distractors and is particularly detrimental in stacked bar charts where comparisons are already challenging.

## When it applies
- In bar charts where key items for comparison are not, or cannot be, placed next to each other.
- When evaluating the layout of a bar chart for potential perceptual pitfalls.

## Exceptions
- If the positions of all bars are fixed by a meaningful, unchangeable order (e.g., a timeline) and the distractors cannot be removed or altered.

## Trade-offs
- Reordering a chart to minimize distractors for one comparison might create new distractors for another comparison.
- Focusing on reducing distractors might conflict with maintaining a logical sort order.

## Signs of Trouble
- **The Picket Fence:** A critical comparison must be made between two bars that are separated by several other bars, some of which are taller than one or both of the target bars.
- **Focus Drifts:** A viewer's attention is drawn to the tall distractor bars instead of the bars they are supposed to be comparing.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Use Interactive Highlighting.** In an interactive setting, allow users to select bars for comparison. The system should then visually emphasize the selected bars (e.g., by increasing their opacity) and de-emphasize all others (e.g., by lowering their opacity or changing them to a light gray).
- **Moderate Redesign: Reorder the Chart.** If possible, reorder the chart to place the bars that need to be compared closer together. For example, if comparing against a benchmark, place the benchmark bar next to the bar for the current item.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Small Multiples or Faceting.** If multiple comparisons need to be made, break the chart into smaller, faceted charts. This can isolate comparison groups and eliminate distractors. For instance, you could have a separate small chart for each region, all sharing a common scale.

title: "Exercise caution when comparing separated short bars"
tags:
  - impact:perceptual
  - chart:bar
  - task:compare
  - task:rank
  - data:quantitative
sources:
  - type: research
    ref: Talbot et al., 2014
    url: https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2014.2346320
    note: "Experiment 1 found that the error from separating bars is much larger when the reference bars are short."
---
## Guidance
Recognize that comparing the heights of short bars is inherently difficult and error-prone, and this difficulty is magnified when the bars are separated by a large distance.

## Why
The perceptual error caused by spatial separation is not uniform; it has a much greater impact when the bars being compared are short. While the absolute error might be small, the *relative* error can become very large, making judgments about which bar is taller, or by how much, unreliable.

## When it applies
- In bar charts with a large y-axis range, which causes some values of interest to appear very short.
- When small but important differences between low-magnitude items need to be communicated.

## Exceptions
- When only a rough sense of magnitude is needed and precise comparison between the short bars is not a primary task.
- When the difference between the short bars is so large (e.g., one is 10 times the other) that it's obvious despite the difficulty.

## Trade-offs
- An attempt to fix this by re-scaling the y-axis, for example by truncating it (starting from a non-zero value) or using a log scale, can be a form of deception and introduces other perceptual distortions. These techniques should be used with extreme caution and clear annotation.

## Signs of Trouble
- **The Lawn:** The chart has a few very tall bars and a large number of very short bars, making the short ones look like a flat "lawn."
- **Indistinguishable Stubs:** It is nearly impossible to tell if one short bar is taller than another short bar several spaces away.
- **High Relative Error:** The difference between two short bars is 5 units, but viewers perceive it as 1 or 10, a high percentage error.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Add Data Labels.** The simplest and most direct solution is to add data labels directly to the short bars. This allows viewers to read the exact values, bypassing the perceptual challenge.
- **Moderate Redesign: Use Interactive Zoom.** In a digital chart, provide an interactive feature like a "spyglass" tool or a brush-and-zoom that allows users to select and magnify a section of the y-axis to see the short bars more clearly.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use a Panel Chart.** Create a two-panel chart. The top panel shows the full chart with all bars for context. The bottom panel shows a "zoomed-in" view of just the shorter bars, using a y-axis scale appropriate for their magnitude. This provides both context and detail without distortion.
