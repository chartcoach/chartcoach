## Guidance

When assigning categorical colors in a scatterplot, identify pairs of classes whose points are spatially close or overlapping. Assign these class pairs colors that are perceptually far apart (e.g., a blue and an orange, not a light green and a teal).

## Why

The human visual system struggles to segment objects that are both spatially close and chromatically similar. By maximizing the perceptual color difference for neighboring clusters, you reduce the cognitive load required to distinguish between them, making class boundaries clearer and reducing interpretation errors.

## When it applies

- In multiclass scatterplots or other point-based charts (e.g., maps with category glyphs) where different categories of marks overlap or are in close proximity.
- When the task requires viewers to accurately identify the class of points, especially in dense or overlapping regions.

## Exceptions

- If classes that are spatially close are also semantically related (e.g., sub-species of a species), using similar colors (e.g., shades of blue) might be a deliberate choice to encode this relationship. This intentionally sacrifices some class separability to communicate hierarchy.

## Trade-offs

- You must "spend" your most distinct color pairs on the most critical adjacencies. This may leave less distinct color pairs (e.g., two shades of green) for other classes, but this is acceptable if those classes are spatially well-separated from each other.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Color Bleeding:** The boundary between two adjacent clusters appears fuzzy because their assigned colors are too similar.
- **Mistaken Identity:** You find yourself frequently misreading the class of a point that lies near the border of two clusters.
- **Legend Check:** You have to constantly look back and forth between the plot and the legend to tell two neighboring classes apart.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Identify and Swap.** Find the most spatially-confused pair of classes. In the legend, swap one of their colors with a color from another class that is far away from both.
- **Moderate Approach: Use a Perceptual Palette.** Start with a color palette designed to be perceptually uniform and maximally distant (e.g., from ColorBrewer or tools like Colorgorical). While this doesn't guarantee optimal *assignment*, it increases the chance that adjacent colors will be distinct.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Model and Optimize.** Use a color difference metric like CIEDE2000 to calculate the perceptual distance between all pairs of colors in your palette. Then, use an algorithm to assign the color pairs with the largest distance to the class pairs with the smallest average spatial distance between their points.
