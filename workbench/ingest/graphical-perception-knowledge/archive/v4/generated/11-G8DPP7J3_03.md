## Guidance

Use distinct color hues (e.g., red, blue, green) to represent different categories that have no inherent order. This is also known as a qualitative or categorical color palette.

## Why

Color hue is an "identity" channel. According to Jock Mackinlay's expressiveness principles, it is perfectly suited for encoding nominal (categorical) data because it does not imply any sense of order or magnitude. Our visual system is adept at rapidly identifying and grouping objects by hue, making it an effective way to distinguish different categories at a glance.

## When it applies

- When encoding unordered categories in any chart type, such as:
    - Different series in a line or bar chart (e.g., `Product A`, `Product B`).
    - Categories in a scatter plot (e.g., `Species A`, `Species B`).
    - Regions or groups on a map.

## Exceptions

- **Ordered Data:** Do not use a qualitative palette of distinct hues for data that has an inherent order (e.g., `Low`, `Medium`, `High` or a range of numbers). This would fail to communicate the ordered nature of the data. Use a sequential or diverging palette instead.
- **High Cardinality:** It is difficult for humans to reliably distinguish more than about 7-10 different color hues. If you have more categories than this, color alone is not a sufficient encoding. You will need to combine it with other techniques like grouping, highlighting, or using other visual channels.

## Trade-offs

- **Accessibility:** A palette of distinct hues must be carefully chosen to be distinguishable by people with color vision deficiencies. A randomly chosen set of hues will likely fail this test.
- **Semantic Associations:** Hues carry strong cultural and emotional associations (e.g., red for danger/loss, green for success/environment). A purely categorical palette might create unintended meanings.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Implied Order:** A chart uses a sequential color palette (e.g., shades of light blue to dark blue) to encode distinct categories like `North America`, `Europe`, and `Asia`. This incorrectly implies that `Asia` is "more" or "higher" than `Europe`.
- **Indistinguishable Hues:** The palette contains multiple colors that are too similar, such as a blue and a purple, or a red and an orange, making it hard to tell them apart, especially for small marks on the chart.
- **Color Overload:** The chart uses a dozen or more different hues, creating a "rainbow" effect that is visually overwhelming and difficult to decipher.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Use a Pre-built Safe Palette.** Replace your current colors with a pre-vetted, colorblind-safe categorical palette. Tools like ColorBrewer, Viz Palette, or Tableau's built-in palettes are excellent sources.
- **Moderate Approach: Reduce Cardinality.** If you have too many categories to encode with color effectively, try to group them into a smaller number of "super-categories". You can encode the super-categories with distinct hues and then use lightness variations or another channel for the sub-categories. For example, color-code `Fruits` and `Vegetables`, then use shapes for `Apple`, `Banana`, `Carrot`, `Broccoli`.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Create an Optimized Palette.** If you have specific brand or semantic constraints, start with those colors and use an optimization tool (like Huemint or Colourmap Hospital) to generate a full palette of complementary colors that are both semantically appropriate and perceptually distinct.
