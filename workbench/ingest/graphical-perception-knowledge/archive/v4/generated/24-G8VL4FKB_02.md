## Guidance

When choosing how to visually encode quantitative data, prioritize encodings that rely on higher-ranked perceptual tasks. The general hierarchy, from most to least accurate, is:
1.  **Position** along a common scale (e.g., bar charts, dot plots)
2.  **Length** (e.g., unaligned bars)
3.  **Angle** or **Direction** (e.g., pie charts, slopes)
4.  **Area** (e.g., bubble charts, treemaps)
5.  **Volume** (e.g., 3D bar charts)
6.  **Shading** or **Color Saturation** (e.g., heatmaps, choropleth maps)

## Why

Graphical perception is the cognitive process of decoding information from a chart. Research by Cleveland & McGill (1984) demonstrated that the choice of visual encoding directly impacts the speed and accuracy of this process. Using a higher-ranked encoding (e.g., position) results in more accurate judgments and better pattern detection than a lower-ranked one (e.g., area). Selecting an encoding from higher up the hierarchy maximizes a viewer's ability to see patterns and understand the data correctly.

## When it applies

- This is a fundamental principle that applies during the initial design phase of any visualization that represents quantitative data.
- It is especially critical when accuracy and the ability to make fine-grained comparisons are important for the audience.
- When evaluating an existing chart to see if its encoding is optimal for its purpose.

## Exceptions

- **Information Density:** Sometimes a lower-ranked encoding is chosen to increase information density. For example, a choropleth map (shading) shows a geographic pattern that would be lost if the data were presented in a simple bar chart (position).
- **Aesthetics & Engagement:** Lower-ranked encodings like area (in a bubble chart) can sometimes be more visually engaging or stylistically appropriate, even if they are less precise. This is a deliberate trade-off.
- **Multiple Variables:** When encoding multiple variables, you must necessarily use a combination of visual channels, some of which will be lower on the hierarchy (e.g., a scatterplot using position for two variables and bubble size/area for a third).

## Trade-offs

- **Accuracy vs. Density:** Higher-ranked encodings like position often require more space (e.g., a bar chart vs. a single colored cell in a heatmap). There can be a trade-off between perceptual accuracy and information density.
- **Accuracy vs. "Big Picture":** While a bar chart is more accurate for value comparison, a treemap (area) or choropleth map (shading) may be better for quickly showing the overall structure or a spatial pattern, even if individual comparisons are imprecise.

## Signs of Trouble

- **"Is this bubble bigger?"** Viewers struggle to determine the relative size of areas (bubble charts), volumes (3D charts), or the intensity of shades (heatmaps).
- **Subtle Differences Lost:** The visualization fails to reveal a known, important but subtle difference between data points because the encoding is not precise enough to make it visible.
- **Mandatory Tooltips:** Viewers must hover over every single mark to understand its value. While tooltips are a useful feature for details-on-demand, relying on them for basic comparisons indicates the primary visual encoding has failed.

## How to Improve

- **Identify the Current Encoding:** First, determine what perceptual task your chart requires for its primary comparison (e.g., bubble chart = area, pie chart = angle, heatmap = color saturation).

- **Move Up the Hierarchy:** Consider if you can redesign the chart to use a higher-ranked task. This often involves switching chart types:
    - If using **Angle** (pie chart), switch to **Position** (bar chart).
    - If using **Area** (bubble chart), consider switching to **Position** (dot plot or bar chart).
    - If using **Color Saturation** (choropleth map), consider if a bar chart for each region (a form of position encoding) would better serve the task of comparison, even if it loses some geographic context.
