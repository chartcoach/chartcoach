## Guidance

The choice to mix "open" and "closed" shapes is most critical when the viewer's task is to judge the number of points (numerosity) or identify a trend/correlation for a specific category within a multi-class plot. This choice is less critical for tasks like judging the average value of a group.

## Why

Tasks like trend-spotting and counting require viewers to perceptually group and isolate a set of marks from surrounding distractors. The "pop-out" effect of mixing open and closed shapes directly aids this grouping process, reducing cognitive load and improving performance. Judging an average position is a different kind of perceptual task that is less affected by this type of "same-category" interference.

## When it applies

- When designing a multi-class scatterplot and prioritizing design decisions.
- If the chart's primary purpose is to enable trend, correlation, or distribution analysis for different categories, then proper shape selection is a high-priority decision.

## Exceptions

- If the chart is intended only for coarse, high-level comparisons of group position (e.g., "are the blue dots generally higher on the y-axis than the red dots?"), then other encoding choices like color or position might be more important than shape.

## Trade-offs

- Focusing on optimizing shape for trend analysis might mean de-prioritizing other aesthetic goals or branding constraints. Acknowledging this trade-off helps in making an informed design decision.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Task-Design Mismatch:** The chart is designed for trend analysis but uses shapes that perceptually interfere with each other (e.g., all closed shapes), making the primary intended task unnecessarily difficult for the viewer.
- **Misguided Effort:** A designer spends significant time optimizing shape aesthetics for a chart where the primary task is a simple average value comparison, a task for which shape choice has minimal impact on performance.

## How to Improve

- **Clarify the Primary Task:** Before finalizing the design, explicitly state the main question the chart should answer. Use this question to guide your encoding choices.
- **Match Design to Task:** If the task is "Compare the correlation of stocks A and B," ensure the shapes for A and B are from different open/closed categories (e.g., circle and cross). If the task is "Is stock A's average price higher than B's?", the specific choice of shapes is less critical, and you can prioritize other factors like aesthetics or brand consistency.
