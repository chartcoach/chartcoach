## Guidance

Avoid using a color gradient (e.g., changing brightness or hue) to represent the continuous passage of time along a 2D trajectory.

## Why

Humans are not adept at accurately perceiving and discriminating small variations in color value or hue. When time is encoded as a gradient, it becomes very difficult for viewers to map a specific shade back to a precise moment in time, leading to significantly larger errors in judgment compared to other encodings like segment length or even size (stroke width). Although it is a common technique, experiments show it is perceptually ineffective for accurate lookup tasks.

## When it applies

- When visualizing movement or trajectory data on a 2D plane (e.g., a map or an abstract plot).
- When a key task for the viewer is to accurately estimate the time elapsed at a specific point on the path (e.g., "At what time did the vehicle reach this location?").

## Exceptions

- **General Overview Only:** If the goal is only to provide a general, non-precise visual sense of direction or flow (e.g., "the path generally went from light to dark"), and accuracy is not important, color may be acceptable. However, it should not be relied upon for any analytical task.
- **Categorical Time:** If time is broken into a few, very distinct categorical chunks (e.g., "Morning," "Afternoon," "Night") and represented with highly discriminable colors, this can be effective. This guideline applies specifically to encoding continuous time with a smooth gradient.

## Trade-offs

- **Aesthetics vs. Accuracy:** A color gradient might look visually appealing and clean, but this aesthetic quality comes at the direct cost of perceptual accuracy. This guideline prioritizes accuracy over a clean look.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Is it Lighter or Darker?" Problem:** Viewers are unable to confidently determine if one point on the trajectory is "earlier" or "later" than another if their colors are similar.
- **Gradient Ambiguity:** A single continuous color gradient (e.g., light blue to dark blue) is used. Viewers struggle to map a specific shade to a percentage of time elapsed (e.g., is this shade 60% or 70% of the way through?).
- **Fast Guessing:** Users give up on accurately decoding the color and just make a quick, often inaccurate, guess. This can be observed in user testing as very fast but highly erroneous completion times.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Explicit Labels.** If you must use a color gradient, add explicit time labels or markers at key intervals (e.g., 25%, 50%, 75%) along the path to provide viewers with reference points.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to Size.** Change the encoding from color value to size (stroke width). While not the best method, it is perceptually more accurate for reading time than color value. The path would get progressively thicker over time.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Switch to Segment Length.** The most effective solution is to replace the color gradient with time ticks that create segments of equal time duration (`TLENGTH`). This encoding is demonstrably more accurate for time-perception tasks.
