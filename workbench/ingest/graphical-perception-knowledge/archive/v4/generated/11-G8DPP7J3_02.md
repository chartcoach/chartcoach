## Guidance

To represent and compare quantitative data, encode values using position along a common, aligned scale. This is the visual encoding used in bar charts, dot plots, and scatter plots. It is more accurately perceived than other visual encodings like length, angle, area, or color saturation.

## Why

Foundational research in graphical perception by Cleveland & McGill established a hierarchy of how accurately humans perform elementary perceptual tasks. Judging position along a common scale is the most accurate task. Tasks like judging length (unaligned bar charts), angle (pie charts), or area (bubble charts) are all demonstrably less accurate, leading to more errors in judgment. Using position ensures that viewers can make the most precise comparisons and estimations.

## When it applies

- The primary goal is for the audience to accurately compare, rank, or look up quantitative values.
- You need to make small, but important, differences between values apparent.
- You are choosing between a bar chart (position), a pie chart (angle), or a bubble chart (area) to show magnitudes.

## Exceptions

- **Showing Part-to-Whole Composition:** While a bar chart is still more accurate, a pie chart's primary strength is emphasizing that the values are parts of a whole. If this is the single most important message and high precision is not required (and you have few slices), a pie chart might be considered.
- **Limited Space & High Cardinality:** When showing a very large number of data points in a small space, area-based charts like treemaps can be more space-efficient than bar charts, at the cost of comparison accuracy.
- **Correlation of Two Variables:** A scatter plot uses two positional scales (X and Y) to show the relationship between two variables, which is optimal for that specific task.

## Trade-offs

- **Space:** Bar charts and dot plots can take up more space (especially vertical or horizontal length) than more compact forms like treemaps.
- **Familiarity/Aesthetics:** In some non-analytical contexts, audiences may be more familiar with or aesthetically prefer other chart forms like pie charts, even if they are less perceptually accurate.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Pie Chart with Many Slices:** It is nearly impossible to accurately compare the sizes of slices in a pie chart, especially if they are close in value or not adjacent.
- **Bubble Chart Comparisons:** Viewers are notoriously bad at accurately comparing the areas of circles. Most people judge by radius or diameter, leading to systematic underestimation of the differences in area.
- **Stacked Bar Chart Comparisons:** In a stacked bar chart, only the bottom-most series of bars rests on a common baseline. It is very difficult to accurately compare the lengths of the segments in the middle or top of the stacks.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** If you must use a less effective chart type like a pie or bubble chart, add direct data labels (the numerical values) to each element. This gives users an "escape hatch" to read the precise value instead of relying on inaccurate perceptual judgment.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch Chart Type.**
    - Replace a pie chart with a bar chart to switch from judging angles to judging position.
    - Replace a bubble chart with a bar chart or a dot plot.
    - "Unstack" a stacked bar chart into a grouped bar chart, so all bars share a common baseline.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Re-evaluate the Core Task.** Step back and confirm the primary goal for the user. If it is indeed comparison, commit to using the most effective chart (bar, dot plot). If the goal is something else (e.g., showing composition as the top priority), ensure the chosen chart form is optimized for that task and its limitations are mitigated (e.g., a pie chart with 3 slices and clear labels).
