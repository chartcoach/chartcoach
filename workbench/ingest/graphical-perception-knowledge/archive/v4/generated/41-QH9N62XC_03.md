## Guidance

When using a parallel coordinates plot to show correlations, invert one of the axes to represent a positive correlation as a negative one (lines crossing). Human perception is significantly more accurate at judging correlation from the pattern of crossing lines than from parallel lines.

## Why

In parallel coordinates, a strong positive correlation appears as a set of mostly parallel lines, while a strong negative correlation appears as lines crossing at a single focal point. Perceptual experiments show that people are much better at detecting the pattern and density of lines crossing at a point (negative correlation) than they are at judging the degree of "parallel-ness" of lines (positive correlation). The visual system can more easily detect the singular pattern of a focal point than the noisy, varied slopes of imperfectly parallel lines.

## When it applies
- When using a parallel coordinates plot (PCP).
- When a primary task for the user is to identify and assess the strength of correlations between adjacent variables.

## Exceptions
- If the axes have a natural, unchangeable direction (e.g., "low" to "high" for temperature) and inverting them would violate user convention and cause misinterpretation, it may be better to keep the standard orientation and accept the lower perceptual performance for positive correlations.

## Trade-offs
- **Convention vs. Perception:** Inverting an axis makes the visualization more perceptually effective but may violate a user's expectation of a standard "low-to-high" orientation, potentially causing initial confusion. A clear axis label indicating the inversion is crucial.
- **Asymmetrical Performance:** Without inversion, performance for judging positive vs. negative correlation is asymmetrical. By inverting axes for positive correlations, you are standardizing on the more effective visual pattern (crossing lines).

## Signs of Trouble
- **Indistinguishable Patterns:** Users cannot tell the difference between a plot showing a moderate positive correlation and one showing no correlation, as both may look like a "jumble of lines."
- **Focus on Outliers:** When viewing positive correlations, users may focus on the few lines that cross (outliers) rather than the overall pattern of parallel-ness.
- **Inconsistent Judgments:** Users are much more confident and accurate when judging negative correlations (crossing lines) than positive ones.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Add a Note.** If you cannot invert the axis, add a text annotation explaining that "more parallel lines mean stronger positive correlation." This offloads the work from perception to cognition.
- **Moderate Redesign: Invert the Axis.** For any pair of axes showing a positive correlation, invert one of them. For example, if both axes run from 0 (bottom) to 100 (top), change one to run from 100 (bottom) to 0 (top). This will transform the visual pattern from parallel lines to crossing lines.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Interactive Inversion.** Implement a feature that allows users to click an axis to invert it. This gives the user control and allows them to actively explore correlations by transforming them into the more perceptually effective representation.
