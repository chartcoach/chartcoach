## Guidance

Be aware that the aspect ratio of bars in a bar chart can systematically bias a viewer's perception of their value. Tall, thin bars tend to be underestimated, while short, wide bars tend to be overestimated.

## Why

Experiments on graphical perception have revealed a systematic bias related to the shape of bars. This is a subtle perceptual phenomenon where the geometry of the mark itself influences our judgment of its encoded value (length). Bars with a "squarish" aspect ratio (where height is closer to width) are judged most accurately, while extreme aspect ratios lead to predictable errors in estimation.

## When it applies

- When designing any bar chart where accurate judgment of magnitude is important.
- When creating responsive visualizations where bar widths might change dramatically at different screen sizes.

## Exceptions

- When bars are so wide that they are perceived more as filled areas than as objects with a specific height (e.g., a divided bar chart showing 100%).
- If direct data labels are present on all bars, the perceptual bias is less of a concern as viewers can rely on the text instead of judging the length.

## Trade-offs

- Adjusting bar width to achieve a less biased aspect ratio can affect the overall dimensions and data density of the chart. For a fixed chart width, wider bars mean fewer bars can be displayed.
- Aesthetic considerations or brand guidelines might dictate a specific bar width that is not perceptually ideal.

## Signs of Trouble

- **"Skyscraper" Bars:** The chart contains extremely tall and thin bars, which are likely to be perceptually underestimated by viewers.
- **"Pancake" Bars:** The chart contains very short and wide bars, which are likely to be perceptually overestimated by viewers.
- **Inconsistent Judgments:** In user testing, viewers consistently overestimate or underestimate values in a predictable way depending on the shape of the bars.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Direct Data Labels.** As a simple and effective mitigation, add data labels to the bars. This gives viewers a direct way to read the value, bypassing the need for perceptual estimation.

- **Moderate Approach: Adjust Aspect Ratio.** If you have control over the chart's design, adjust the bar widths to make them more "squarish" and less extreme. Avoid using very skinny or very fat bars, especially if precise visual comparison is a key goal.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Switch to a Dot Plot.** If this bias is a significant concern, consider using a dot plot instead of a bar chart. A dot plot encodes value using only the position of a point, which is not subject to the same aspect ratio bias. This cleanly separates the data encoding (position) from the mark (the dot).
