```md
## Guidance

For the most accurate and effective visual representation, use position on a common scale (as in bar charts, scatter plots, and line charts) to encode your data, regardless of whether it is quantitative, nominal, or ordinal.

## Why

The human visual system is more accurate at perceiving and comparing differences in position along a common axis than any other visual variable (like angle, length, area, or color). This principle, established by foundational research, holds true across all primary data types, making position the most robust and reliable encoding channel. Using position leads to faster and more accurate interpretation by viewers.

## When it applies
- This is a near-universal guideline that applies to almost any visualization where accurate value comparison, ranking, or lookup is a primary goal.
- It is the default, most effective choice for encoding the most important variables in your dataset.

## Exceptions
- **Showing Composition:** While bar charts (position) can show part-to-whole relationships, some designers may prefer area-based charts like treemaps or pie charts (angle/area) if the primary goal is to emphasize the "part-of-a-whole" concept and precise comparison is secondary.
- **Geographic Data:** For map-based visualizations, position is already used to encode geographic location. In these cases, other channels like color (for choropleths) or size (for proportional symbols) must be used to encode quantitative values for those locations.

## Trade-offs
- **Space:** Charts that rely on position, like bar charts or scatter plots, can require more screen or page space than more compact representations like heatmaps or glyphs.
- **Variable Limit:** A single 2D chart can only use position to encode two, or at most three, variables (X, Y, and faceting/small multiples). Other channels are required for additional variables.

## Signs of Trouble
- **Hard to Compare:** Viewers struggle to accurately rank categories or tell which value is larger. This is common when using angle (pie charts), area (bubble charts), or color saturation.
- **Misinterpretation:** Viewers consistently misjudge the magnitude of differences between data points.
- **Reliance on Labels:** The chart is unreadable without direct data labels on every element, suggesting the visual encoding itself is failing.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Add Position Guides.** If you must use a chart that doesn't use position (e.g., a pie chart), add leader lines to align labels or use annotations that make comparisons easier. This is a patch, not a fix.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Better Encoding.** Change the primary visual channel to position. For example, convert a pie chart or a bubble chart into a bar chart. This aligns all values on a common baseline, making comparisons perceptually easier and more accurate.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Re-evaluate the Task.** Reconsider the main goal of the chart. If the task is comparison or ranking, redesign the visualization to use position on a common scale as the primary encoding channel from the start. For example, instead of a choropleth map using color, consider a bar chart of the locations, sorted by value.
```

```md
## Guidance

When the primary task is to show the relationship or correlation between two quantitative variables, use a scatterplot.

## Why

Scatterplots encode two variables using position on orthogonal axes (X and Y). The human visual system is highly effective at judging the shape, direction, and tightness of the resulting cloud of points to estimate correlation. Experiments consistently show that scatterplots are among the most accurate and reliable visualizations for this task, performing well for both positive and negative correlations.

## When it applies
- The main goal is for a user to determine the existence, strength, and direction (positive or negative) of a relationship between two continuous variables.
- You need to identify outliers or clusters in a bivariate distribution.

## Exceptions
- **Very Large Datasets:** With hundreds of thousands or millions of points, overplotting can make a standard scatterplot unreadable, obscuring the true data density. In this case, binned plots (like a 2D histogram or hexbin plot) or density plots are better alternatives.
- **Time-Series Data:** If one of the variables is time, a line chart is often more conventional and effective at showing trends and patterns over time, even though the underlying data is correlational.

## Trade-offs
- **Overplotting:** As mentioned, scatterplots can become dense and unreadable with large amounts of data, hiding the underlying structure.
- **Limited Variables:** A single scatterplot can only show the relationship between two variables. To show more, a scatterplot matrix (SPLOM) is needed, which can become complex.

## Signs of Trouble
- **The Hairball Effect:** Points are so dense and overlapping that the chart becomes a solid, un-interpretable shape, hiding density variations.
- **Outlier Dominance:** A few extreme outliers are stretching the axes, causing the majority of the data points to be clumped together in a small area.
- **Difficulty Judging Trend:** Users cannot confidently determine the direction or strength of the relationship.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Adjust Opacity and Size.** Reduce the opacity and/or the size of the points. This helps reveal areas of high density in cases of moderate overplotting.
- **Moderate Redesign: Use Binning.** If overplotting is severe, switch from plotting individual points to using a 2D histogram (squares) or a hexbin plot. These charts group points into bins and use color to encode the number of points in each bin, making density patterns explicit.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use a Scatterplot Matrix (SPLOM).** If you need to show correlations among multiple (3+) variables, use a SPLOM to display all pairwise scatterplots in a grid. This provides a comprehensive overview of all relationships in the dataset.
```

```md
    note: "Experiment 2 found that stacked line, stacked area, and stacked bar charts showing positive correlation produced unreliable results, meeting the exclusion criteria for being at 'chance' level performance (Section 4.3 & 5.2)."
---
## Guidance

Do not use stacked area charts or stacked line charts when the primary goal is for a user to judge the strength of a correlation. Performance on these charts is often no better than random guessing.

## Why

In stacked charts, only the bottom-most series is anchored to a stable baseline (y=0). All subsequent series are plotted on top of the one below it, meaning their baseline is irregular and constantly changing. This makes it perceptually difficult to judge the values or trends of any series other than the first one. When trying to judge correlation, the viewer must compare two series, at least one of which has a distorted, floating baseline. This introduces significant cognitive load and perceptual error, making accurate judgment of correlation nearly impossible.

## When it applies
- When the user's main task is to determine the relationship (correlation) between two or more series.
- This applies to stacked area charts, stacked line charts, and to a lesser extent, stacked bar charts.

## Exceptions
- **Composition Over Time:** The primary goal of a stacked area chart is to show the change in a whole and its composition over time. If the user's task is to see the total and roughly how it is divided, it can be appropriate, but it is not suitable for comparing the individual components to each other.

## Trade-offs
- While stacked charts are good at showing the total sum of the components, they achieve this by sacrificing the ability to accurately compare the individual components (except for the one on the baseline). The choice of chart depends on which task is more important: seeing the total or comparing the parts.

## Signs of Trouble
- **Can't See the Trend:** It's impossible to tell if two non-baseline series are moving together or in opposition.
- **Incorrect Conclusions:** Users incorrectly believe two series are correlated because they appear to have similar shapes, when this is just an artifact of the changing baseline.
- **Focus on the Total:** Viewers can only comment on the total value, not the relationship between the individual parts.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Unstack the chart.** Convert the stacked area or stacked bar chart into a regular (unstacked) line chart or a grouped bar chart. This gives each series its own stable baseline.
- **Moderate Redesign: Use Small Multiples.** Separate each series into its own small chart (a panel). This gives each series a dedicated space and baseline, making its individual trend clear and enabling comparison across panels.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use a Scatterplot.** If the data is not a time-series, the most direct way to show correlation is to use a scatterplot, plotting one series on the X-axis and the other on the Y-axis. This is the most perceptually effective visualization for the correlation task.
```

```md
id: prefer-negative-correlation-in-pcp
title: "In parallel coordinates, flip axes to create negative correlations"
tags:
  - impact:perceptual
  - chart:parallel-coordinates
  - task:correlation
  - data:quantitative
  - visual:position
  - visual:angle
sources:
  - type: research
    ref: Harrison et al., 2014
    url: https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2014.2346979
    note: "Section 4.5 and Figure 5.a show that parallel coordinates plots depicting negatively correlated data significantly outperform those depicting positively correlated data (p < 0.001) for the correlation task."
---
## Guidance

When using a parallel coordinates plot to show correlations, invert one of the axes to represent a positive correlation as a negative one (lines crossing). Human perception is significantly more accurate at judging correlation from the pattern of crossing lines than from parallel lines.

## Why

In parallel coordinates, a strong positive correlation appears as a set of mostly parallel lines, while a strong negative correlation appears as lines crossing at a single focal point. Perceptual experiments show that people are much better at detecting the pattern and density of lines crossing at a point (negative correlation) than they are at judging the degree of "parallel-ness" of lines (positive correlation). The visual system can more easily detect the singular pattern of a focal point than the noisy, varied slopes of imperfectly parallel lines.

## When it applies
- When using a parallel coordinates plot (PCP).
- When a primary task for the user is to identify and assess the strength of correlations between adjacent variables.

## Exceptions
- If the axes have a natural, unchangeable direction (e.g., "low" to "high" for temperature) and inverting them would violate user convention and cause misinterpretation, it may be better to keep the standard orientation and accept the lower perceptual performance for positive correlations.

## Trade-offs
- **Convention vs. Perception:** Inverting an axis makes the visualization more perceptually effective but may violate a user's expectation of a standard "low-to-high" orientation, potentially causing initial confusion. A clear axis label indicating the inversion is crucial.
- **Asymmetrical Performance:** Without inversion, performance for judging positive vs. negative correlation is asymmetrical. By inverting axes for positive correlations, you are standardizing on the more effective visual pattern (crossing lines).

## Signs of Trouble
- **Indistinguishable Patterns:** Users cannot tell the difference between a plot showing a moderate positive correlation and one showing no correlation, as both may look like a "jumble of lines."
- **Focus on Outliers:** When viewing positive correlations, users may focus on the few lines that cross (outliers) rather than the overall pattern of parallel-ness.
- **Inconsistent Judgments:** Users are much more confident and accurate when judging negative correlations (crossing lines) than positive ones.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Add a Note.** If you cannot invert the axis, add a text annotation explaining that "more parallel lines mean stronger positive correlation." This offloads the work from perception to cognition.
- **Moderate Redesign: Invert the Axis.** For any pair of axes showing a positive correlation, invert one of them. For example, if both axes run from 0 (bottom) to 100 (top), change one to run from 100 (bottom) to 0 (top). This will transform the visual pattern from parallel lines to crossing lines.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Interactive Inversion.** Implement a feature that allows users to click an axis to invert it. This gives the user control and allows them to actively explore correlations by transforming them into the more perceptually effective representation.
```

```md
id: prefer-ordered-line-charts
title: "Sort the x-axis of line charts by a variable to improve correlation judgment"
tags:
  - impact:perceptual
  - chart:line
  - task:correlation
  - data:quantitative
  - data:ordinal
  - visual:position
sources:
  - type: research
    ref: Harrison et al., 2014
    url: https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2014.2346979
    note: "Section 4.5 and Figures 5.d/5.e show that an 'ordered line chart' (sorted by one of the variables) significantly outperformed a standard line chart and a radar chart for judging positive correlation (p < 0.001)."
---
## Guidance

When using a line chart to show the correlation between two variables (and the x-axis does not represent time), sort the data points along the x-axis according to one of the variables. This creates a smoother, more monotonic line that makes it easier to perceive the trend of the second variable.

## Why

In a standard line chart with an arbitrary or alphabetical x-axis order, the lines connecting the points can be jagged and noisy, obscuring the underlying relationship between the two variables. By sorting the data based on one variable, you create a smooth, continuous line for that variable (a straight diagonal line if it's plotted, or just the sorted order). The line for the second variable will then show its trend relative to the first in a much clearer, less noisy way. This reduces the perceptual and cognitive load required to discern the correlation.

## When it applies
- When using a line chart to show a relationship between two quantitative variables.
- When the x-axis does not have a natural, intrinsic order (like time).
- When the user's primary task is to judge the correlation between the two variables.

## Exceptions
- **Time-Series Data:** Never re-order a time-series axis. The chronological sequence is the most critical piece of information.
- **Categorical Data:** If the x-axis represents categories with a conventional order (e.g., "Small, Medium, Large" or days of the week), re-sorting may violate user expectations and cause confusion.

## Trade-offs
- Sorting by a variable imposes an order that might not be intuitive to the viewer if they are used to an alphabetical or other default sort order. The choice of which variable to sort by also influences the final appearance.

## Signs of Trouble
- **Jagged Mess:** The line chart is a noisy, jagged mess of up-and-down movements, making it impossible to see an overall trend or relationship.
- **Order-Dependence:** Changing the sort order of the x-axis dramatically changes the perceived relationship, indicating the visual representation is not stable.
- **No Clear Pattern:** Viewers cannot determine if the relationship between the variables is positive, negative, or non-existent.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Add a Trend Line.** Overlay a statistical trend line (e.g., a linear regression line) on top of the noisy line chart. This explicitly shows the underlying trend that is difficult to perceive.
- **Moderate Redesign: Sort the Data.** Re-order the data points based on their value for one of the variables. For example, if plotting "Height vs. Weight," sort the data by "Height" from smallest to largest before plotting. This will smooth the visual pattern.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Switch to a Scatterplot.** For showing correlation between two quantitative variables, a scatterplot is almost always the superior choice. It directly represents the relationship without being dependent on the sort order of a third, categorical variable or index.
```
