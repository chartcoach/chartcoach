## Guidance

Be aware that perceptually uniform, monotonic-luminance colormaps (like viridis), while excellent for many tasks, can make it harder for viewers to judge and compare the magnitude of gradients (i.e., "steepness") in scalar fields.

## Why

These colormaps are designed to be smooth and continuous, intentionally avoiding the creation of visual artifacts or boundaries. While this is a strength for accurately representing continuous data, it removes the salient features (color bands) that viewers subconsciously use as proxies to estimate the density of change. Without these features, judging the overall "activity" of a field is more difficult.

## When it applies

- When evaluating a visualization where the key takeaway should be the relative "steepness" or rate of change in the data.
- When you receive user feedback that a heatmap or color-coded map "looks flat" or that it's hard to see structural differences between two versions of the map.

## Exceptions

- This guidance is less relevant when the primary task is not gradient perception. For tasks like reading a specific value, identifying the maximum value, or assessing the overall distribution, perceptually uniform colormaps are often the best choice and are highly recommended for their accuracy and accessibility.

## Trade-offs

- You may be choosing a colormap that is superior for accessibility (colorblind-safe) and value lookups, but at the cost of reduced performance for the specific task of gradient comparison. Prioritizing one task may weaken another.

## Signs of Trouble

- **"Is there a difference?":** When showing two maps (e.g., a 'before' and 'after' simulation), users cannot tell which one represents a more volatile or steeper dataset, even when a significant numerical difference exists.
- **"It's all a blur":** The smooth color transitions make the entire map look like a soft, undifferentiated field, hiding important structural variations.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach: Switch to a Diverging Colormap.** Change the colormap to a diverging one (e.g., blue-white-red). The sharp change in the middle acts as a natural contour line, helping viewers see how tightly packed the "above" and "below" regions are, which is a proxy for gradient.
- **Moderate approach: Create a Dedicated Gradient View.** Generate a second visualization that explicitly encodes the gradient of the scalar field. This separates the concern of showing value from the concern of showing rate of change, allowing you to choose the best possible encoding for each.
- **Comprehensive approach: Allow User Control.** Empower users to switch between a default perceptually uniform colormap and an alternative (like diverging or binned) designed to highlight gradients. This lets them select the best tool for their current analytical task.
