## Guidance

When using shape to encode different data categories in a single chart, combine "open" shapes (e.g., +, ×, *) with "closed" shapes (e.g., ●, ■, ▲) to make them easier to tell apart.

## Why

The human visual system pre-attentively groups open shapes and closed shapes into distinct perceptual categories. Using a mix of these types makes one group "pop out" from the other, reducing the cognitive effort needed to distinguish between data series, especially in dense or cluttered plots.

## When it applies

- When using multiple shape encodings in a single visualization, such as a multi-class scatterplot.
- When the primary task for the viewer is to distinguish between the different categories or to analyze the properties of one category in the presence of others.

## Exceptions

- If a chart is very sparse and the data categories are already well-separated by position, the choice of shape is less critical.
- When brand guidelines strictly limit the available shapes to only one type (e.g., only circles and squares are permitted).

## Trade-offs

- Some open shapes (like asterisks) can be more visually complex or "heavy" than simple closed shapes, which might affect the overall aesthetic balance of the chart.
- This approach intentionally creates perceptual distance, which may be undesirable if the goal is to show a subtle or unified relationship between categories.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Category Confusion:** Viewers have difficulty telling which mark belongs to which category, especially at a glance or in a cluttered chart.
- **Slow Interpretation:** Viewers have to consciously search for all marks of a given category instead of perceiving them as a single, cohesive group.
- **The "All-the-Same" Effect:** In a plot that uses only closed shapes (e.g., circles, squares, triangles), the different categories visually blend together into a single, hard-to-parse cloud of points.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** If you are currently using two closed shapes (e.g., circle and square), change one of them to an open shape (e.g., circle and plus sign). This simple swap can significantly improve discriminability.
- **Moderate Redesign:** Systematically review your default shape palette. For a 3-category plot, instead of using circle, square, and triangle, use a mix like circle, plus sign, and square.
- **Comprehensive Approach:** For a multi-category plot, create a shape palette that deliberately pairs open and closed shapes to maximize the perceptual distance between all categories being displayed. Test this palette in a realistic, cluttered chart to confirm its effectiveness.

## Guidance

In a multi-class scatterplot, avoid encoding different categories with shapes that are all from the same perceptual group. For example, do not use only "closed" shapes (like circles, squares, and triangles) to represent three different categories.

## Why

Shapes from the same perceptual group (e.g., all closed or all open) create more perceptual interference with each other. This makes it harder for a viewer to mentally isolate one category and perform analytical tasks like judging its numerosity (how many points?), distribution, or trend, as the other "similar" shapes compete for attention and clutter the view.

## When it applies

- For multi-class scatterplots where viewers need to perform analysis tasks (like judging correlation, density, or numerosity) on individual categories within the plot.
- This is especially important in charts with moderate to high data density, where symbols overlap or are close together.

## Exceptions

- When the primary task is simply to see the overall combined distribution of all points, and the distinction between categories is secondary.
- In very sparse plots where categories are well-separated by position, reducing the potential for interference.

## Trade-offs

- Following this guideline may limit your palette of available shapes, potentially conflicting with aesthetic or branding requirements that favor a "cleaner" look of using only simple, closed shapes.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Analytical Struggle:** Viewers report that it's "hard to see the trend for the squares because of all the circles getting in the way."
- **Task Errors:** Accuracy is low or response time is high for tasks like "Which category has more points in the upper-right quadrant?" or "Does the 'triangle' category show a positive correlation?".
- **Visual "Noise":** One category's marks feel like noise that obscures the signal of another, rather than a distinct group.

## How to Improve

- **Minimal Change:** If your chart uses circles and squares to show two categories, change the squares to plus signs. This breaks the "all closed shapes" interference and makes each category easier to isolate visually.
- **Systematic Review:** Audit your organization's or tool's default shape palettes. Ensure that for N > 1 categories, the default shapes are a mix of open and closed types to minimize perceptual interference out-of-the-box. For example, a default palette could be [circle, plus, square, cross, triangle].

## Guidance

When one data category in a chart is the primary focus of analysis and others serve as context, encode the primary category with a "closed" shape (e.g., a circle ●) and the contextual categories with "open" shapes (e.g., plus signs +).

## Why

Research shows that closed shapes are processed more quickly and accurately as targets. However, they are also more susceptible to perceptual interference from other closed shapes. Pairing a closed-shape target with open-shape distractors leverages the processing speed advantage of the closed shape while minimizing the perceptual interference from the contextual shapes, making the primary category stand out.

## When it applies

- In multi-class scatterplots where the analytical goal is asymmetrical—that is, focused on one category against the backdrop of others.
- For example, when analyzing "our product's" performance (primary, closed shape) versus "competitors'" (context, open shapes).

## Exceptions

- If all categories are of equal importance, this approach can create an unintended and misleading visual hierarchy. In that case, balancing the salience of all shapes is more important.
- When the primary target category has very few data points, an open shape might be too visually light; a closed shape could give it necessary presence.

## Trade-offs

- This approach intentionally creates a visual hierarchy by making the closed shape more salient. This is a negative consequence if all categories should be perceived as equally important.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Unbalanced Focus:** In a chart where all categories are meant to be equal, the category encoded with a closed shape (among open ones) draws too much viewer attention.
- **Reverse Struggle:** When the primary target is an open shape and the distractors are closed shapes, the task can become more difficult because the context is more salient than the target.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** Identify the single most important category in your chart's narrative. Change its symbol to a simple, filled circle and change all other category symbols to plus signs or crosses.
- **Moderate Redesign:** In addition to changing the shape, you can further enhance the focus on the primary category using color (e.g., a saturated color for the primary, gray for the context) to create a clear figure-ground relationship.

## Guidance

The choice to mix "open" and "closed" shapes is most critical when the viewer's task is to judge the number of points (numerosity) or identify a trend/correlation for a specific category within a multi-class plot. This choice is less critical for tasks like judging the average value of a group.

## Why

Tasks like trend-spotting and counting require viewers to perceptually group and isolate a set of marks from surrounding distractors. The "pop-out" effect of mixing open and closed shapes directly aids this grouping process, reducing cognitive load and improving performance. Judging an average position is a different kind of perceptual task that is less affected by this type of "same-category" interference.

## When it applies

- When designing a multi-class scatterplot and prioritizing design decisions.
- If the chart's primary purpose is to enable trend, correlation, or distribution analysis for different categories, then proper shape selection is a high-priority decision.

## Exceptions

- If the chart is intended only for coarse, high-level comparisons of group position (e.g., "are the blue dots generally higher on the y-axis than the red dots?"), then other encoding choices like color or position might be more important than shape.

## Trade-offs

- Focusing on optimizing shape for trend analysis might mean de-prioritizing other aesthetic goals or branding constraints. Acknowledging this trade-off helps in making an informed design decision.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Task-Design Mismatch:** The chart is designed for trend analysis but uses shapes that perceptually interfere with each other (e.g., all closed shapes), making the primary intended task unnecessarily difficult for the viewer.
- **Misguided Effort:** A designer spends significant time optimizing shape aesthetics for a chart where the primary task is a simple average value comparison, a task for which shape choice has minimal impact on performance.

## How to Improve

- **Clarify the Primary Task:** Before finalizing the design, explicitly state the main question the chart should answer. Use this question to guide your encoding choices.
- **Match Design to Task:** If the task is "Compare the correlation of stocks A and B," ensure the shapes for A and B are from different open/closed categories (e.g., circle and cross). If the task is "Is stock A's average price higher than B's?", the specific choice of shapes is less critical, and you can prioritize other factors like aesthetics or brand consistency.
