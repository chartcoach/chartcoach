## Guidance

Do not superpose (overlay) two or more sets of bars in the same chart space if the viewer's goal is to compare their overall mean or range.

## Why

Superposing bars creates significant visual clutter. It forces the viewer to perform a difficult mental filtering task: first, segregate the bars into their respective sets based on color, and only then attempt to judge the collective properties of each set. This process is cognitively demanding and perceptually error-prone, leading to the least precise judgments for summary tasks like comparing means or ranges.

## When it applies

- When designing a bar chart to compare the summary statistics (e.g., mean, range) of two different datasets.
- When tempted to save space by overlaying two bar charts instead of placing them in separate panels (small multiples).

## Exceptions

- **Item-to-Item Comparison:** When the primary task is to find the largest *difference between corresponding bars* (a "delta" comparison). Prior research shows superposition can be very effective for this task, as the "overhang" between bars becomes a salient visual feature that directly represents the difference.
- **Highlighting a Subset:** If one set of bars is a subset of the other, superposition can effectively show the part-to-whole relationship for each category.

## Trade-offs

- **Spatial Efficiency:** The main reason to superpose bars is to save space. Avoiding it in favor of separate charts (e.g., stacked or adjacent small multiples) requires more screen or page real estate. This is a direct trade-off between spatial efficiency and perceptual precision.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Visual Clutter:** The chart looks like a "jumble of bars," and it is difficult to visually isolate one series from the other.
- **"Just Guessing":** Viewers report that they are "just guessing" which set has the higher average or wider range because it's too difficult to tell from the overlaid visualization.
- **Focus on Items, Not Sets:** Viewers focus on comparing individual pairs of bars (e.g., "the blue bar is taller here") instead of forming an impression of the sets as a whole.
- **Color-Reliance:** The chart becomes unreadable if printed in grayscale or viewed by a user with color vision deficiency, as color is the only channel separating the two data series.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Use Transparency.** If you must superpose the bars, make one set semi-transparent to reduce some of the visual occlusion and interference. This is only a minor improvement but can help slightly.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Grouped Bar Chart.** Instead of overlaying the bars, place corresponding bars side-by-side. This keeps them geographically close for comparison but gives each bar its own un-occluded space, making them easier to read.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Small Multiples.** Separate the two charts completely, placing them one above the other (stacked) or side-by-side (adjacent). The research found the stacked arrangement to be the most precise for comparing means and ranges, making it the ideal choice.
