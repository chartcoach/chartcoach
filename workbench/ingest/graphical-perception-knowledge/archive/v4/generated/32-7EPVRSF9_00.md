## Guidance

To make it easier for viewers to accurately compare the values of two or more bars, place them as close together as possible. Avoid making viewers compare bars on opposite ends of the chart.

## Why

The minimum perceptible difference between two bars, known as the Just Noticeable Difference (JND), increases significantly as the distance between the bars grows. Viewers judge bar heights by comparing the positions of their endpoints. This task is perceptually easy when the bars are adjacent but becomes difficult and error-prone when they are separated by other bars or empty space.

## When it applies

- When a primary goal of a bar chart is to have viewers compare or rank the magnitudes of specific categories.
- When designing layouts for small multiples of bar charts where comparisons across charts are intended.

## Exceptions

- If the categorical order of the bars is fixed and meaningful (e.g., alphabetical, chronological, or geographical) and this order is more important than facilitating direct comparisons. In this case, reordering the bars to aid comparison would disrupt the primary structure of the data.

## Trade-offs

- Reordering bars to facilitate a specific comparison might make other comparisons harder or break a conventional sort order (like alphabetical) that the audience expects.
- Grouping bars might require more thoughtful design to avoid creating misleading clusters.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Far-Apart Comparison:** The chart's narrative or annotations ask the viewer to compare two bars that are on opposite ends of the chart.
- **User Uncertainty:** Viewers express uncertainty or make errors when asked which of two distant, similarly-sized bars is taller.
- **"Looks the Same":** Two bars with slightly different values are perceived as identical because the distance between them is too large for the small difference to be noticeable.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Gridlines or Annotations.** If you cannot reorder the bars, add subtle gridlines that span the plot area. This helps the eye track positions across the chart. Alternatively, add direct data labels to the specific bars you want to compare.

- **Moderate Approach: Reorder the Bars.** If the bar order is not fixed, sort the chart to bring the bars of interest next to each other. For example, sorting the chart by value places all bars of similar height close together.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Use a Dot Plot or Slope Chart.** If multiple, non-adjacent comparisons are critical, a dot plot may be a better choice as it de-emphasizes the bar "body" and focuses on the position of the point. To explicitly show the difference between two specific items, a slope chart can be highly effective.
