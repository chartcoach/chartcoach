## Guidance
When choosing a visualization for comparing time series patterns, select a technique that aligns with the specific type of similarity you want to emphasize. The choice of visualization (e.g., line chart, horizon graph, colorfield) directly influences how viewers perceive similarity.

## Why
Human perception of similarity is not absolute; it is shaped by the visual encoding. A line chart, horizon graph, and colorfield can lead a viewer to different conclusions about which two time series are "most similar." This is because each visualization emphasizes or de-emphasizes different signal characteristics, such as local time shifts (warping), amplitude, or vertical offset. Mismatching the visualization and the intended similarity logic can lead to confusion, incorrect interpretations, and a loss of trust in automated similarity search results.

## When it applies
- When designing systems for visual similarity search or clustering of time series data.
- When presenting the results of an automated similarity search algorithm to an expert for validation.
- When choosing a chart type to help users compare multiple temporal patterns.

## Exceptions
- If the task is simple value lookup or trend identification for a single series, the choice of visualization has less impact on the perception of similarity to other series.

## Trade-offs
- A visualization optimized for one type of similarity (e.g., shape regardless of time shifts) will likely be suboptimal for another (e.g., shape at exact time points). There is no single "best" visualization for all similarity tasks.
- More complex visualizations like horizon graphs may better match certain similarity logics but can have a steeper learning curve and slower task completion times compared to standard line charts.

## Signs of Trouble
- **User-Machine Disagreement:** Users consistently disagree with the "top match" provided by an automated similarity search algorithm.
- **Inconsistent Judgments:** Different users looking at the same chart make wildly different similarity judgments, indicating high subjectivity.
- **Verbal Friction:** Users describe patterns as similar in ways that contradict the visualization's emphasis (e.g., saying "the shapes are the same" while the chart's colors for them are completely different).

## How to Improve
- **Quick: Clarify the Goal.** Before choosing a chart, explicitly define what makes two patterns similar for your task. Should time shifts be ignored? Is amplitude important? Use this definition to guide your choice.

- **Moderate: Match Vis to Logic.** Choose a visualization that is known to align with your desired similarity logic.
  - For similarity that **ignores local time shifts** (like Dynamic Time Warping), consider **horizon graphs**.
  - For similarity that **ignores amplitude/offset** (like z-normalization), use **line charts** or **colorfields**.

- **Comprehensive: System-Wide Alignment.** If using an automated similarity algorithm, ensure the visualization presented to the user has the same invariance properties as the algorithm. For example, if the algorithm is invariant to time-warping (uses DTW), present the results using a horizon graph, which perceptually favors this type of similarity.

## Guidance
When comparing time series patterns where local shifts or stretches in time should be ignored, use horizon graphs instead of line charts or colorfields.

## Why
Horizon graphs use a dual encoding: color for high-level value ranges (e.g., positive/negative bands) and layered position/shape for detailed values. Research shows this encourages viewers to focus on the overall pattern and shape, making them less sensitive to local misalignments or "warping" along the time axis. This perceptual tendency aligns well with similarity algorithms like Dynamic Time Warping (DTW) that are designed to find similar shapes regardless of temporal shifts.

## When it applies
- When comparing temporal patterns where the exact timing is less important than the overall shape.
- For application domains like EEG analysis, gait analysis, or speech recognition, where patterns can be faster, slower, or slightly shifted but still represent the same underlying event.
- When presenting results from a similarity search that uses a time-warping-invariant measure like DTW.

## Exceptions
- When the exact timing, duration, or alignment of events is critical. In such cases, a line chart is more appropriate as it makes temporal deviations more apparent.
- When the audience is unfamiliar with horizon graphs, as their interpretation requires learning and can be slower.

## Trade-offs
- **Clarity vs. Speed:** While horizon graphs align better with time-warping invariance, they can be slower for users to interpret and may lead to lower agreement between users compared to line charts.
- **Sensitivity:** Horizon graphs are very sensitive to changes in amplitude and vertical offset, which can be problematic if those factors should also be ignored (see related guideline).

## Signs of Trouble
- **Timing Fixation:** With a line chart, users are rejecting good shape matches solely because they are slightly misaligned in time.
- **Algorithm Mismatch:** Your system uses a DTW-based similarity search, but users viewing the results on a line chart complain that the top matches "don't line up."

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Use Small Multiples.** If you must use line charts, placing the query and potential matches close to each other in a small-multiple layout can help users make a more holistic shape comparison rather than a strict point-by-point alignment.

- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to Horizon Graphs.** Replace the line charts or colorfields with horizon graphs. This will perceptually guide users to prioritize shape similarity over exact temporal alignment.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Educate Users.** When introducing horizon graphs, provide a brief tutorial or annotation explaining how to interpret the layered bands and colors, and explicitly state that the goal is to compare overall shapes, even if they are stretched or shifted.

## Guidance
Avoid using horizon graphs when the goal is to compare the shape of time series patterns regardless of their amplitude (height) or vertical offset (baseline). Use line charts or colorfields instead.

## Why
Horizon graphs use discrete, colored bands to represent value ranges. A small change in a pattern's amplitude or a slight vertical shift can cause parts of the signal to cross a band boundary, resulting in a dramatic change of color. Viewers perceive this color change as a significant difference, making them judge the patterns as dissimilar even when their underlying shapes are identical. This makes horizon graphs poorly suited for tasks where data has been z-normalized or where amplitude/offset should be ignored.

## When it applies
- When comparing z-normalized time series.
- When the analysis task requires finding patterns of a similar shape, irrespective of their scale or absolute value (e.g., finding similar stock price trends, not similar prices).
- When presenting results from a similarity search algorithm that is invariant to amplitude and offset.

## Exceptions
- When the absolute value of the time series and its relationship to a baseline (often zero) is a critical part of the comparison. The color bands in a horizon graph are effective at highlighting when a signal crosses these important thresholds.

## Trade-offs
- While line charts and colorfields are better for amplitude-invariant comparison, they may be less effective at highlighting temporal warping (local time shifts) compared to horizon graphs. You are trading off sensitivity to one type of invariance for another.

## Signs of Trouble
- **False Negatives:** Users fail to identify patterns with identical shapes but different amplitudes as being similar.
- **Color Distraction:** Users focus on differences in color bands between two horizon graphs rather than comparing the layered shapes.
- **Normalization Confusion:** When viewing z-normalized data in a horizon graph, users complain that the results "don't make sense" because the visualization introduces perceptual differences that were removed from the data.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Reduce Bands.** If you must use a horizon graph, using fewer bands (e.g., just one for positive and one for negative) can reduce the number of boundaries a shape can cross, mitigating the issue. However, this also reduces the information density of the chart.

- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to Line Charts.** Replace the horizon graphs with standard line charts. Line charts encode value continuously using position, which allows viewers to more easily compare relative shapes and ignore absolute amplitude.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Provide an Alternative View.** Offer users a toggle to switch between a horizon graph view (good for time-warping) and a line chart view (good for amplitude-invariance), allowing them to use the best representation for their immediate question.

## Guidance
For tasks requiring rapid visual comparison of many time series, consider using colorfields (1D heatmaps). They can enable faster performance than line charts or horizon graphs.

## Why
Experiments on time series similarity perception found that participants were able to make judgments fastest when using colorfields. This suggests that encoding values as color strips allows for quick, high-level "gestalt" comparisons that are faster than tracing the shapes of line charts or decoding the layers of horizon graphs.

## When it applies
- When performance and speed are critical, such as in an interactive system where a user must quickly review dozens or hundreds of potential pattern matches.
- For overview tasks where identifying general patterns of similarity is more important than analyzing fine-grained details of the signal.

## Exceptions
- This speed advantage may not apply if the similarity logic is complex. Colorfields are perceptually sensitive to temporal warping (stretching/shifting) and are less suitable for that type of comparison.
- If precise value comparison is needed, the continuous position encoding of a line chart is superior to the binned color encoding of a colorfield.

## Trade-offs
- **Speed vs. Detail:** Colorfields are faster for overviews but sacrifice the fine-grained detail and precise shape representation available in a line chart.
- **Speed vs. Invariance:** While potentially faster, colorfields are not a good choice for similarity that needs to be invariant to temporal warping. You may be trading appropriate perceptual logic for speed.

## Signs of Trouble
- **Detail Loss:** Users complain they cannot see the exact shape or small fluctuations of the signal in the colorfield view.
- **Warping Sensitivity:** Users using a colorfield are incorrectly rejecting patterns that are good matches but are slightly stretched or compressed in time.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Increase Vertical Space.** Ensure the colorfield strips have sufficient height so that color variations are clearly visible.

- **Moderate Redesign: Provide a Detail-on-Demand View.** Use colorfields in a gallery view for rapid scanning and selection, but allow the user to click or hover to see a more detailed line chart of the selected item and its closest matches.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Test with Your Task.** Since performance is task-dependent, run a small, informal test with your users and data to confirm that colorfields provide a speed benefit for your specific similarity criteria before committing to them in a production system.
