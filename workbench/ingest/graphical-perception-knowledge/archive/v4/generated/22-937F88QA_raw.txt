## Guidance

For tasks that require viewers to estimate or look up specific quantitative values on a continuous map, use a colormap with maximum hue variation, such as a rainbow or spectral scheme.

## Why

Colormaps that vary non-monotonically along multiple perceptual channels (like rainbow schemes with their wide hue range) are best at counteracting simultaneous contrast illusions. These visual illusions are a primary cause of error when judging the value of a colored region based on its surroundings. Mixing in a monotonic luminance ramp (as in spiral colormaps like `cubehelix`) reduces the dynamic hue range and diminishes estimation accuracy.

## When it applies

-   When the primary task for a continuous quantitative map (like a weather map or scientific visualization) is to read the specific value at a given point.
-   This guideline applies regardless of the data's spatial complexity (i.e., it works for both simple, smooth maps and complex, detailed maps).

## Exceptions

-   If the task also involves identifying geometric shapes or fine patterns, a rainbow colormap is a poor choice as it can obscure these features.
-   If accessibility for users with color vision deficiency is a primary concern, the red-green components of a standard rainbow map are highly problematic.

## Trade-offs

-   **Pro:** Significantly improves accuracy for quantity estimation tasks.
-   **Con:** Can severely impair the perception of fine-grained patterns and gradients. It also creates significant accessibility barriers for users with color vision deficiency.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Inaccurate Readings:** Users consistently mis-estimate values, especially when comparing distant points on the map that have the same value but appear different due to their surroundings.
-   **User Complaints:** Viewers report that it's "hard to tell the exact value" or that colors in different areas look the same even when the legend shows they should be different.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix:** If currently using a single-hue sequential or spiral (`cubehelix`) colormap, switch to a multi-hue scheme. This will immediately increase the hue variation and likely improve estimation accuracy.

-   **Moderate Approach:** Switch to a fully saturated, multi-hue colormap like `spectral` (e.g., from ColorBrewer) or `rainbow`. The study found `rainbow` was the most accurate, followed by `spectral`.

-   **Comprehensive Approach:** In a system where multiple tasks are possible, provide the user with a choice of colormaps labeled by their intended task. For example, offer a "Quantitative (Rainbow)" option for value lookups and a "Patterns (Diverging)" option for shape analysis.

## Guidance

To help viewers perceive and match longitudinal patterns in continuous maps with high spatial frequency (i.e., complex, fine-grained data), use a diverging colormap with equidistant luminance steps.

## Why

Diverging ramps that are dark at the ends and bright in the middle provide "maximal perceptual resolution" by creating strong luminance contrast. This controlled variation in lightness enables the visual system to resolve fine-grained, high-frequency patterns more easily, making shapes and structures pop. In contrast, colormaps without this structured luminance profile (like rainbow or single-hue sequential) can obscure these details.

## When it applies

-   When the primary task is to identify shapes, trends, or match patterns (e.g., identifying tissue aberrations in a medical scan or geologic formations).
-   This is most critical in datasets with significant small-scale variation (high spatial frequency). For simple, low-frequency data, the choice of colormap has less impact on pattern perception.

## Exceptions

-   If the primary task is to accurately estimate specific quantitative values, this type of colormap performs poorly compared to hue-varying schemes like rainbow.
-   If the data does not have a meaningful midpoint, a diverging colormap may be semantically inappropriate.

## Trade-offs

-   **Pro:** Optimizes for pattern perception and shape recognition in complex data.
-   **Con:** Sacrifices accuracy for quantity estimation (value look-up) tasks.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Missed Features:** Users fail to identify or match fine-grained patterns or structures in the visualization.
-   **"Muddy" Appearance:** The map looks flat or muddled, with important details washed out. This is a common symptom when using rainbow or single-hue sequential colormaps on complex data.
-   **False Boundaries:** A rainbow colormap creates artificial edges (especially around yellow and cyan) that distract from the true data patterns.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix:** If you are using a rainbow or single-hue colormap for a pattern-matching task on a complex map, switch to any standard diverging colormap, such as Blue-Red. This will likely provide an immediate improvement.

-   **Moderate Redesign:** Choose a well-designed diverging colormap from a tool like ColorBrewer (e.g., `Spectral`, `RdBu`) or use the `coolwarm` scheme. The study found `coolwarm` and `spectral` were the top performers for this task.

-   **Comprehensive Approach:** Ensure the chosen diverging colormap has a non-monotonic, V-shaped luminance profile (dark at the ends, bright in the middle). Use tools that allow you to inspect the luminance profile of your colormap to verify this property.

## Guidance

Do not use rainbow, single-hue sequential, or spiral (e.g., `cubehelix`) colormaps when the task is to identify or match fine-grained patterns in spatially complex data.

## Why

These colormaps performed no better than a simple greyscale ramp for perceiving patterns in data with high spatial frequency. Their luminance profiles do not provide the necessary perceptual resolution to distinguish fine structures. The uncontrolled luminance of a rainbow map creates visual noise and false boundaries, while the monotonic luminance of sequential and spiral maps lacks the contrast needed to make complex shapes stand out.

## When it applies

-   When analyzing continuous maps with a high degree of small-scale variation (high spatial frequency).
-   When the viewer's goal is to see shapes, trends, or features within the data.

## Exceptions

-   If the primary task is *only* quantity estimation (value lookup), a rainbow colormap is a strong choice, even with complex data.
-   For simple, low-frequency data, the negative impact of these colormaps is less pronounced, although they still do not offer an advantage for pattern perception.

## Trade-offs

-   By avoiding these colormaps, you are prioritizing clarity for pattern perception. This comes at the cost of the high accuracy that a rainbow map could provide for pure quantity estimation tasks.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **"Can't see the forest for the trees":** The colormap creates so much visual noise that underlying structures are hidden. The vibrant colors of a rainbow map can be particularly distracting.
-   **False Boundaries:** The sharp hue shifts in a rainbow colormap (especially around yellow and cyan) create artificial lines and regions that do not exist in the data, misleading the viewer.
-   **Washed-Out Details:** When using a sequential or spiral map, fine details in complex regions appear to blend together into a single color.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix:** Switch from a rainbow, sequential, or spiral scheme to a standard diverging one. The study found diverging colormaps like `coolwarm` and `spectral` were significantly better for this task.

-   **Moderate Redesign:** Use a tool like ColorBrewer to select a diverging palette (e.g., `RdBu`, `PiYG`, `Spectral`) that is designed for displaying continuous data and has a controlled luminance profile.

-   **Comprehensive Approach:** For any new colormap, analyze its luminance profile. For pattern perception in complex data, you should see a "V" shape, where luminance is high in the middle and low at both ends. Avoid colormaps with flat or monotonically increasing luminance profiles.

## Guidance

For tasks requiring viewers to compare the steepness of gradients in continuous maps with high spatial frequency (complex data), use a colormap with a range of fully saturated hues (like rainbow) or a diverging ramp with varying saturation.

## Why

Increased spatial complexity makes gradients more pronounced, and colormaps with strong hue or saturation variation leverage this effect to make differences in slope more perceptible. In contrast, colormaps that rely primarily on monotonic luminance (like single-hue sequential or spiral ramps) do not provide a significant benefit over simple greyscale for this task.

## When it applies

-   When the task is to judge how quickly a value is changing across a map with fine-grained features (e.g., identifying areas of rapid pressure change on a weather map).
-   This is most effective for complex data (high spatial frequency). For simple data (low spatial frequency), color provides no significant benefit for gradient perception.

## Exceptions

-   For simple data with low spatial frequency, a greyscale map works just as well for perceiving gradients.
-   If the primary task is pattern matching (not gradient perception), a rainbow map should be avoided, as it performs poorly.

## Trade-offs

-   This may conflict with guidelines for pattern perception. While `coolwarm` (diverging) and `rainbow` both performed well for gradient perception, `rainbow` performed poorly for pattern perception. `coolwarm` is a safer bet if both tasks might be performed.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Indistinguishable Slopes:** Users cannot reliably tell which of two areas on a map is steeper or has a faster rate of change.
-   **Near-Chance Performance:** When asked to compare gradients in complex areas, user accuracy is near 50%, indicating they are essentially guessing.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix:** If using a simple sequential (`singlehue`) or spiral (`cubehelix`) colormap for a gradient task, switch to a diverging map like `coolwarm` or even a `rainbow` map. The study found `coolwarm`, `rainbow`, and `blueyellow` all significantly improved performance for this task.

-   **Moderate Redesign:** Choose a colormap that specifically varies saturation or has a wide hue range. The top performers in the study were diverging ramps with chroma variation (`coolwarm`, `blueyellow`) and fully saturated hue ramps (`rainbow`).

-   **Comprehensive Approach:** When designing a visualization system, if gradient perception is a key task, ensure the available colormaps include options with strong hue and saturation variation. Label them appropriately to guide users (e.g., "Best for Gradients").

## Guidance

Rather than reflexively discouraging the use of rainbow colormaps, consider them a reasonable, and sometimes optimal, choice for tasks that require quantitative precision (value lookup, gradient estimation) but not geometric precision (pattern matching).

## Why

Contrary to a common heuristic in visualization, empirical evidence shows that rainbow colormaps can be the most accurate choice for estimating specific values from a continuous map, regardless of data complexity. They also perform well for estimating gradients in complex data. Their well-known weakness lies in obscuring fine-grained geometric patterns and creating false boundaries. The choice of colormap should be task-dependent.

## When it applies

-   When the primary audience (e.g., scientists, meteorologists) needs to perform elementary visual analytic tasks like characterizing distributions, determining ranges, and filtering by value.
-   When the goal is to extract specific numbers from the map ("quantitative precision") rather than understand its overall shape ("geometric precision").

## Exceptions

-   Never use a rainbow colormap if the primary task is identifying fine-grained shapes, features, or patterns. It performs very poorly on this task.
-   It is a poor choice if accessibility for users with color vision deficiency is a priority, as its adjacent reds and greens are indistinguishable for such users.

## Trade-offs

-   You are explicitly prioritizing quantitative accuracy (value lookup) over geometric accuracy (pattern perception).
-   You are deprioritizing accessibility for users with red-green color vision deficiency.
-   Hue-varying (like rainbow) and diverging colormaps support orthogonal tasks; they should be considered complementary choices depending on the goal, not mutually exclusive options where one is always "bad."

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Dogmatic Rejection:** A team dismisses a rainbow colormap out of hand, citing general "best practices" without considering that the primary user task is quantity estimation, where it excels.
-   **Mismatched Tool:** A team uses a diverging colormap for a value-lookup task, resulting in higher user error, because they were told to "never use rainbow."

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Approach:** For a quantitative lookup task, test whether a rainbow colormap improves user accuracy over the existing "perceptually uniform" sequential or diverging map.

-   **Moderate Approach:** In a tool or system, provide users with a choice of colormaps, but label them by their intended task. For example, offer a "Quantitative View (Rainbow)" option and a "Structural View (Diverging)" option.

-   **Comprehensive Approach:** When selecting a default colormap, consciously decide which task is most important. If quantity estimation is key, `rainbow` is a valid choice. If pattern perception is key, a diverging map like `coolwarm` is better. If tasks are mixed, a "safer" option like `coolwarm` may be preferable, as it performed well on two of the three tasks tested (gradient and pattern perception).
