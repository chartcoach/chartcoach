## Guidance

Do not assume that a more familiar chart type will be read faster by viewers. While familiarity reduces learning time, it does not guarantee quicker task completion.

## Why

Research on part-to-whole charts found that viewers spent significantly *more* time looking at familiar charts (like pie charts and stacked bars) than at novel, unfamiliar circular charts when performing the same estimation task. The authors speculate this could be due to factors like aesthetic engagement or a different cognitive process. This finding challenges the common assumption that familiarity always equates to faster performance.

## When it applies

- When choosing between a conventional, familiar chart and a less common but potentially more efficient alternative.
- When evaluating the effectiveness of a visualization, consider both accuracy and completion time as separate metrics, as they may not be correlated.

## Exceptions

- **Immediate Recognition is Critical:** For tasks requiring an instantaneous "at-a-glance" read with zero learning time (e.g., a KPI on a real-time dashboard, an icon on a sign), familiarity is paramount. The cognitive cost of interpreting a novel form outweighs any other performance benefit.
- **Untrained, Broad Audience:** When communicating to a wide, public audience, the risk of misinterpretation with a novel chart is high. Sticking to familiar conventions is often the safest and most effective choice for mass communication.

## Trade-offs

- **Efficiency vs. Accessibility:** Choosing a less familiar chart may improve performance (speed, accuracy) for dedicated viewers but can alienate or confuse a general audience. You are often trading broad, instant accessibility for expert efficiency.
- **Engagement vs. Speed:** A longer viewing time isn't always negative. It might indicate higher engagement or deeper processing. However, if speed is the primary goal, this is a direct trade-off.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Dogmatic Chart Choice:** Your team consistently defaults to a "standard" chart (e.g., "we always use pie charts for this") without considering alternatives or evaluating if it's the best choice for the task and data.
- **Assumptions Over Evidence:** Design decisions are justified with "it's what people are used to" without any evidence that this familiarity leads to better outcomes for the specific goal.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Reframe the Discussion.** When proposing a less familiar chart, shift the justification from novelty to performance. For example, say "This version is less common, but studies suggest it can be read more accurately." This frames the choice around effectiveness, not just convention.

- **Moderate Approach: Test Your Assumptions.** Conduct informal user testing with both a familiar and a novel chart option using your specific data. Ask a few colleagues to perform a simple task with each and time them. The results may surprise you and provide evidence to support a better design choice.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Build a Visual Vocabulary.** Develop a team-wide guide of approved chart types that includes both standard and alternative options. For each chart, provide clear guidance on its strengths, weaknesses, and the specific tasks, data types, and audience goals it best supports. This encourages decisions based on principles and evidence rather than just habit.
