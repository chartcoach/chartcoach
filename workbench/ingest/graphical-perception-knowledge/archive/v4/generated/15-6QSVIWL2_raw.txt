## Guidance

For tasks requiring users to accurately compare or rank quantitative values, encode data using position along a common, aligned scale (e.g., bar charts, dot plots). This is perceptually more accurate than encoding values using length (unaligned bars), angle (pie charts), or area (bubble charts, treemaps).

## Why

Human perception is most accurate at judging differences in position along an aligned axis. Experiments consistently show that using position encoding leads to significantly lower error rates compared to judgments of length, angle, or area. When accuracy is paramount, choosing a perceptually superior encoding is critical.

## When it applies

- When the primary task is to compare, judge proportions, or rank quantitative values.
- When it's important for the audience to discern small but meaningful differences between data points.
- When creating static or interactive dashboards for a general audience.

## Exceptions

- When the primary task is not comparison but seeing part-to-whole relationships, a pie chart (angle) might be used, but a bar chart is still often better.
- When showing geographical data on a map, area (e.g., in a choropleth) is the inherent encoding, though its perceptual limitations for comparison should be acknowledged.
- When displaying hierarchical data, a treemap (area) may be necessary to show the structure, even though it's poor for precise comparisons.

## Trade-offs

- **Aesthetics vs. Accuracy:** A bubble chart or pie chart might seem more visually engaging to some, but this comes at the cost of perceptual accuracy. A bar chart is often a more "boring" but more effective choice.
- **Space:** A bar chart may take up more screen real estate than a more compact representation like a treemap, but it provides greater clarity for comparison.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Inaccurate Takeaways:** Users misjudge which slice, bubble, or area is larger.
- **High Cognitive Load:** Viewers have to carefully study the chart and its legend to make simple comparisons.
- **"Which is bigger?" Questions:** If users frequently ask for clarification on the relative size of marks, it's a sign the encoding is not effective.
- **Chart Type Mismatch:** Using a pie chart, bubble chart, or treemap when the main goal is to compare values across categories.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** If you must use a pie or bubble chart, add direct data labels (e.g., "42%") to each mark. This provides an "escape hatch" for the user, allowing them to read the exact values instead of relying on flawed perceptual judgments of angle or area.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Bar Chart.** The most effective improvement is to change the chart type. Convert the pie chart, bubble chart, or treemap into a simple bar chart. This aligns all values to a common baseline, making comparisons intuitive and accurate.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Use a Dot Plot.** For a clean, minimalist design that maximizes the data-ink ratio while retaining perceptual accuracy, consider a dot plot. Like a bar chart, it uses position on a common scale but with less visual clutter.

## Guidance

When comparing parts of a whole where a position-based chart (like a bar chart) is not an option, encoding values as angles (e.g., in a pie or donut chart) is more accurate than encoding them as 2D area (e.g., in a bubble chart or treemap).

## Why

This is a "lesser of two evils" principle. The human visual system is poor at making precise, quantitative judgments of both 2D area and angle. However, research shows that people are slightly better at judging proportional differences in angles than they are in areas. This results in lower average error rates for pie charts compared to bubble charts in comparison tasks.

## When it applies

- When you are constrained to use a chart that does not rely on a common positional scale (e.g., due to client request, style guide, or specific part-to-whole emphasis).
- When choosing between a pie chart and a bubble chart to represent proportional data.

## Exceptions

- **The primary guideline is to always prefer position (bar chart, dot plot) for comparison.** This guidance only applies in the specific scenario where that is not possible.
- If the visualization's goal is to show a massive number of data points where individual comparison is impossible (e.g., a "bubble-scape"), area may be used for general texture and density.
- If the visualization needs to represent a hierarchy, a treemap (area) is a standard choice, even though it's poor for precise comparisons between non-adjacent cells.

## Trade-offs

- **Clarity for Novelty:** You are choosing a slightly better but still perceptually weak encoding. The main trade-off is sacrificing the high accuracy of a bar chart for a circular format that might be perceived as more novel but is harder to read.
- **Part-to-Whole vs. Comparison:** Pie charts emphasize the "part-of-a-whole" relationship but make comparisons between slices difficult. A bar chart makes comparisons easy but the whole is less immediately obvious.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Gross Misinterpretations:** Users are making significant errors when comparing the size of bubbles or treemap cells.
- **Lack of Confidence:** Users express uncertainty or frustration when asked to compare values in an area-based chart.
- **Inability to Compare:** The visualization contains many different-sized areas, and it's impossible for a viewer to confidently rank them by size.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Switch from Area to Angle.** If you are using a bubble chart to show the composition of a small number of categories (e.g., < 7), convert it to a pie or donut chart. The comparison will be slightly easier for viewers.
- **Moderate approach: Use a Labeled Donut Chart.** A donut chart functions like a pie chart but opens up the center for a summary value or label. Adding direct percentage labels to each arc further mitigates the perceptual difficulty.
- **Comprehensive approach: Re-evaluate and Use a Bar Chart.** The best solution is to revisit the decision to avoid a position-based chart. In almost all cases, a simple sorted bar chart will communicate proportional data more effectively and accurately than a pie, donut, bubble, or treemap chart.

## Guidance

When designing treemaps or other charts where viewers must compare rectangular areas, avoid making the shapes perfectly square (1:1 aspect ratio). Comparisons between slightly non-square rectangles can lead to more accurate judgments than comparisons between perfect squares.

## Why

This counter-intuitive finding suggests that viewers may use a mental shortcut when judging 2D area, relying on 1D length comparisons of the sides as a proxy. When comparing two squares, this heuristic is less effective because all sides are equal. When comparing two non-square rectangles, the differences in side lengths provide additional cues that can aid in estimating the difference in area. Therefore, layout algorithms that strictly enforce "squarification" may inadvertently hinder perceptual accuracy.

## When it applies

- When designing or choosing an algorithm for a treemap where a key user task is to compare the relative sizes of different cells.
- When creating any visualization that uses the area of rectangles to encode quantitative data for comparison.

## Exceptions

- If the primary goal of the treemap is compact space-filling, showing hierarchy, or creating an aesthetically "tidy" appearance, and precise size comparison is a secondary goal, then a squarified layout may be acceptable.
- This finding is specific to comparison. If the task is simply to identify the largest or smallest rectangle, the effect may be less pronounced.

## Trade-offs

- **Aesthetics vs. Perceptual Accuracy:** A squarified treemap often looks more organized and balanced. Allowing for more varied aspect ratios may appear messier but can improve the chart's effectiveness for comparison tasks.
- **Algorithm Complexity:** Squarified treemap algorithms are standard in many tools. Using an alternative that allows for more varied aspect ratios may require custom implementation or different tooling.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Inaccurate Area Judgments:** You observe that users are making significant errors when asked to estimate the proportional difference between two cells in a squarified treemap.
- **Over-reliance on Labels:** Users ignore the visual areas entirely and only read the text labels to make comparisons, defeating the purpose of the visualization.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Acknowledge the Limitation.** If you must use a standard squarified treemap, be aware of this perceptual pitfall. Ensure that direct value labels are present and legible so users are not forced to rely solely on area judgment for important comparisons.
- **Moderate Approach: Adjust Algorithm Parameters.** If the tool or library you are using allows, adjust the treemap layout algorithm's parameters to relax the squarification constraint. Prioritize a layout that produces more varied aspect ratios over one that produces perfect squares.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Choose an Alternative Chart Type.** If precise comparison is the most critical task, a treemap is likely the wrong chart type. Convert the data to a bar chart or a dot plot, which use the much more accurate encoding of position on a common scale. This fundamentally solves the perceptual problem instead of just mitigating it.
