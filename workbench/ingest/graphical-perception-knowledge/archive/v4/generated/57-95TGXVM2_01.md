## Guidance

For ordinal data (e.g., "low," "medium," "high") or binned quantitative data, use a sequential color scale that varies primarily in saturation or lightness. Avoid using a multi-hue "rainbow" scale.

## Why

The human brain does not perceive the colors of the rainbow (red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo, violet) as having a natural, intuitive order. This makes it difficult for viewers to rank values or judge the magnitude of differences. In contrast, a single-hue scale that varies from light to dark (e.g., light blue to dark blue) has a clear perceptual order that is easy to interpret. This makes comparisons and ranking tasks faster and more accurate. Furthermore, rainbow scales can introduce accessibility issues for colorblind users and create misleading visual artifacts where none exist in the data.

## When it applies

- When encoding any data that has a natural order, including:
  - Ordinal categories (e.g., "small", "medium", "large"; "agree", "neutral", "disagree").
  - Binned quantitative data (e.g., 0-10, 11-20, 21-30).
- When using choropleth maps, heatmaps, or any chart where color represents ordered magnitude.

## Exceptions

- For **nominal** (unordered) categorical data, using distinct hues (e.g., blue, orange, green) is appropriate because there is no inherent order to convey.
- Some specialized multi-hue sequential scales (like Viridis or Magma) are carefully designed to be perceptually uniform and perform well, but these are exceptions to the general rule against arbitrary rainbow scales.

## Trade-offs

- **Visual Appeal:** Rainbow palettes are often perceived as vibrant and engaging, while a single-hue palette might seem more muted. This is a trade-off between aesthetic appeal and perceptual accuracy.
- **Discriminability:** While a single-hue scale is better for ordering, a multi-hue scale might make it easier to distinguish between many different levels, though this comes at the cost of interpretability.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Rainbow Legend:** The color legend shows a spectrum of colors like red, yellow, green, and blue to represent a continuous or ordered variable.
- **No Natural Order:** It's impossible to tell if "green" is greater or less than "yellow" without constantly referring back to the legend.
- **False Boundaries:** Sharp transitions between colors (e.g., from yellow to green) can create the illusion of significant shifts in the data where none exist.
- **Colorblind Inaccessibility:** Using a colorblindness simulator reveals that different colors in the scale become indistinguishable (e.g., red and green appear as the same shade).

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Switch to Grayscale.** As a quick test or solution, convert your rainbow scale to a simple grayscale. This immediately enforces a perceptual order from light to dark and reveals if the rainbow was masking or creating patterns.

- **Moderate Redesign: Use a Single-Hue Sequential Scale.** Choose a single color (e.g., blue) and create a palette that varies from a very light shade of that color to a very dark one. Most visualization tools have built-in options for this (e.g., "Blues," "Greens").

- **Comprehensive Approach: Adopt a Perceptually Uniform Colormap.** Use a professionally designed multi-hue sequential colormap like Viridis, Plasma, or Cividis. These palettes are engineered to be:
  1.  **Perceptually Ordered:** They increase in lightness, so the order is clear.
  2.  **Perceptually Uniform:** A change in the data corresponds to an equivalent perceptual change in color.
  3.  **Colorblind-Safe:** They are readable by people with common forms of color vision deficiency.
