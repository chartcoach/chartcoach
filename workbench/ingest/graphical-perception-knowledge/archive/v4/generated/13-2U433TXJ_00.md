## Guidance
When choosing a visualization for comparing time series patterns, select a technique that aligns with the specific type of similarity you want to emphasize. The choice of visualization (e.g., line chart, horizon graph, colorfield) directly influences how viewers perceive similarity.

## Why
Human perception of similarity is not absolute; it is shaped by the visual encoding. A line chart, horizon graph, and colorfield can lead a viewer to different conclusions about which two time series are "most similar." This is because each visualization emphasizes or de-emphasizes different signal characteristics, such as local time shifts (warping), amplitude, or vertical offset. Mismatching the visualization and the intended similarity logic can lead to confusion, incorrect interpretations, and a loss of trust in automated similarity search results.

## When it applies
- When designing systems for visual similarity search or clustering of time series data.
- When presenting the results of an automated similarity search algorithm to an expert for validation.
- When choosing a chart type to help users compare multiple temporal patterns.

## Exceptions
- If the task is simple value lookup or trend identification for a single series, the choice of visualization has less impact on the perception of similarity to other series.

## Trade-offs
- A visualization optimized for one type of similarity (e.g., shape regardless of time shifts) will likely be suboptimal for another (e.g., shape at exact time points). There is no single "best" visualization for all similarity tasks.
- More complex visualizations like horizon graphs may better match certain similarity logics but can have a steeper learning curve and slower task completion times compared to standard line charts.

## Signs of Trouble
- **User-Machine Disagreement:** Users consistently disagree with the "top match" provided by an automated similarity search algorithm.
- **Inconsistent Judgments:** Different users looking at the same chart make wildly different similarity judgments, indicating high subjectivity.
- **Verbal Friction:** Users describe patterns as similar in ways that contradict the visualization's emphasis (e.g., saying "the shapes are the same" while the chart's colors for them are completely different).

## How to Improve
- **Quick: Clarify the Goal.** Before choosing a chart, explicitly define what makes two patterns similar for your task. Should time shifts be ignored? Is amplitude important? Use this definition to guide your choice.

- **Moderate: Match Vis to Logic.** Choose a visualization that is known to align with your desired similarity logic.
  - For similarity that **ignores local time shifts** (like Dynamic Time Warping), consider **horizon graphs**.
  - For similarity that **ignores amplitude/offset** (like z-normalization), use **line charts** or **colorfields**.

- **Comprehensive: System-Wide Alignment.** If using an automated similarity algorithm, ensure the visualization presented to the user has the same invariance properties as the algorithm. For example, if the algorithm is invariant to time-warping (uses DTW), present the results using a horizon graph, which perceptually favors this type of similarity.
