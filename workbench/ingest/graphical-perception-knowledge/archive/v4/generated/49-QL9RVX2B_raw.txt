## Guidance

For simple part-to-whole comparisons with a small number of categories (e.g., 3-7), use a pie chart or a bar chart instead of a single-level treemap.

## Why

Research shows that when estimating the proportion of a category, viewers make significantly larger errors when reading from a single-level treemap compared to a pie chart. The area judgments required for treemaps, especially with varying rectangle aspect ratios, are less precise than the angle and area judgments in pie charts for this specific task.

## When it applies

- When your primary task is for viewers to understand, estimate, or compare the proportions of a few categories that make up a whole.
- When you are visualizing non-hierarchical data.
- When the number of categories is low (e.g., under 8).

## Exceptions

- **Hierarchical Data:** This guideline does **not** apply when visualizing hierarchical (nested) data. Treemaps are specifically designed for showing multiple levels of a hierarchy within a whole, a task for which pie charts are completely unsuitable.
- **High Cardinality:** When you have a very large number of categories, a treemap can be more effective at showing the general distribution and identifying the largest components, even if individual value estimation is poor.

## Trade-offs

- **Space Efficiency:** A treemap can be more space-efficient than a pie chart or bar chart, especially as the number of categories increases. However, this efficiency comes at the cost of perceptual accuracy for individual comparisons.
- **Familiarity:** Pie charts and bar charts are more familiar to a general audience than treemaps, potentially reducing cognitive load.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Misleading Proportions:** Viewers misjudge the relative sizes of the rectangles in the treemap, especially when comparing a tall, thin rectangle to a wide, short one.
- **Inaccurate Takeaways:** When asked to estimate percentages from a treemap, viewer answers have a high error rate.
- **Viewer Hesitation:** Viewers take a long time to answer questions about the proportions shown in a treemap.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** If you must use a treemap for non-hierarchical data, add direct labels with percentages or absolute values to each rectangle. This provides an "escape hatch" for viewers, allowing them to read the exact values instead of relying on less accurate perceptual judgments of area.

- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Stacked Bar Chart.** A 100% stacked bar chart maintains the part-to-whole concept and uses length, which is easier to compare for the segments at either end of the bar.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Switch to a Simple Bar Chart.** If the primary task is to compare the categories, a standard bar chart is the most perceptually accurate option. All bars share a common baseline (zero), making length comparisons straightforward and precise. You lose the explicit visual representation of the "whole," but gain significant accuracy in comparison.

## Guidance

When performance (speed and accuracy) is a top priority for a data-literate audience, consider using novel circular area charts (such as a "circular slice" chart) as a high-performance alternative to a traditional pie chart.

## Why

An experimental study found that a novel circular chart, where values were encoded by the area of sliding circles, was read both faster and with lower error than a standard pie chart for part-to-whole comparison tasks. This challenges the assumption that familiar charts are always the most effective and suggests that alternative designs can be perceptually superior.

## When it applies

- The primary goal is maximizing the speed and accuracy of part-to-whole estimations.
- The visualization is intended for a data-literate audience (e.g., an internal analytics team) that is open to learning new chart types.
- The visualization will be used repeatedly, justifying the initial learning cost.

## Exceptions

- **General Audience:** For a broad, general audience or a one-off visualization, the universal recognizability of a pie chart is often more valuable than a marginal performance gain from a novel chart. The cognitive effort required to learn the new form can cause confusion and negate any benefits.
- **Simplicity is Key:** If the goal is immediate, effortless communication with no room for ambiguity, stick with a highly conventional chart like a bar chart or pie chart.

## Trade-offs

- **Familiarity vs. Performance:** You trade the instant recognizability and low learning curve of a pie chart for potential gains in reader speed and accuracy.
- **Cognitive Load:** A novel chart type increases the initial cognitive load for the viewer as they must first understand how to read it before they can interpret the data it shows.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Viewer Confusion:** Viewers express confusion, asking "What does this chart mean?" or "How do I read this?"
- **Incorrect Interpretation:** Viewers misinterpret the visual encoding, leading to flawed takeaways from the data.
- **Design Distraction:** The novelty of the chart design distracts from the data itself; viewers focus on the "cool chart" rather than the message.

## How to Improve

- **Minimal: Annotate Clearly.** If you use a novel chart, provide a clear title and a brief, explicit annotation explaining the encoding. For example: "Each segment's area represents its share of the total."

- **Moderate: Gradual Introduction.** Introduce the new chart type in a low-stakes environment first. You could also pair it with a more familiar chart (like a bar chart) showing the same data to help viewers learn the new form.

- **Comprehensive: Prioritize Convention for Broad Audiences.** If your audience is general or the context requires zero learning curve, stick with a more conventional and perceptually effective chart type, such as a bar chart, which is both familiar and highly accurate for comparison tasks.

## Guidance

Do not assume that a more familiar chart type will be read faster by viewers. While familiarity reduces learning time, it does not guarantee quicker task completion.

## Why

Research on part-to-whole charts found that viewers spent significantly *more* time looking at familiar charts (like pie charts and stacked bars) than at novel, unfamiliar circular charts when performing the same estimation task. The authors speculate this could be due to factors like aesthetic engagement or a different cognitive process. This finding challenges the common assumption that familiarity always equates to faster performance.

## When it applies

- When choosing between a conventional, familiar chart and a less common but potentially more efficient alternative.
- When evaluating the effectiveness of a visualization, consider both accuracy and completion time as separate metrics, as they may not be correlated.

## Exceptions

- **Immediate Recognition is Critical:** For tasks requiring an instantaneous "at-a-glance" read with zero learning time (e.g., a KPI on a real-time dashboard, an icon on a sign), familiarity is paramount. The cognitive cost of interpreting a novel form outweighs any other performance benefit.
- **Untrained, Broad Audience:** When communicating to a wide, public audience, the risk of misinterpretation with a novel chart is high. Sticking to familiar conventions is often the safest and most effective choice for mass communication.

## Trade-offs

- **Efficiency vs. Accessibility:** Choosing a less familiar chart may improve performance (speed, accuracy) for dedicated viewers but can alienate or confuse a general audience. You are often trading broad, instant accessibility for expert efficiency.
- **Engagement vs. Speed:** A longer viewing time isn't always negative. It might indicate higher engagement or deeper processing. However, if speed is the primary goal, this is a direct trade-off.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Dogmatic Chart Choice:** Your team consistently defaults to a "standard" chart (e.g., "we always use pie charts for this") without considering alternatives or evaluating if it's the best choice for the task and data.
- **Assumptions Over Evidence:** Design decisions are justified with "it's what people are used to" without any evidence that this familiarity leads to better outcomes for the specific goal.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Reframe the Discussion.** When proposing a less familiar chart, shift the justification from novelty to performance. For example, say "This version is less common, but studies suggest it can be read more accurately." This frames the choice around effectiveness, not just convention.

- **Moderate Approach: Test Your Assumptions.** Conduct informal user testing with both a familiar and a novel chart option using your specific data. Ask a few colleagues to perform a simple task with each and time them. The results may surprise you and provide evidence to support a better design choice.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Build a Visual Vocabulary.** Develop a team-wide guide of approved chart types that includes both standard and alternative options. For each chart, provide clear guidance on its strengths, weaknesses, and the specific tasks, data types, and audience goals it best supports. This encourages decisions based on principles and evidence rather than just habit.
