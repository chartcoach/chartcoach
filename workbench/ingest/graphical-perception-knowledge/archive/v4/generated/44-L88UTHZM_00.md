## Guidance

For comparing or ranking quantitative values, encode data using position along a common, aligned scale (like in a bar chart or dot plot). This is more perceptually accurate than using length, angle, area, or color saturation.

## Why

The human visual system is most accurate at judging differences in position along a common scale. Other visual channels, such as angle (in pie charts), area (in bubble charts), or color saturation (in heatmaps), lead to larger estimation errors and less accurate comparisons. This principle, established by Cleveland and McGill, is a cornerstone of effective visualization design.

## When it applies

- When the primary task is to enable users to accurately compare, rank, or look up quantitative values.
- When it is important for viewers to notice subtle but meaningful differences between data points.
- This guidance applies to most common chart types intended for quantitative comparison.

## Exceptions

- When the goal is to show a general trend or part-to-whole relationship where exact comparisons are less important, other encodings might be acceptable (e.g., area in treemaps for hierarchical composition).
- When showing correlation in a scatterplot, two position channels are used to reveal a pattern, not just for simple comparison along one axis.
- When visualizing data on a map, position is already used to encode geography, so another channel (like color or size) must be used for the quantitative value.

## Trade-offs

- Charts that rely heavily on position (like bar charts or dot plots) can sometimes take up more space than area-based charts (like treemaps).
- Some audiences may find a pie chart more intuitively familiar for a simple part-to-whole comparison, even if it is less perceptually accurate.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Inaccurate Judgments:** Viewers consistently misjudge the relative size of values (e.g., a value that is 2x another does not look twice as large).
- **Invisible Differences:** Small but important differences between values are not visible or are hard to discern.
- **Label Dependency:** The chart is unreadable and comparisons are impossible without adding direct data labels to every mark.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Data Labels.** If you must use a less accurate encoding like angle or area, add direct data labels (e.g., "42%") to each mark. This provides an escape hatch, allowing users to read the exact values instead of relying on flawed perceptual estimation.

- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to Aligned Length.** If using unaligned lengths or angles (like in a pie chart), switch to a chart that uses length from a common baseline, such as a stacked bar chart. While not as accurate as a standard bar chart for all comparisons, it's an improvement over angle.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Position on a Common Scale.** Redesign the chart to use the most accurate encoding. Replace a pie chart, donut chart, or bubble chart with a standard bar chart or a dot plot. This directly leverages the most effective visual channel for quantitative comparison.
