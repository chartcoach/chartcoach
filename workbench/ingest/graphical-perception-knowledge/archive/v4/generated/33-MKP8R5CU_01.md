## Guidance

Be aware that the height of bars immediately adjacent to a target bar can introduce a small perceptual bias. A bar surrounded by taller neighbors may be perceived as slightly shorter or lower in rank than it actually is.

## Why

This phenomenon, known as the "neighborhood effect," is a form of visual illusion called "length contrast." The human brain's perceptual system does not judge visual elements in isolation; the immediate visual context influences perception. While research confirms this effect exists in bar charts, its impact is small compared to other biases related to the overall data distribution.

## When it applies

- This subtle bias is present in any bar chart, but is most variable in unsorted charts where bars of very different heights can be placed next to each other.
- It becomes a relevant (though minor) consideration in high-stakes visualizations where extreme precision in judgment is required.

## Exceptions

- For the vast majority of general-purpose visualizations, this effect is small enough to be considered negligible. The paper's authors conclude that "designers do not need to worry too much about neighborhood effects" because they are overshadowed by much larger data-inherent effects.

## Trade-offs

- Actively trying to mitigate this small effect could add unnecessary complexity. For example, increasing the spacing between all bars might slightly reduce the illusion but would also decrease data density and make the chart take up more space. Prioritizing larger issues like overall sorting is more impactful.

## Signs of Trouble

- **High-Stakes Precision:** The chart is used for a critical decision where a perceptual error of 1-2% in rank or value could lead to a wrong conclusion.
- **Surprising Adjacency:** In an unsorted chart, an important bar happens to fall between the two tallest bars, creating a context that maximizes the potential for underestimation.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** In most cases, no action is needed. Simply be aware that this minor bias exists. Prioritize addressing more significant issues like sorting the chart by value.

- **Moderate approach:** If the chart must remain unsorted, consider if grouping the bars by a secondary categorical variable could create more visually homogeneous "neighborhoods," thereby reducing the variability of the effect.

- **Comprehensive approach:** The most effective way to manage neighborhood context is to sort the bar chart by value. This creates a smooth and predictable context for every bar, minimizing the random influence of adjacent bar heights.
