## Guidance

When encoding a third variable in a scatterplot using size (a bubble chart) or color lightness, recognize that viewers' perception of the average (mean) position will be systematically biased towards the larger or darker marks. This is known as the "weighted average illusion."

## Why

The human visual system automatically gives more perceptual "weight" to more salient marks. Larger and darker objects are more salient, so they disproportionately influence our subconscious judgment of the center of a group of points. This happens even when viewers are trained and consciously know they should treat all points equally. This can lead to significant misinterpretations of the data's central tendency.

## When it applies

- When using scatterplots where a third quantitative variable is encoded using mark size (bubble charts) or color lightness/saturation.
- When a key task for the viewer is to estimate the average position, find the center of the data, or judge whether points are above or below the average.

## Exceptions

- When the explicit goal is to have viewers perceive a **weighted average**, and this is clearly communicated. For example, if a bubble chart of countries uses population for the size encoding, you may actually *want* the viewer to perceive the population-weighted average GDP and life expectancy. If this is the intent, it should be made explicit to the user.

## Trade-offs

- **Information Density vs. Perceptual Accuracy:** Encoding a third variable with size or lightness increases the information density of the chart. The trade-off is that this comes at the cost of reduced accuracy in judging the mean of the two primary (x/y) variables.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Visual Center Mismatch:** The visual "center of gravity" of the points seems to be pulled away from the mathematical mean toward a cluster of large or dark marks.
- **User Misjudgment:** When asked, users incorrectly identify points as being "above average" or "below average" because their perceived mean is skewed.
- **Salience Gradient:** The chart has a clear visual gradient where the largest or darkest marks are concentrated in one area (e.g., the top-right corner).

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add a Reference.** Add explicit reference lines or a point marker to show the true mathematical mean. This gives viewers a concrete anchor to compare against, helping to counteract the perceptual bias.

- **Moderate Redesign: Switch Channels.** The biasing effect is stronger for size than for color lightness. If the task allows, switch from encoding the third variable with size to encoding it with color lightness/saturation to reduce the magnitude of the bias.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Separate the Encodings.** If accurate mean estimation is critical, do not use size or color to encode the third variable in the same chart. Instead, use small multiples to facet the data by the third variable, or use a completely separate chart (like a histogram or bar chart) to show its distribution.

## Guidance

Be aware that when the variable encoded by size or lightness is correlated with the variables on the x and y axes, the bias in perceived mean position is significantly amplified.

## Why

Correlation creates spatial clusters of visually salient marks. For example, in a chart with a positive correlation, all the large or dark marks will group together in the top-right corner. This spatial grouping makes that region even more dominant in your visual field, causing your brain to overweight it more heavily when estimating the average. This can also create the illusion of higher point density in that region, further skewing perception.

## When it applies

- When creating a trivariate scatterplot (e.g., bubble chart) where you know or suspect there is a correlation between the x/y position variables and the third variable encoded by size or color.
- The more highly correlated the data, the more severe the bias will be.

## Exceptions

- There are no known exceptions to this perceptual effect. If the conditions are met (trivariate scatterplot with correlated data), the bias is likely to be amplified. The only recourse is to change the design or add clarifying elements.

## Trade-offs

- You cannot change the correlation in your data. The trade-off is in how you choose to display it. Using a size or lightness encoding for a highly correlated variable is efficient for showing the relationship, but it makes the chart highly susceptible to the weighted average illusion. You must choose between clearly showing the three-way relationship and enabling accurate mean-judgments.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Visual Gradient:** The scatterplot exhibits a clear visual gradient of size or color. For example, points gradually get larger and darker as you move from the bottom-left to the top-right.
- **Clustered Salience:** All the "heavy" looking marks (large or dark) are visibly clustered in one part of the chart.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Annotations and References.** If you must use the chart, add a text annotation that explicitly warns the user about the potential for perceptual bias due to the data's structure. Combine this with explicit reference lines for the true mean.

- **Moderate Redesign: Use Small Multiples.** Instead of encoding the correlated variable with size or color, use it to create small multiples. For example, bin the third variable into "low," "medium," and "high" categories and show three separate scatterplots. This breaks the strong visual gradient and allows for more accurate comparisons within each plot.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Re-evaluate the Task.** If the primary task is to judge the mean, and the data is highly correlated, a trivariate scatterplot is a poor choice. Consider if the user's question can be answered better with a different visualization that doesn't suffer from this specific bias, such as a parallel coordinates plot or a set of linked bar charts.

## Guidance

When adding a third quantitative variable to a scatterplot, and the viewer's task involves judging the mean position, prefer encoding the third variable with **color lightness** (or saturation) instead of **size**.

## Why

Research shows that the "weighted average illusion"—where salient marks disproportionately influence the perceived mean—is much stronger for size than for color lightness. Viewers' mental "weighting" of large marks is heavier than their weighting of dark marks. Therefore, using lightness results in a more accurate perception of the central tendency of the primary x/y variables.

## When it applies

- You are designing a trivariate scatterplot and need to choose a channel for the third quantitative variable.
- A primary task for the user is to judge the central tendency (the average) of the data points.
- You have to choose between a bubble chart (size) and a color-coded scatterplot (lightness/saturation).

## Exceptions

- If the primary task is for viewers to **compare the individual values** of the third variable, not the mean of the x/y variables. Size is a more perceptually accurate channel than color lightness for comparing individual quantitative values (as per Cleveland & McGill's hierarchy). In this case, a bubble chart might be preferable, but you must accept the trade-off of a biased mean perception.

## Trade-offs

- **Accuracy of Mean vs. Accuracy of Third Variable:** By choosing lightness to get a more accurate perceived mean, you sacrifice some precision in the viewer's ability to compare the values of the third variable itself.
- **Occlusion:** A benefit of using lightness over size is that it eliminates the problem of large marks occluding (hiding) smaller ones.
- **Display Variability:** Color lightness can be more sensitive to screen calibration and ambient lighting conditions than size.

## Signs of Trouble

- Your visualization is a bubble chart.
- You know from user research or task analysis that viewers are trying to find the "average," "center," or "typical" point in the data.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Improvement: Switch the Encoding Channel.** Change the visual encoding for the third variable from `size` to a sequential `color` scale (e.g., from light gray to black). This is often a one-line change in visualization code and will immediately reduce the perceptual bias.
