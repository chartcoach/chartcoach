## Guidance

In a multiclass scatterplot, identify classes that are visually "weak"—meaning they are sparse, have low point density, or are heavily intermingled with other classes. Assign these hard-to-see classes colors that have a high luminance contrast against the chart's background.

## Why

Classes that lack spatial coherence are already perceptually difficult to discern as a group. Assigning them a low-contrast color (like light yellow or pale gray on a white background) can render them effectively invisible, leading viewers to overlook them entirely. A high-contrast color makes these weak classes salient, ensuring they are not missed during analysis.

## When it applies

- For multiclass scatterplots on a uniform background (e.g., white, black, or gray).
- When the dataset contains classes of vastly different sizes, densities, or degrees of overlap.
- When it is important that no class, no matter how small or scattered, is overlooked.

## Exceptions

- If the explicit goal is to de-emphasize outliers or minor, unimportant classes, using a lower-contrast color for them (e.g., gray) can be an intentional and effective design choice to direct attention elsewhere.

## Trade-offs

- This approach may require assigning your most salient, high-contrast colors to the "messiest" or smallest data classes. This might conflict with a desire to assign those same salient colors to the most "important" classes for storytelling purposes.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Vanishing Class:** A category listed in the legend seems to be completely missing from the plot, only to be found upon close inspection as a few faint, low-contrast points.
- **Background Camouflage:** Points of a certain color are difficult to spot because they blend in with the chart's background.
- **Uneven Salience:** The largest, densest clusters are assigned the brightest colors, while small, sparse clusters are given faint colors, making the small clusters even harder to notice.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: The Yellow Swap.** If you have a class that is hard to see on a white background, check if it's assigned a color like yellow, light green, or pale cyan. Swap this color with a dark, high-contrast color like a deep blue, purple, or dark red from another, more prominent class.
- **Moderate Approach: Check All Ratios.** Use a contrast checker tool to measure the WCAG contrast ratio of every color in your palette against the background. Reserve the colors with the highest ratios (e.g., > 3:1) for the classes you identify as the sparsest or most heavily overlapped.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Weight Contrast by Separability.** Implement a system that first calculates a "non-separability score" for each class (based on density and overlap). Then, when assigning colors, weight the selection process so that classes with higher non-separability scores are preferentially assigned colors with higher background contrast.
