## Guidance

When designing a multi-class scatterplot, do not be afraid to include additional "distractor" categories, even if a primary task is for the user to compare two specific categories.

## Why

The human visual system's capacity for selective attention allows viewers to focus on specific categories (e.g., "blue" and "orange") and effectively ignore others (e.g., "green"), as long as the categories are perceptually distinct. The presence of additional, irrelevant data does not "distract" or interfere with the ability to perform an aggregate comparison on a target subset. This enables the display of richer contextual information without harming specific sub-tasks.

## When it applies

- When creating a scatterplot with three or more categories, where users might be interested in comparing any given pair of them.
- When deciding whether to filter a dataset down to just two categories or show the broader context of all categories.

## Exceptions

- **Poor Category Distinction:** The guideline assumes the categories are easily distinguishable. If a poor color palette with similar hues is used, viewers will struggle to isolate the groups of interest, and performance will degrade.
- **Extreme Visual Complexity:** Performance can suffer if the chart becomes excessively complex due to a combination of many distractor categories *and* other conflicting visual cues (e.g., varied shapes and sizes).
- **The "Where's Waldo" Problem:** If there are very many categories (e.g., >10-12), it may become cognitively difficult for a user to even find the two categories they wish to compare.

## Trade-offs

- **Context vs. Simplicity:** Showing more categories provides richer context but also increases the total number of objects and colors on the screen, which can make the chart feel more cluttered or intimidating to some viewers.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Indistinguishable Categories:** The colors (or shapes) used for different categories are too similar, making it difficult for viewers to mentally isolate the groups they want to compare.
- **Cognitive Overload:** There are so many categories that the viewer feels overwhelmed and isn't sure where to begin their analysis.
- **Unhelpful Context:** The additional categories shown provide no useful context and only serve to clutter the view.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Ensure a Distinct Palette.** Use a color palette where every color is perceptually distinct from every other color. Tools like `Colorgorical` or `Viz Palette` can help generate or test these.

- **Moderate Approach: Add Interactive Filtering/Highlighting.** Provide an interactive legend that allows users to click on categories to highlight them or fade out all others. This empowers users to reduce clutter themselves and focus on the comparisons they care about.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Re-evaluate the Chart Choice.** If you have many categories (e.g., more than 7-8), a single scatterplot may not be the best choice. Consider alternatives that handle high cardinality better, such as a dot plot faceted by category, a beeswarm plot, or small multiples.
