```md
## Guidance

When using color to represent distinct categories, use an algorithmic approach to adjust the colors to be as perceptually different from each other as possible. The goal is to maximize the minimum perceptual distance (e.g., CIEDE2000 distance) between all pairs of colors in your palette.

## Why

Manually-selected color palettes, even those chosen for aesthetic or semantic reasons, often contain colors that are perceptually too similar. This makes it difficult for viewers to quickly and accurately distinguish between categories, especially for small marks on a chart or for viewers with color vision deficiencies. Algorithmic optimization provides a systematic way to ensure that every category is clearly differentiable from every other category.

## When it applies

- When using color to encode nominal (categorical) data.
- When you have an initial palette (e.g., from brand guidelines or based on metaphors) that needs to be made more effective for data visualization.
- When creating a reusable color palette for a system or dashboard where consistency and clarity are paramount.

## Exceptions

- **Ordered Data:** This guidance is for categorical (nominal) data. For quantitative or ordinal data, you should use a sequential or diverging palette, not a palette of maximally distinct hues.
- **Very Few Colors:** If you are only using two or three very distinct colors (e.g., red, blue, grey), formal optimization may be unnecessary, though still potentially beneficial.

## Trade-offs

- **Aesthetics vs. Distinctness:** A purely optimized palette may not be as aesthetically coordinated as a hand-picked one. The most distinct colors might feel jarring or disconnected when used together.
- **Semantic Meaning vs. Distinctness:** Optimization can shift colors away from their original semantic or brand meaning. For example, a specific "brand blue" might be shifted to a slightly different hue to make it more distinct from a green in the palette. This is a direct trade-off that requires careful consideration (see `preserve-semantic-color` guideline).

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Squint Test:** If you squint at your chart, do different color categories blend together or become indistinguishable?
- **Subtle Neighbors:** Does your palette contain two colors that are very similar in hue, saturation, or lightness (e.g., a light green and a teal, or a navy and a royal blue)?
- **Legend-Chart Back-and-Forth:** Do users have to repeatedly look between the legend and the chart to be sure which category a mark belongs to?
- **Colorblind Simulation Failure:** When viewed through a colorblindness simulator (e.g., for Deuteranopia or Protanopia), do two or more colors become identical?

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Manual Adjustment.** Identify the two most similar colors in your palette and manually adjust one of them to be more different. For example, make one significantly lighter or change its hue. This is less precise but better than nothing.
- **Moderate Approach: Use an Online Optimizer.** Use a tool like Colourmap Hospital or Huemint. Input your starting colors, and let the tool generate an optimized version. This provides a good balance of control and effectiveness.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Integrate Constrained Optimization.** For palettes with strong semantic requirements, use a tool that allows constrained optimization. Fix certain colors (like background or a key brand color) and apply constraints to others (e.g., "only adjust saturation/lightness, not hue") to balance perceptual distance with semantic integrity.
```

```
## Guidance

When algorithmically optimizing a categorical color palette, apply constraints to prevent colors from shifting too far from their original, meaningful values. For example, fix the hue of a color while allowing the optimization algorithm to adjust its saturation and lightness to improve distinctness.

## Why

Unconstrained optimization algorithms prioritize maximizing perceptual distance above all else. This can cause them to radically alter colors, breaking important semantic associations. For example, a "red" category might become orange, or two colors might swap places entirely. This destroys the purpose of using meaningful colors (e.g., brand colors, historical conventions, or metaphors) and can confuse viewers. Constraints provide a necessary balance between perceptual optimization and semantic stability.

## When it applies

- When the initial color palette has established semantic meaning.
- **Brand Colors:** Visualizations that must adhere to a corporate or organizational brand identity.
- **Historical Conventions:** Charts that use colors established by long-standing tradition, such as the color-coded lines of the London Tube Map.
- **Metaphorical Colors:** Palettes where colors are chosen to represent concepts (e.g., red for "danger," green for "safe"; or colors associated with political parties).
- **Logological Association:** Colors associated with a name or term (e.g., using a "cherry" red for a "California" category, as shown in the source paper's seismology case study).

## Exceptions

- **No Pre-existing Meaning:** If you are generating a new palette from scratch with no prior semantic constraints, you can use unconstrained optimization to achieve the maximum possible perceptual distance between colors.

## Trade-offs

- **Optimal Distance vs. Semantic Fidelity:** The primary trade-off is clear: applying constraints will likely result in a lower (less optimal) perceptual distance than an unconstrained optimization. You are sacrificing some degree of differentiability to preserve meaning. The goal is to find a "good enough" level of distinctness while keeping the colors recognizable.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Semantic Mismatch:** An "optimized" palette contains colors that no longer align with their intended meaning. The "blue" brand color has turned purple; the "grass green" is now a "lime green".
- **Color Swap:** Two categories have swapped colors after optimization (e.g., the originally blue series is now green, and the green series is now blue).
- **Loss of Memorability:** A familiar, color-coded system becomes unfamiliar because the colors have changed too much, forcing users to re-learn the associations.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Lock Key Colors.** Identify the 1-2 most important semantic colors in your palette. In an optimization tool, "lock" them so they cannot be changed at all, and allow the algorithm to only adjust the other, less critical colors.
- **Moderate Approach: Constrain Hue.** For all semantic colors, apply a tight constraint on their hue (e.g., allow only a ±5% or ±10° change). This preserves the core color identity (e.g., "blue" stays "blue") while giving the algorithm freedom to adjust saturation and lightness to increase contrast with other colors.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Tiered Constraints.** Apply different levels of constraints based on importance. For example:
    1.  Fix the background and foreground/text colors completely.
    2.  Apply a very tight hue constraint to primary brand colors.
    3.  Apply a looser hue constraint to secondary semantic colors.
    4.  Allow non-semantic colors to be changed freely.
    This provides a sophisticated balance between maintaining identity and improving the palette's overall performance.
```

```
## Guidance

To represent and compare quantitative data, encode values using position along a common, aligned scale. This is the visual encoding used in bar charts, dot plots, and scatter plots. It is more accurately perceived than other visual encodings like length, angle, area, or color saturation.

## Why

Foundational research in graphical perception by Cleveland & McGill established a hierarchy of how accurately humans perform elementary perceptual tasks. Judging position along a common scale is the most accurate task. Tasks like judging length (unaligned bar charts), angle (pie charts), or area (bubble charts) are all demonstrably less accurate, leading to more errors in judgment. Using position ensures that viewers can make the most precise comparisons and estimations.

## When it applies

- The primary goal is for the audience to accurately compare, rank, or look up quantitative values.
- You need to make small, but important, differences between values apparent.
- You are choosing between a bar chart (position), a pie chart (angle), or a bubble chart (area) to show magnitudes.

## Exceptions

- **Showing Part-to-Whole Composition:** While a bar chart is still more accurate, a pie chart's primary strength is emphasizing that the values are parts of a whole. If this is the single most important message and high precision is not required (and you have few slices), a pie chart might be considered.
- **Limited Space & High Cardinality:** When showing a very large number of data points in a small space, area-based charts like treemaps can be more space-efficient than bar charts, at the cost of comparison accuracy.
- **Correlation of Two Variables:** A scatter plot uses two positional scales (X and Y) to show the relationship between two variables, which is optimal for that specific task.

## Trade-offs

- **Space:** Bar charts and dot plots can take up more space (especially vertical or horizontal length) than more compact forms like treemaps.
- **Familiarity/Aesthetics:** In some non-analytical contexts, audiences may be more familiar with or aesthetically prefer other chart forms like pie charts, even if they are less perceptually accurate.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Pie Chart with Many Slices:** It is nearly impossible to accurately compare the sizes of slices in a pie chart, especially if they are close in value or not adjacent.
- **Bubble Chart Comparisons:** Viewers are notoriously bad at accurately comparing the areas of circles. Most people judge by radius or diameter, leading to systematic underestimation of the differences in area.
- **Stacked Bar Chart Comparisons:** In a stacked bar chart, only the bottom-most series of bars rests on a common baseline. It is very difficult to accurately compare the lengths of the segments in the middle or top of the stacks.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** If you must use a less effective chart type like a pie or bubble chart, add direct data labels (the numerical values) to each element. This gives users an "escape hatch" to read the precise value instead of relying on inaccurate perceptual judgment.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch Chart Type.**
    - Replace a pie chart with a bar chart to switch from judging angles to judging position.
    - Replace a bubble chart with a bar chart or a dot plot.
    - "Unstack" a stacked bar chart into a grouped bar chart, so all bars share a common baseline.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Re-evaluate the Core Task.** Step back and confirm the primary goal for the user. If it is indeed comparison, commit to using the most effective chart (bar, dot plot). If the goal is something else (e.g., showing composition as the top priority), ensure the chosen chart form is optimized for that task and its limitations are mitigated (e.g., a pie chart with 3 slices and clear labels).
```

```
  - type: research

    url: https://doi.org/10.1145/22949.22950
    note: "The original paper formalizing the expressiveness and effectiveness of visual encodings, identifying color hue as ideal for nominal data."
---

## Guidance

Use distinct color hues (e.g., red, blue, green) to represent different categories that have no inherent order. This is also known as a qualitative or categorical color palette.

## Why

Color hue is an "identity" channel. According to Jock Mackinlay's expressiveness principles, it is perfectly suited for encoding nominal (categorical) data because it does not imply any sense of order or magnitude. Our visual system is adept at rapidly identifying and grouping objects by hue, making it an effective way to distinguish different categories at a glance.

## When it applies

- When encoding unordered categories in any chart type, such as:
    - Different series in a line or bar chart (e.g., `Product A`, `Product B`).
    - Categories in a scatter plot (e.g., `Species A`, `Species B`).
    - Regions or groups on a map.

## Exceptions

- **Ordered Data:** Do not use a qualitative palette of distinct hues for data that has an inherent order (e.g., `Low`, `Medium`, `High` or a range of numbers). This would fail to communicate the ordered nature of the data. Use a sequential or diverging palette instead.
- **High Cardinality:** It is difficult for humans to reliably distinguish more than about 7-10 different color hues. If you have more categories than this, color alone is not a sufficient encoding. You will need to combine it with other techniques like grouping, highlighting, or using other visual channels.

## Trade-offs

- **Accessibility:** A palette of distinct hues must be carefully chosen to be distinguishable by people with color vision deficiencies. A randomly chosen set of hues will likely fail this test.
- **Semantic Associations:** Hues carry strong cultural and emotional associations (e.g., red for danger/loss, green for success/environment). A purely categorical palette might create unintended meanings.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Implied Order:** A chart uses a sequential color palette (e.g., shades of light blue to dark blue) to encode distinct categories like `North America`, `Europe`, and `Asia`. This incorrectly implies that `Asia` is "more" or "higher" than `Europe`.
- **Indistinguishable Hues:** The palette contains multiple colors that are too similar, such as a blue and a purple, or a red and an orange, making it hard to tell them apart, especially for small marks on the chart.
- **Color Overload:** The chart uses a dozen or more different hues, creating a "rainbow" effect that is visually overwhelming and difficult to decipher.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Use a Pre-built Safe Palette.** Replace your current colors with a pre-vetted, colorblind-safe categorical palette. Tools like ColorBrewer, Viz Palette, or Tableau's built-in palettes are excellent sources.
- **Moderate Approach: Reduce Cardinality.** If you have too many categories to encode with color effectively, try to group them into a smaller number of "super-categories". You can encode the super-categories with distinct hues and then use lightness variations or another channel for the sub-categories. For example, color-code `Fruits` and `Vegetables`, then use shapes for `Apple`, `Banana`, `Carrot`, `Broccoli`.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Create an Optimized Palette.** If you have specific brand or semantic constraints, start with those colors and use an optimization tool (like Huemint or Colourmap Hospital) to generate a full palette of complementary colors that are both semantically appropriate and perceptually distinct.
```

```
title: "Avoid using area or color saturation to encode nominal data"

    note: "In Table 3, summarizes Mackinlay's (1986) expressiveness principles, which mark Area (Ar) and Color Saturation (CS) as inappropriate (X) for nominal (N) data because they imply order."
  - type: research
    ref: Mackinlay, 1986
    url: https://doi.org/10.1145/22949.22950
    note: "The original paper explaining that ordered visual channels (like size and saturation) should not be used for unordered data (nominal) as it violates the principle of expressiveness."
---

## Guidance

Do not use visual channels that imply magnitude or order, such as area, size, or color saturation/lightness, to represent distinct, unordered (nominal) categories.

## Why

Area (the size of a shape) and color saturation (the intensity from gray to a pure hue) are perceived as ordered quantities. A larger shape is interpreted as "more" than a smaller one; a more saturated color is seen as "stronger" or "higher value" than a less saturated one. Using these channels for nominal data, which has no inherent order, creates a false and misleading visual hierarchy. This violates the fundamental "expressiveness principle" of visualization: the visual representation should only express the properties of the data, and not create new, unsupported ones.

## When it applies

- When encoding nominal data (unordered categories), such as `Country`, `Department`, or `Product Type`.
- When choosing between using color hue, shape, or size to differentiate categories.

## Exceptions

- **Highlighting:** You can legitimately use size or saturation to draw attention to a specific category of interest, but this should be a conscious choice for emphasis, not the primary encoding for all categories.
- **Composite Glyphs:** In complex visualizations, size might be used to encode a *second, quantitative* variable associated with a category (e.g., on a map, use color hue for the country and the size of the dot for its population). In this case, size is correctly encoding quantitative data, not the nominal category itself.

## Trade-offs

- Using shape as an alternative can also have issues. Some shapes are more visually salient than others, and using too many different shapes becomes a "key-value" lookup problem that is cognitively demanding.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Misleading Magnitude:** A bar chart uses different shades of the same color (a sequential scheme) to represent different continents. This makes it appear as if one continent is "higher" or "lower" than another on some imaginary scale.
- **False Ranking with Size:** A visualization uses differently sized circles to represent different political parties. This incorrectly implies that parties with larger circles are more "important" or have "more" of some unstated quantity.
- **Bubble Grid for Categories:** A chart shows categories as a grid of bubbles of different sizes. This is an incorrect application of a bubble chart, which should use size to encode a quantitative value.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Switch to Hue.** If you are using saturation (shades of one color), replace the palette with a categorical one using distinct hues (e.g., blue, green, orange).
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to Shape.** If you are using size, change the encoding to use different shapes (e.g., circles, squares, triangles) of the *same size*. This is effective for a small number of categories.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Use a Combination.** For a larger number of categories, consider a multi-channel approach. For example, use a few distinct hues for major groups, and then different shapes within those color groups for subgroups. Ensure that neither the hues nor the shapes imply an unintended order.
```

```
title: "Use scatter plots to show correlation between two variables"

## Guidance

When the primary task is to help a user understand the relationship or correlation between two quantitative variables, the most effective visualization is a scatter plot.

## Why

A scatter plot directly maps the two variables to the x and y axes, encoding each data point's values as a position. This allows the human visual system to pre-attentively perceive patterns in the distribution of points, such as linear trends, clusters, and outliers, which are the hallmarks of correlation. Empirical studies have confirmed that viewers can judge the strength of a correlation more accurately from a scatter plot than from other visualizations like parallel coordinates.

## When it applies

- When you want to see if two numerical variables are related (e.g., "Is there a relationship between a person's height and weight?").
- When you are looking for clusters, outliers, or non-linear relationships in bivariate data.
- When choosing between a scatter plot and a dual-axis line chart or parallel coordinates for showing a relationship.

## Exceptions

- **Multiple Variables (>2):** If the goal is to see correlations across *many* variables simultaneously, a scatter plot matrix (multiple small scatter plots) or a parallel coordinates plot can be used. However, be aware that judging the correlation between any two variables in a parallel coordinates plot is less accurate than in a dedicated scatter plot.
- **Time-Series Data:** If one of the variables is time, a line chart is the standard and most effective convention. If you are comparing two time-series variables, plotting them on the same line chart (with a single, shared y-axis if possible) is appropriate.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Dual-Axis Line Chart:** Using a line chart with two different y-axes to imply a correlation is highly misleading and should be avoided. The perceived relationship can be manipulated arbitrarily by changing the scale of either axis.
- **Separate Charts:** Presenting two separate bar or line charts and asking the viewer to mentally compare them to find a correlation is cognitively demanding and ineffective.
- **Overplotting:** In a scatter plot with a very large number of data points, the points can plot on top of each other, obscuring the density and true distribution. This is known as overplotting.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix (for overplotting): Reduce Opacity.** If you have overplotting, reduce the opacity of the points. This allows areas with high density to appear darker, revealing the underlying distribution.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Scatter Plot.** If you are using a dual-axis chart or two separate charts, combine the data into a single, correctly-scaled scatter plot.
- **Comprehensive Approach (for overplotting): Use Aggregation.** For very dense scatter plots, use techniques to address overplotting directly. This can include:
    - **Binning:** Group the points into 2D bins and use color to show the density in each bin (creating a 2D histogram or heatmap).
    - **Contour Lines:** Show the density of points using contour lines, similar to a topographical map.
    - **Sampling:** Plot only a random sample of the data points if the overall trend is more important than individual points.
```

```
title: "Be aware of aspect ratio bias in bar charts"
tags:
  - impact:perceptual
  - impact:ethical
  - chart:bar
  - task:compare
  - task:rank
  - data:quantitative
sources:
  - type: research
    ref: Zeng & Battle, 2023
    url: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581349
    note: "Summarizes in Table 8 and Section 5.2.3 the findings from Ceja et al. (2020) that the systematic bias in bar charts is related to their aspect ratio: tall bars are underestimated, wide bars are overestimated, and square-ish bars have no systematic bias."
  - type: research
    ref: Ceja et al., 2020
    url: https://doi.org/10.1109/TVCG.2020.3023028
    note: "The recent experiment that identified and quantified the systematic bias in bar chart perception related to aspect ratio."
---

## Guidance

Recognize that the aspect ratio of bars in a bar chart can introduce systematic perceptual bias. Viewers tend to underestimate the value of tall, thin bars and overestimate the value of short, wide bars. Bars with a "squarish" aspect ratio exhibit the least bias.

## Why

While bar charts are generally one of the most effective chart types for comparing magnitudes, recent research has revealed a subtle perceptual flaw. The judgment of a bar's value is not based on length alone, but is influenced by its two-dimensional area and shape. This leads to a consistent and predictable bias based on the bar's aspect ratio. Being aware of this bias is crucial for creating charts that are as accurate as possible and for interpreting them correctly.

## When it applies

- In any bar chart where accurate judgment of magnitude is important.
- When designing responsive charts where bar aspect ratios may change dramatically across different screen sizes.
- When evaluating the potential for misinterpretation in a bar chart.

## Trade-offs

- **Space vs. Accuracy:** The ideal "squarish" aspect ratio for minimizing bias may conflict with layout constraints. For example, fitting a large number of categories into a chart may force the bars to become very thin, introducing underestimation bias.
- **Aesthetics:** Deliberately controlling for aspect ratio may lead to charts that look unusual or aesthetically unpleasing (e.g., having very wide bars to represent small values).

## Signs of Trouble

- **Pencil-Thin Bars:** A chart contains very tall, skinny bars. Viewers will likely underestimate their relative and absolute values.
- **Short, Stubby Bars:** A chart contains very short, wide bars. Viewers will likely overestimate their values.
- **Inconsistent Aspect Ratios:** In a responsive layout, the bars appear tall and thin on mobile but short and wide on desktop, introducing different biases on each platform.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Direct Data Labels.** This is the most effective and immediate way to mitigate the bias. By providing the exact numerical value on or near each bar, you give the viewer an "escape hatch" to read the true value, bypassing their potentially biased perceptual judgment.
- **Moderate Redesign: Adjust Aspect Ratios.** Where possible, adjust the chart's overall dimensions and the bar widths to make the bars, on average, more "squarish". This may involve increasing bar width, adding space between bars, or changing the chart's height.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Switch to a Dot Plot.** Consider replacing the bar chart with a dot plot. A dot plot encodes value using only the position of the dot, not its length or area. This removes the aspect ratio variable entirely and relies on the most accurate perceptual task: position on a common scale. This is a particularly strong choice when you anticipate the chart will be viewed across many different screen sizes.

```
