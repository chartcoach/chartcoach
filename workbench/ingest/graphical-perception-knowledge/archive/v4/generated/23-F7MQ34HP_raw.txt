## Guidance
For tasks requiring comparison of multiple time series at a single, specific point in time (a "local" comparison), use a shared-space technique like a standard line chart where all series are plotted in the same coordinate space.

## Why
Shared-space techniques place all data on a common baseline and scale within a single view. This allows for direct, preattentive visual comparison of values at a specific time point without requiring significant eye movement between different charts, leading to faster performance.

## When it applies
- When the primary task is to find the highest/lowest value or compare values among several time series at a specific, localized point or a narrow time window.
- When the number of time series is relatively low (e.g., under 8), so that visual clutter and line overlap are manageable.

## Exceptions
- When the number of time series is high, the visual clutter and overlap from a shared-space chart can become overwhelming, making it difficult to distinguish individual lines. In this case, a split-space technique like small multiples may be clearer, despite being slower for purely local comparisons.

## Trade-offs
- **Gains:** Faster performance for local comparison tasks.
- **Sacrifices:** Can become cluttered and illegible as the number of time series increases. Less effective for tasks that require following a single series over a long duration or comparing series at distant points in time.

## Signs of Trouble
- **"Spaghetti" Chart:** The chart is a tangled mess of overlapping lines, making it impossible to distinguish one from another.
- **Color Ambiguity:** So many lines are present that the colors become difficult to tell apart, especially for thin lines.
- **Slow Performance on Global Tasks:** Users are slow when asked to identify trends or compare series across the entire time span, as they struggle to trace individual lines through the clutter.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Highlight Key Series.** If you must use a shared-space chart with many series, use color and stroke width to highlight the most important series and de-emphasize others (e.g., by making them gray and thin).
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to Small Multiples.** If the main tasks involve global comparisons or following individual series, switch to small multiples. This eliminates overlap and makes each series clear.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Offer Interactive Filtering.** Provide an interactive legend that allows users to toggle the visibility of different time series. This lets them reduce clutter on demand and focus on a smaller subset for comparison.

## Guidance
For tasks that require comparing multiple time series across a wide or dispersed time span, use a split-space technique like small multiples or horizon graphs.

## Why
Shared-space charts with many overlapping lines create significant visual clutter, making it cognitively demanding to follow an individual series across a long distance. Split-space techniques, like small multiples, eliminate this overlap by giving each time series its own dedicated chart. This reduces the cognitive load and makes it easier and faster to perform comparisons over a wide visual span.

## When it applies
- When the task involves comparing trends, patterns, or specific values at points that are far apart in time.
- When the number of time series is high enough that a standard line chart would become a cluttered "spaghetti" plot.
- When the clarity of each individual series is more important than the speed of direct comparison at a single point in time.

## Exceptions
- If the primary task is a purely local comparison (i.e., finding the max value at a single time point), shared-space charts are faster because they don't require eye movement between charts.

## Trade-offs
- **Gains:** Greatly improved clarity and performance for tasks over a wide time span, especially with many series. Scales well to higher numbers of series.
- **Sacrifices:** Slower performance for purely local comparisons. Requires more total screen real estate than a single shared-space chart.

## Signs of Trouble
- **Spaghetti Chart:** In a standard line chart, you can't follow a single colored line from one end of the chart to the other without losing it in a tangle of other lines.
- **User Complaints:** Viewers complain that the chart is "too busy" or "confusing," or that they "can't tell which line is which."
- **High Error Rates:** When asked to compare values at distant points in a shared-space chart, users make frequent errors.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Switch to Small Multiples.** The most direct way to fix a cluttered shared-space chart is to convert it into small multiples.
- **Moderate Approach: Synchronize Axes and Highlighting.** Ensure all charts in the small multiple set use the same y-axis scale for valid comparison. Implement linked highlighting, where hovering over one chart highlights the corresponding point in time on all other charts.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Consider Horizon Graphs for High Density.** If vertical space is extremely limited and you have a very large number of time series, horizon graphs can be a more space-efficient alternative to small multiples, though they have a steeper learning curve.

## Guidance
For general-purpose visualization of multiple time series, prefer standard line charts (for fewer series) or small multiples (for more series).

## Why
While specialized techniques like horizon graphs or braided graphs exist, studies show that simple line charts and small multiples are more versatile and perform consistently well across a range of common analytical tasks, from local value lookups to global trend comparisons. They have a lower cognitive barrier to entry and are more robust for general audiences.

## When it applies
- When designing a dashboard or tool where users will need to perform a variety of tasks on the same time series data.
- When the audience may not be familiar with more advanced or abstract chart types like horizon graphs.
- When a reliable, all-around performer is needed, rather than a chart hyper-optimized for a single, specific task.

## Exceptions
- When screen space is extremely limited and data density is very high, a more compact representation like a horizon graph might be necessary, despite its higher cognitive load for some tasks.
- When a very specific task is the sole priority (e.g., only local comparisons), a shared-space chart might be marginally faster than small multiples.

## Trade-offs
- **Gains:** Versatility, reliability across tasks, and ease of interpretation for a general audience.
- **Sacrifices:** May not be the absolute fastest or most space-efficient technique for every single specialized task.

## Signs of Trouble
- **User Confusion:** Viewers are unsure how to read a more complex chart (e.g., a horizon graph), asking what the different colors or bands mean.
- **Slow Performance on Simple Tasks:** A complex chart type is used, and it slows down performance on what should be a simple, quick task (e.g., using a horizon graph for a max value lookup).
- **Inflexible Design:** The chosen chart type excels at one task but is very poor for other, equally important tasks that users need to perform.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Default to Simplicity.** Start with a simple line chart. If it becomes cluttered, switch to small multiples. Only consider more complex chart types if there is a compelling, specific need that these simple forms cannot meet.
- **Moderate Approach: Provide a Toggle.** If a specialized view (like a horizon graph) is useful for a specific expert task, consider providing it as an alternative view that users can switch to, but keep the simpler form (small multiples or a line chart) as the default.

  - type: good

  - type: bad
    description: A pie chart with several similarly-sized slices. It is very difficult to tell which slice is larger or to accurately estimate the ratio between them.
---
## Guidance
To enable accurate comparison and ranking of categorical data, use a bar chart instead of a pie chart.

## Why
Humans are significantly better at accurately judging and comparing lengths along a common baseline (the primary visual task in a bar chart) than they are at judging angles, areas, or arc lengths (the tasks required by a pie chart). This perceptual advantage makes bar charts more effective for almost all comparison and ranking tasks.

## When it applies
- When the primary goal is for the user to compare values between categories.
- When the user needs to rank categories from highest to lowest.
- When there are more than 2-3 categories, making angle comparisons in a pie chart increasingly difficult.

## Exceptions
- When the goal is to show a part-to-whole relationship with only two categories (e.g., 75% vs 25%), a pie chart can be effective and immediately recognizable.
- When you need to emphasize that the values sum to a meaningful total (100%). However, a stacked bar chart can often serve this purpose with greater clarity for individual components.

## Trade-offs
- **Gains:** Greatly increased accuracy and speed for comparison and ranking tasks.
- **Sacrifices:** May be less effective at communicating a simple part-to-whole message for 2-3 categories than a pie chart. A bar chart can take up more space than a pie chart, especially if category labels are long.

## Signs of Trouble
- **The "Is it bigger?" Question:** Viewers are unable to confidently tell which of two similarly-sized slices in a pie chart is larger.
- **Multiple Legends:** A pie chart requires a separate color legend, forcing viewers to look back and forth to decode the chart.
- **3D or Exploded Slices:** The pie chart uses 3D effects or has "exploded" slices, which further distort the angles and areas, making accurate perception impossible.
- **More than 5 slices:** The pie chart has many small slices, becoming a "rainbow wheel" that is impossible to interpret.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Switch to a Bar Chart.** The most effective improvement is to change the chart type from a pie chart to a bar chart.
- **Moderate Approach: Sort and Label.** If you switch to a bar chart, sort the bars in descending or ascending order (unless there is a natural ordering to the categories). Add direct data labels to the bars to facilitate precise lookups.
- **Comprehensive Approach: If you must use a Pie Chart...** If required to use a pie chart, limit it to 2-3 categories, add direct percentage labels to each slice, and never use 3D or exploding effects. For more categories, a bar chart is always a better alternative.

title: "Prioritize position encoding over other visual channels for accuracy"

    url: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581349
    note: "Summarizes the foundational effectiveness rankings from Cleveland & McGill (1984) and Mackinlay (1986), which establish position on a common scale as the most accurate visual channel for quantitative tasks."
examples:
  - type: good
    description: A scatter plot uses position on the X and Y axes to encode two quantitative variables, allowing for accurate judgment of values and relationships.
  - type: bad
    description: A bubble chart that encodes a primary quantitative variable using the area of circles (size) instead of position. Small differences in value are very difficult to perceive accurately from differences in area.
---
## Guidance
For encoding quantitative, ordinal, and nominal data, prioritize using position on a common scale (e.g., points on a line, bars on an axis) as it is the most perceptually accurate visual channel.

## Why
Decades of graphical perception research have established a clear hierarchy of visual encodings based on how accurately humans can perceive them. Position along a common scale is at the top of this hierarchy, followed by length, angle, area, and finally color saturation/hue. By mapping your most important data to the most accurate channel (position), you enable viewers to make more precise judgments and comparisons.

## When it applies
- When encoding quantitative data for comparison or ranking (e.g., bar charts, scatter plots, line charts).
- When encoding ordinal data where the order and relative difference are important.
- When encoding nominal data where clear separation is needed (e.g., using position in small multiples or faceting).

## Exceptions
- Position is a "high-cardinality" channel, but you only have two or three dimensions (X, Y, and maybe Z for 3D, which is often problematic). If you need to encode more than 2-3 variables, you must use other, less effective channels like color, size, or shape.
- When a less accurate overview is acceptable. For example, using color hue on a map (a choropleth) is less accurate than using bar heights on a map (a prism map), but it is more conventional and can provide a good "gist" of the data.

## Trade-offs
- **Gains:** The highest possible perceptual accuracy for the encoded data.
- **Sacrifices:** You can only encode two, or at most three, variables using position. All other variables must be relegated to less accurate channels. Over-reliance on position can lead to very large or complex charts if many data points are to be shown without aggregation.

## Signs of Trouble
- **The "Guessing Game":** Viewers are trying to compare the areas of two bubbles or the saturation of two colors and cannot agree on which is greater.
- **Important Data is Hard to Read:** The most important variable in your dataset is encoded with shape or texture, making it nearly impossible to compare.
- **Redundant, Conflicting Encodings:** The same variable is encoded with both position and color, but the color scale is misleading (e.g., a non-perceptual rainbow scale), creating a conflict that confuses the viewer.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Promote Your Main Variable.** Identify the most important variable for your chart's message. Ensure it is mapped to position (X or Y axis). Relegate secondary variables to color or size.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch Chart Types.** If your current chart (e.g., a bubble chart) uses a less effective channel for its primary comparison, switch to a chart type that uses position. For example, instead of a bubble chart (size), use a bar chart (length/position) or a dot plot (position).
- **Comprehensive Approach: Use Faceting (Small Multiples).** If you have more than two primary variables to compare, use faceting to create small multiples. This allows you to use position (X and Y) within each facet, while using the position of the facets themselves to encode a third or fourth categorical variable. This leverages the power of position for multiple variables.

title: "Use color saturation or brightness, not hue, to represent ordered data"
tags:

  - access:color-vision-risk

  - data:ordinal
  - data:quantitative
  - task:rank

    url: https://doi.org/10.1145/3544548.3581349
    note: "Synthesizes findings from Mackinlay (1986) on expressiveness and empirical work like Golebiowska & Coltekin (2022) on the unintuitive nature of rainbow schemes for ordered tasks."
examples:
  - type: good
    description: A sequential color scheme that varies from light blue to dark blue. It is intuitively understood that darker shades represent higher values.
  - type: bad
    description: A rainbow (spectral) color scheme used to represent temperature on a map. It's not clear if green is higher or lower than yellow, or if red is the highest value, leading to misinterpretation.
---
## Guidance
When encoding ordinal (ordered) or quantitative data with color, use a scale that varies in saturation or brightness (e.g., light blue to dark blue). Avoid using a scale that varies in hue (e.g., a rainbow scale).

## Why
The human visual system naturally perceives variations in brightness and saturation as a representation of "more" or "less," making sequential color schemes intuitive for ordered data. In contrast, variations in hue (like red, green, blue) are perceived as indicating different categories, not a specific order. A rainbow color scale has no natural perceptual ordering, which can lead to confusion and misinterpretation of the data.

## When it applies
- When creating a choropleth map to show a quantitative value like population density or income level.
- When coloring a heatmap to show magnitudes.
- When using color to encode any quantitative or ordinal variable in a chart.

## Exceptions
- When encoding purely nominal (categorical) data where there is no inherent order, a qualitative palette using different hues is appropriate (e.g., blue for 'Category A', orange for 'Category B').
- A diverging palette (e.g., blue-white-red) can be effective for quantitative data when there is a meaningful midpoint or zero value, as it uses two different hues to represent values above and below the midpoint.

## Trade-offs
- **Gains:** Intuitive, accurate interpretation of ordered data. Improved accessibility for users with color vision deficiencies, as changes in lightness are often more robustly perceived than changes in hue.
- **Sacrifices:** May be less visually "vibrant" or "colorful" than a rainbow palette, which some audiences may find more aesthetically engaging, despite its perceptual flaws.

## Signs of Trouble
- **The Rainbow Problem:** The chart uses a spectral (rainbow) color scheme for quantitative data.
- **Order Confusion:** Viewers are unsure whether green represents a higher or lower value than yellow.
- **False Boundaries:** The abrupt changes in hue in a rainbow palette can create the illusion of sharp boundaries in the data where none exist.
- **Accessibility Failure:** When viewed with a colorblindness simulator, distinct hues (like red and green) merge into an indistinguishable color, making the chart unreadable.

## How to Improve
- **Quick Fix: Use a Pre-built Sequential Scheme.** Most modern visualization tools (like D3, Datawrapper, Tableau) offer built-in, perceptually-vetted sequential color schemes (e.g., "Blues", "Viridis"). Switch from a custom rainbow scheme to one of these.
- **Moderate Approach: Design a Single-Hue Scheme.** Create a color scale that varies only in saturation and/or brightness of a single hue. For example, start with a light gray-blue and progress to a dark, saturated blue.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Use a Perceptually Uniform Color Space.** When designing color schemes, work in a perceptually uniform color space like CIELAB, CIELUV, or HCL. This ensures that a given step-change in the data corresponds to a perceptually equal change in the color, making the scale more accurate and easier to interpret. Tools like `d3-scale-chromatic` are based on these principles.
