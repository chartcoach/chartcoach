## Guidance

When using visual variables like shape or color for categorical data, arrange them in an order that maximizes how different they look from one another. Avoid relying on the default order of a software's palette, which may group similar-looking items together.

## Why

Default palettes often group items by visual characteristic (e.g., all triangles together, then all squares), not by perceptual distinctness. This can make it hard for viewers to tell categories apart at a glance, increasing cognitive load and the risk of misinterpretation. A perceptually re-ordered palette places the most distinct items first and ensures a greater visual gap between neighbors, improving the speed and accuracy of distinguishing between data categories.

## When it applies

- When encoding categorical (nominal) data with visual channels like color hue or shape.
- In charts with multiple categories that need to be distinguished, such as multi-class scatterplots, grouped bar charts, or multi-line charts.
- When designing legends for categorical data.

## Exceptions

- When the order of categories is inherently meaningful and must be preserved (e.g., "Strongly Disagree" to "Strongly Agree").
- When strict brand guidelines dictate a specific, fixed order for a color palette.
- When there are very few categories (e.g., 2 or 3) that are already highly distinct by default.

## Trade-offs

- A perceptually optimized palette may appear less "tidy" than a default palette that groups similar shapes or hues.
- It requires extra effort to determine the optimal perceptual order, rather than simply accepting a software's default.

## Evaluate

- [ ] The chart legend or palette groups visually similar items together (e.g., a plus `+` next to a cross `x`).
- [ ] It is difficult to quickly tell two or more categories apart in the chart without carefully consulting the legend.
- [ ] The palette contains symbols or colors that are perceptually very close, leading to potential confusion.

## Repair

1.  **Manually re-order the palette.** Swap adjacent items that look too similar. For example, instead of `circle, square, plus, cross`, try `circle, plus, square, cross` to separate the two line-based symbols with a solid shape.
2.  **Generate a new palette.** Use a tool like Colorgorical to create a new, perceptually distinct color palette from scratch.
3.  **Choose a better set of symbols.** Instead of a tool's default shape set, select symbols specifically designed for high discriminability (e.g., a mix of open and closed shapes, simple and complex shapes).
