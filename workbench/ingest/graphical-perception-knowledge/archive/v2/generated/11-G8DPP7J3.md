## Guidance

When using color to distinguish between different categories, use an algorithmic tool to optimize your palette. The goal is to maximize the *minimum perceptual distance* between any two colors, ensuring that even the most similar pair is easy to tell apart.

## Why

The human eye does not perceive color differences linearly. Two colors that seem different in a color picker can become nearly indistinguishable when used for small points in a scatterplot or thin lines in a line chart. Relying on intuition, brand guidelines, or default palettes often results in some color pairs being much harder to distinguish than others.

By algorithmically optimizing the palette using a perceptual color model (like CIEDE2000), you ensure that every color is as discriminable from every other color as possible. This makes the chart easier and faster to read accurately.

## When it applies

- When using color to encode **categorical data** in any chart type (e.g., groups in a scatterplot, series in a line chart, segments in a bar chart).
- When you have a predefined set of colors you must start with, such as **brand colors** or colors with **strong semantic meaning** (e.g., red for "danger," green for "safe").
- When creating a reusable color palette for a dashboard, report, or design system.

## Exceptions

- When using a well-established, pre-vetted, and perceptually uniform palette, such as **Okabe-Ito**, which is already designed for high discriminability and accessibility.
- When your "palette" consists of only two or three very distinct colors (e.g., blue, orange, and gray), where optimization is unlikely to yield significant improvements.

## Trade-offs

- **Aesthetics vs. Perception:** An algorithmically optimized palette prioritizes perceptual distance, which may not always align with traditional aesthetic harmony. The resulting colors might feel less coordinated than a hand-picked palette.
- **Original Intent vs. Optimized Value:** The optimization process will likely change the original HEX/RGB values of your colors. If you need to preserve brand or semantic colors exactly, you can apply constraints to limit how much a color can change, but this will result in a less-than-maximally-optimal palette.

## Evaluate

- [ ] Are any two colors in the palette difficult to tell apart when viewed as small marks (dots, thin lines) or when they are far from the legend?
- [ ] Does the palette pass a colorblindness simulation? (Even if optimized, it's a necessary check).
- [ ] Has a color difference tool been used to measure the CIEDE2000 distance between all pairs of colors? Is the minimum distance score low (e.g., under 20-25)?

## Repair

1.  **Input your colors into an optimizer.** Take your existing palette (e.g., a list of HEX codes) and load it into a tool like "I want hue" or "Colorgorical".
2.  **Run the optimization.** Use the tool's function to refine the palette, which will adjust the colors to increase the perceptual distance between them.
3.  **Apply constraints if needed.** If you must preserve certain colors (e.g., a background color, a key brand color), "lock" them or apply tight constraints to limit how much they can change. You can often constrain the optimization to hue, saturation, or lightness to maintain the original color's character while improving its performance.
4.  **Export and use the new palette.** Replace your old color values with the new, optimized ones.
