## Guidance

When a chart requires viewers to compare the lengths of two segments stacked within the same bar, introduce a visual gap or an intervening element between them.

## Why

When two bar segments are stacked directly on top of each other, a strong perceptual bias occurs. Viewers tend to unconsciously compare the length of one segment to the **total length of both segments combined** (a part-to-whole judgment) instead of comparing the two segments to each other (a part-to-part judgment). This leads to significant and systematic underestimation.

Introducing a small gap breaks this visual grouping, discouraging the part-to-whole bias and prompting a more accurate length-to-length comparison.

## When it applies

This guidance is most relevant for **divided bar charts** (also called floating bar charts) where a key analysis task is to compare the magnitudes of two or more non-aligned segments within the same bar.

## Exceptions

If the primary goal is to emphasize the part-to-whole relationship (e.g., showing what fraction one segment is of a combined total), then keeping the segments adjacent might be intentional. However, be aware that this actively hinders direct comparison between the parts. If accurate part-to-part comparison is not a goal, this guideline may be ignored.

## Trade-offs

-   **Space:** Introducing a gap requires slightly more vertical space.
-   **Continuity:** A gap can slightly disrupt the perception of the bar as a single continuous entity, although this is often a minor effect.

## Evaluate

-   [ ] Does the chart require viewers to compare the lengths of two segments stacked in the same column?
-   [ ] Are those two segments positioned directly adjacent to one another with no visual separation?

## Repair

1.  **Introduce a Gap:** The simplest fix is to add a small, visible gap between the two segments. This immediately breaks the perceptual bias.
2.  **Use an Intervening Bar:** If a gap is not possible, separate the segments with a third, often neutral-colored, bar segment. Research shows this also reduces the comparison error.
3.  **Change Chart Type:** If the comparison is critical, consider a chart that uses a common baseline, which is perceptually superior. A **grouped bar chart** or two separate, simple bar charts would allow for a much more accurate comparison.
