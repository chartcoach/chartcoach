## Guidance

To help your audience understand the relationship (or correlation) between two numerical variables, use a scatterplot. Each variable gets its own axis, and each data point is plotted as a dot at the intersection of its values.

## Why

Human perception is highly attuned to judging the strength of a relationship from the shape of a cloud of points. Research consistently shows that scatterplots are among the most effective charts for this task, allowing for more precise judgments of correlation compared to many other chart types. While charts like parallel coordinates can also be effective—especially for negative correlations—scatterplots generally offer the best and most consistent performance for judging both positive and negative relationships.

## When it applies

- When your primary goal is to show the relationship between two quantitative (numerical) variables.
- When you want to reveal patterns such as linear or non-linear trends, clusters, and outliers.
- When you are testing a hypothesis about whether two variables move together (positive correlation), in opposite directions (negative correlation), or not at all.

## Exceptions

- **When one variable is time**, a line chart is usually more conventional and effective for showing trends over time.
- **When dealing with many variables (>2)**, a correlation matrix (heatmap) or a parallel coordinates plot may be more space-efficient for showing all pairwise relationships at once, though they are less precise for judging any single pair.
- **When you have very few data points**, a simple table or a paired bar chart might be just as clear and require less interpretation.

## Trade-offs

- Scatterplots can suffer from **overplotting** when there are many overlapping data points, which can hide the true data density.
- While excellent for two variables, scatterplots do not natively scale to showing relationships between three or more variables without adding complexity via other visual channels like color or size.

## Evaluate

- [ ] The chart is intended to show a relationship between two numerical variables, but it's not a scatterplot (e.g., it's a dual-axis line chart or a grouped bar chart).
- [ ] The chart is a scatterplot, but severe overplotting makes the points a solid, unreadable mass.

## Repair

1.  If using another chart type (like a line or bar chart) for a non-temporal relationship, **change it to a scatterplot**.
2.  If the scatterplot suffers from overplotting, **apply transparency** to the points or **reduce their size**.
3.  If overplotting is still extreme, consider switching to a **2D density plot** (a heatmap or contour plot) or a **hexbin plot** to show where the most points are concentrated.
