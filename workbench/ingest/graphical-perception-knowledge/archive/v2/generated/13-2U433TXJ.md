## Guidance

When comparing time series, choose a visualization that matches how you define similarity. Different chart types make certain pattern variations—like shifts in time or changes in height—either more or less noticeable.

## Why

The visual encoding of a chart influences what patterns we perceive as "similar." Research shows that our notion of similarity is not independent of the visualization we use.

-   **Horizon graphs** help us see similarity even when patterns are temporally stretched or shifted (warped).
-   **Line charts and colorfields** are better for identifying similar shapes when we want to ignore differences in amplitude and vertical position.

Using the wrong chart for your specific definition of similarity can lead to misinterpretations and poor analytical decisions.

## When it applies

-   When performing visual similarity search or clustering on time series data.
-   When deciding between line charts, horizon graphs, or color-based representations (like heatmaps or colorfields) for a comparison task.
-   When your underlying similarity algorithm has specific invariances (e.g., tolerance for temporal warping or amplitude scaling) and you want the visual representation to match.

## Exceptions

-   If the primary task is not similarity comparison but something else, like identifying absolute peak values or reading exact data points, other guidelines may take precedence. A standard line chart is often best for reading specific values.
-   If vertical space is extremely limited, horizon graphs and colorfields are more compact than line charts, which might be a necessary trade-off against their perceptual quirks.

## Trade-offs

-   **Horizon Graphs**: While effective for spotting temporally warped similarity, they can be slower to interpret and are sensitive to small changes in amplitude, which can cause misleading color shifts at the band boundaries.
-   **Colorfields/Heatmaps**: While spatially efficient, they are less effective at communicating similarity when temporal warping is present and abstract away the fine-grained details of a pattern's shape.
-   **Line Charts**: A good general-purpose choice, but they can become cluttered with many overlapping series and take up more vertical space than the alternatives.

## Evaluate

-   [ ] Are you trying to identify similar patterns that are shifted or warped in time? If so, a colorfield might be hindering your analysis.
-   [ ] Are you trying to identify similar shapes regardless of their height or vertical position (amplitude/offset invariant)? If so, a horizon graph might be misleading you.
-   [ ] Does your chosen chart type feel slow or difficult to use for making similarity judgments? (Research suggests horizon graphs can be the most cognitively demanding for this task).

## Repair

1.  **First, clarify your similarity criteria:** Decide what "similar" means for your task. Should patterns that are stretched, compressed, or shifted in time still be considered a match? Should patterns of different heights be considered a match?
2.  **To see similarity despite temporal warping:** Use a **horizon graph** or a **line chart**. Avoid colorfields.
3.  **To see similarity despite amplitude/offset differences:** Use a **line chart** or a **colorfield**. Avoid horizon graphs.
4.  **When in doubt, start with a line chart.** It is a well-understood and flexible representation for most time series comparison tasks.
