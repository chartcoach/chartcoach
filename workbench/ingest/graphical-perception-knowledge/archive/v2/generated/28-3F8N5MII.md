## Guidance

For quantitative data shown in a colormap (like a heatmap or choropleth map), map larger values to darker colors.

## Why

People have a strong cognitive bias known as the "dark-is-more" bias. We intuitively associate darker colors with greater quantity, density, or importance. When a visualization aligns with this built-in expectation, it becomes easier and faster to understand. Forcing a user to interpret a "light-is-more" scale adds cognitive load and increases the risk of misinterpretation, as they must actively fight their intuition. Research shows that users process "dark-is-more" colormaps significantly faster.

## When it applies

- When using a colormap to represent a single, continuous quantitative variable (i.e., sequential data).
- In chart types like heatmaps, choropleth maps, and other surface visualizations.
- When the primary goal is for the audience to quickly identify areas of high and low value.

## Exceptions

- **On dark backgrounds:** The "dark-is-more" bias can be weakened or even reversed by a competing "opaque-is-more" bias. If a colormap on a dark background looks like it's fading from a bright color to black, people may perceive the brighter (more opaque) color as "more". In this specific case, a "light-is-more" scale can be effective. For general-purpose charts that must work on any background, "dark-is-more" is the safer default.
- **Semantic Resonance:** If the data represents a concept that is intuitively "light" (e.g., brightness, temperature, sunshine), a "light-is-more" encoding may feel more natural to the audience. Even here, proceed with caution and ensure the legend is exceptionally clear.

## Trade-offs

- Sticking to a "dark-is-more" rule might conflict with brand guidelines or specific aesthetic goals that favor lighter palettes.
- If a single chart must be displayed on both light and dark backgrounds (e.g., in a slide deck with different themes), you must be especially careful. The safest choice is a "dark-is-more" encoding with a colormap that does *not* create an opacity effect (e.g., a multi-hue palette like Viridis or Magma).

## Evaluate

- [ ] Lighter colors are used to represent the highest values in the data.
- [ ] The legend shows a scale where the "High" or "More" label is next to the lightest color.
- [ ] A user glancing at the chart is likely to draw the wrong conclusion about where the most important values are.

## Repair

1. **Invert the color scale.** The simplest fix is to reverse the colormap so that darker colors represent higher values.
2. **Update the legend.** Ensure the labels on the legend are correctly aligned with the new color mapping (e.g., "High" is next to the darkest color).
3. **Choose a robust palette.** If the chart will be used in multiple contexts (e.g., light and dark mode), select a perceptually uniform, multi-hue color palette that is less likely to create conflicting opacity effects.
