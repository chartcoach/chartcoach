## Guidance

Choose a colormap based on your audience's primary task and the visual complexity of your data. There is no single "best" colormap; the optimal choice depends on what you want the user to see.

## Why

Different colormaps are optimized for different perceptual tasks. Using the wrong map for the task can impair accuracy, slow down interpretation, and even cause viewers to see patterns that don't exist.

-   **For looking up specific values:** Colormaps with a wide range of hues (like rainbow) perform best. The distinct colors act as clear signposts, helping people more accurately map a color back to a value on the legend.
-   **For seeing shapes and patterns:** Colormaps that vary smoothly in lightness (like sequential or diverging schemes) are more effective. Our visual system is tuned to interpret changes in lightness as changes in shape, making it easier to see structure, especially in visually complex data.

## When it applies

-   When creating continuous quantitative maps like heatmaps, contour maps, or any scientific visualization that maps a smooth data attribute (e.g., temperature, pressure, density) to a color gradient.

## Exceptions

-   **Strong domain conventions:** If a specific field (e.g., physics, meteorology) has a deeply ingrained colormap standard, defying it may cause more confusion than it solves, even if the standard is perceptually suboptimal.
-   **Branding constraints:** If your organization's brand palette is strict, you may have to work within its limitations, even if it prevents you from using a perceptually ideal colormap.

## Trade-offs

-   You cannot perfectly optimize for all tasks at once. A colormap that is excellent for looking up specific values (like rainbow) is often poor for seeing fine-grained patterns. You must prioritize the most critical task for your audience.
-   Using a task-appropriate but unconventional colormap may require a short adjustment period for viewers accustomed to seeing a "standard" (but less effective) map.

## Evaluate

-   [ ] A rainbow colormap is used for a visualization where the primary goal is to identify subtle or complex patterns.
-   [ ] A grayscale or single-hue sequential colormap is used where the primary goal is to accurately read a value at a specific point.
-   [ ] A single, all-purpose colormap is used across a dashboard or report for different charts with different analytical goals.

## Repair

1.  **First, identify the primary task.** Ask yourself: "What is the single most important thing I want my user to do with this chart?" Is it to read a value, or to see a pattern?

2.  **If the task is to look up values:** Use a colormap with high variation in hue. A multi-hue sequential or even a carefully designed rainbow scheme can be effective here.

3.  **If the task is to see patterns in complex data:** Use a diverging colormap (like blue-white-red) or a sequential colormap with a large, uniform change in lightness. This will make structures and shapes more apparent.

4.  **If the tasks are mixed:** Prioritize the most critical task. If both are equally important, consider providing a control to let the user toggle between a "value" colormap and a "pattern" colormap, or create separate, optimized charts for each task.
