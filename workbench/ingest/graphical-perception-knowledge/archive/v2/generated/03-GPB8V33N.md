## Guidance

Choose a **parallel coordinates plot (PCP)** when the main goal is to find patterns like clusters or outliers across many quantitative variables.

Prefer a **scatterplot** when the main goal is to assess the correlation or relationship between just two variables.

## Why

Our visual system is good at making holistic judgments about simple shapes. A parallel coordinates plot turns a row of data with many attributes into a single, connected line. This allows you to quickly spot lines that follow a similar path (clusters) or lines that behave completely differently from the rest (outliers).

In contrast, a scatterplot is purpose-built to show the relationship between two variables on its X and Y axes. The overall shape of the cloud of points makes it easy to judge the strength and direction of a correlation (e.g., positive, negative, or none). While a scatterplot matrix can show many variables, it requires you to mentally connect dots across many separate charts to understand the profile of a single data point, which is a much slower and more error-prone task.

## When it applies

- You are working with a tabular dataset containing three or more quantitative columns (e.g., product specs, sensor readings, patient metrics).
- Your primary analysis tasks are to:
    - **Find clusters:** Identify groups of data points that have similar profiles.
    - **Detect outliers/anomalies:** Spot individual data points that are unusual.
    - **Compare profiles:** See how different data points compare across all attributes.

## Exceptions

- **The task is value lookup:** If you just need to find a specific number (e.g., "What was the horsepower of the Ford Pinto?"), a simple table is almost always faster and more accurate than any chart.
- **Your audience is unfamiliar with PCPs:** Parallel coordinates plots can be unintuitive for first-time viewers. If you don't have time for a brief explanation, a more familiar (though less powerful) chart like a scatterplot matrix might be a safer choice.
- **You have too many data points:** With thousands of lines, a PCP can become a dense, unreadable block of color (an "overplotting" problem). In these cases, techniques like aggregation, filtering, or highlighting are necessary.

## Trade-offs

- **Power vs. Learnability:** A PCP is a powerful tool for multivariate pattern-finding but has a steeper learning curve than a familiar scatterplot. You trade immediate intuitiveness for deeper analytical capability.
- **Holistic Profile vs. Bivariate Detail:** By choosing a PCP, you optimize for seeing the overall profile of a data point across all variables. You sacrifice the ability to easily and accurately judge the precise correlation between any two specific variables.

## Evaluate

- [ ] A scatterplot matrix is being used, but the primary task is to find individual outliers or trace a single item's journey across all attributes.
- [ ] Users are struggling to compare the profiles of two or more items because they have to mentally link points across multiple separate charts.
- [ ] A parallel coordinates plot is being used when the only question is "How strong is the correlation between variable A and variable B?"

## Repair

1.  **Swap the chart type:** If the task is finding multivariate patterns (clusters, outliers), replace the scatterplot matrix with a parallel coordinates plot. If the task is judging bivariate correlation, use a single scatterplot.
2.  **Educate the user:** If a PCP is the right chart but your audience is confused, add annotations or a brief "how to read this" guide. Interactivity, like highlighting a line on hover (brushing), can also dramatically improve comprehension.
3.  **Combine approaches:** Use a PCP for the main overview, but allow users to click on two axes to generate a detailed scatterplot of just those two variables on demand. This gives you the best of both worlds.
