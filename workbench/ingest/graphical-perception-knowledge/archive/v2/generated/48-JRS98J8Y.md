## Guidance

Use continuous colormaps that are perceptually uniform and ordered to accurately represent your data.

## Why

The goal of a colormap is to translate data values into color. A good colormap ensures that this translation is intuitive and accurate.

*   **Uniformity** means that equal steps in the data look like equal steps in color, preventing you from seeing false gradients or missing subtle ones.
*   **Order** means you can easily tell which colors represent higher or lower values without constantly checking the legend.

Violating these principles, as the common rainbow colormap does, can introduce misleading artifacts, obscure important features, and make the data harder to interpret for everyone, especially for people with color vision deficiencies.

## When it applies

- When mapping a continuous quantitative variable (e.g., temperature, pressure, elevation) to color.
- When using color to represent ordered data (e.g., low, medium, high).
- In chart types like heatmaps, choropleth maps, or any scientific visualization where color represents a scalar field.

## Exceptions

- When the goal is to intentionally highlight a specific range of values (e.g., a critical threshold). In this case, you might deliberately create a non-uniform colormap with high contrast around that value.
- For categorical data, where there is no inherent order, you should use a qualitative palette of distinct colors, not a continuous one.

## Trade-offs

- Maximizing the number of distinguishable steps (discriminative power) can sometimes slightly compromise perfect perceptual uniformity.
- Highly uniform, monotonic colormaps (like grayscale) are very clear but may be less aesthetically engaging or have lower discriminative power than a multi-hue colormap like Viridis.

## Evaluate

- [ ] The colormap has sharp, distinct bands of color, like the jump from yellow to green in a rainbow scale. This suggests non-uniformity.
- [ ] It's hard to tell if blue is higher or lower than green without looking at the legend. This suggests a lack of intuitive order.
- [ ] The colormap has a bright, saturated band in the middle (e.g., yellow) that draws attention away from the ends of the scale. This indicates non-monotonic luminance.
- [ ] Converting the visualization to grayscale makes it unreadable because different data values map to the same shade of gray.

## Repair

1.  **Switch to a better default.** Replace rainbow, jet, or other legacy colormaps with a perceptually uniform sequential (e.g., Viridis, Plasma) or diverging (e.g., cool-warm) colormap. Most modern visualization libraries (like Matplotlib, Plotly) provide these as defaults or built-in options.
2.  **For sequential data**, choose a colormap that increases steadily in lightness from one end to the other.
3.  **For diverging data**, use a colormap with a neutral, light color at the center and two distinct, perceptually-balanced hues moving towards the endpoints.
