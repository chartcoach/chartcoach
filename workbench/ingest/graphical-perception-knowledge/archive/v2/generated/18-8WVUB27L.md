## Guidance

To help people judge overall spatial patterns or "texture" in a scalar field (like a heatmap or terrain map), use a colormap with some distinct color bands, such as a diverging or even a spectral (rainbow) scheme.

## Why

The discrete color bands created by these schemes act as a visual shortcut, or heuristic. Research shows that viewers can quickly estimate the size and frequency of these color patches to judge the overall "steepness" or "activity" in the data. Smooth, perceptually uniform colormaps (like `viridis`) lack these distinct bands, making this type of judgment harder and requiring larger differences in the data to be noticeable.

## When it applies

- When the primary task is to compare the **overall** gradient, texture, or spatial variability between two or more maps.
- When visualizing 2D scalar fields, such as terrain maps, weather data, or simulation outputs.
- When the goal is for a user to identify regions of high "activity" or rapid change at a glance.

## Exceptions

- **Value look-up:** If the primary task is for users to read precise values at specific points, a smooth, monotonic-luminance colormap is more effective and less likely to create misleading "false" boundaries.
- **Accessibility:** Standard rainbow colormaps are not accessible to users with color vision deficiencies and should be avoided if accessibility is a priority. Some diverging schemes are much safer.
- **Data without a meaningful midpoint:** Diverging colormaps are designed for data with a clear central breakpoint (like zero). If your data is purely sequential (e.g., 0 to 100), a sequential colormap is more appropriate.

## Trade-offs

- **Task-specific performance:** You gain sensitivity for judging overall spatial patterns, but you may lose accuracy for tasks involving reading specific data values. The color bands that help with pattern perception can be perceived as abrupt jumps that interfere with value estimation.
- **Perception of professionalism:** Rainbow colormaps are often considered unscientific and unprofessional, even though they may perform well for this specific task. A diverging colormap is often a safer and more credible choice.

## Evaluate

- [ ] A smooth, sequential colormap (like a single-hue progression or `viridis`) is used, but the main goal is to compare overall texture or find regions of high gradient.
- [ ] The visualization feels "flat," and it's hard to quickly get a sense of which areas are more "active" or "variable" than others without close inspection.

## Repair

1.  **Switch to a diverging colormap.** If your data has a meaningful midpoint, change the colormap to a perceptually-ordered diverging scheme (e.g., blue-white-red). This introduces a central break and hue variation that helps reveal patterns.
2.  **Add contour lines.** If you must use a smooth, sequential colormap, consider explicitly adding contour lines on top of the visualization. This re-introduces the discrete boundaries that aid in pattern perception.
3.  **Use a better spectral scheme (with caution).** If the task absolutely demands it, replace a standard rainbow with a more perceptually-ordered spectral scheme like `viridis` or `cividis`, but be aware that you lose the very "discontinuities" that make this technique work. This is more of a fallback to the general "avoid rainbow" rule than a direct fix for this guideline.
