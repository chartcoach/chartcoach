## Guidance

To help viewers quickly and accurately identify the single greatest change between two datasets, use an animated transition that morphs one chart into the other.

## Why

The human visual system processes motion as a primitive feature. During an animated transition where all items move for the same duration, the velocity of a mark becomes an emergent and powerful visual signal that directly encodes the magnitude of its change. The fastest-moving object is the one with the largest change, allowing for rapid, pre-attentive identification without requiring conscious calculation or memory.

### Core Principle

Make the most important comparison the easiest to see. By mapping the data "delta" directly to the perceptual feature of "velocity," you offload a complex cognitive task (scan, compare, remember) into a simple perceptual one (spot the fastest object).

## When it applies

- **The primary task is to find the "biggest mover"** or identify the single data point with the maximum change (increase or decrease) between two states.
- You are comparing **exactly two datasets** or two points in time.
- The visualization is presented in an **interactive medium** (screen) that supports animation.
- This applies to chart types like **bar charts** and **donut/pie charts**.

## Exceptions

- **The task is to judge overall similarity or correlation.** Animation was found to be ineffective for this task, as velocity only highlights individual changes, not the collective pattern.
- **You are comparing more than 2-4 datasets simultaneously.** The cognitive load of tracking many moving objects at once quickly becomes overwhelming.
- **The medium is static (print, PDF).** Animation is not possible in these formats.
- **For slope charts (or line charts with few points),** overlaid static charts were found to be more effective than animation for this task.

## Trade-offs

- **Ephemeral:** The information is only available during the transition. It cannot be scrutinized in a static state.
- **Requires Interaction:** The user must trigger the animation to see the comparison.
- **Can be Disorienting:** If not designed carefully (e.g., without using easing or if changes are massive and uncontrolled), large movements can be jarring or difficult to follow.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Scanning Hell:** Viewers have to slowly scan back and forth between two static charts, trying to mentally measure and compare each corresponding pair of marks.
- **"Did anything change?":** Viewers struggle to notice or quantify changes between two states, especially when the changes are subtle.
- **Verbal requests for the "biggest mover":** If users have to ask "which one changed the most?", it's a sign that the visualization isn't making this comparison easy.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Use an Overlaid Chart.** If animation is not feasible, superimposing the two datasets (e.g., using different colors or opacities for the two series) is the next best static alternative. This co-locates the marks and reduces eye movement.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Implement Animated Transitions.** Introduce a control (like a toggle button or slider) that triggers a smooth, animated transition between the two chart states.
  - Use "cubic in-out" easing for the transition to make the acceleration and deceleration feel natural.
  - Ensure the transition duration is long enough to perceive movement but short enough to feel responsive (e.g., 1.5 seconds).

## Guidance

When the task is to compare the overall similarity or correlation between two bar charts, arrange them in a mirrored, back-to-back layout rather than a standard side-by-side layout.

## Why

The human visual system has a dedicated and highly efficient mechanism for detecting symmetry. By arranging two charts (e.g., bar charts) in a mirrored fashion, their combined form creates a single, larger shape. The degree of symmetry in this combined shape serves as a powerful perceptual proxy for the degree of correlation between the two datasets. A highly correlated pair creates a highly symmetric shape, which is easier to perceive than mentally comparing two separate patterns.

### Core Principle

Leverage the brain's built-in perceptual strengths. Instead of forcing viewers to perform a difficult cognitive task (pattern matching), transform the data into a format that a highly-tuned perceptual system (symmetry detection) can solve automatically.

## When it applies

- The primary task is to judge **overall correlation or similarity** between two datasets.
- You are comparing **exactly two datasets**.
- The chart type lends itself to mirroring, such as **bar charts**.

## Exceptions

- **Comparing more than two datasets.** A mirrored layout is fundamentally a binary comparison and does not scale to three or more series.
- **Task is to find a specific value or the largest change.** Other layouts, like animation or overlaid charts, are better for these tasks.
- **Audience unfamiliarity.** The layout is unconventional and may require a moment of orientation for viewers accustomed to standard small multiples.

## Trade-offs

- **Limited Scalability:** This technique is inherently limited to comparing two series.
- **Unconventional:** May be less immediately intuitive to a general audience without a brief period of adaptation compared to standard small multiples.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Incorrect Correlation Judgments:** Viewers struggle to determine which of several pairs of charts is more "similar" or "consistent."
- **Point-by-Point Comparison:** Viewers resort to slowly comparing individual bars one by one, rather than perceiving the overall pattern.
- **Animation is Ineffective:** If you're using animation for a correlation task, viewers will struggle because motion highlights individual deltas, not the global pattern.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Use an Adjacent Layout.** If a mirrored layout is too complex to implement, a standard side-by-side (adjacent) small multiple layout is better than a stacked layout. Ensure the charts share a common baseline and y-axis.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Implement a Mirrored Layout.**
  - For two horizontal bar charts, place them back-to-back, sharing a central y-axis. One chart's bars extend to the left, and the other's extend to the right.
  - The resulting "butterfly" or "pyramid" shape's symmetry will reveal the correlation.

## Guidance

For tasks that require comparing specific corresponding values between two datasets, prefer an overlaid (superimposed) layout over separate small multiples.

## Why

Placing data in the same visual space (co-location) makes comparison significantly more efficient. When charts are overlaid, corresponding data points are close together, allowing for direct perceptual judgment of their difference with minimal eye movement. In contrast, comparing values across separate small multiples requires the viewer to shift their gaze, encode the first value into working memory, shift their gaze again, find the corresponding value, and then perform a mental comparison. This process is slower, more cognitively demanding, and more prone to error.

### Core Principle

Reduce cognitive load by minimizing the distance—both physical and mental—required for comparison. The closer two things are in space, the easier they are to compare.

## When it applies

- The task involves **direct comparison of specific values** between two series (e.g., "Which product had higher sales in June?", "Find the biggest gap between Series A and Series B").
- This is particularly effective for **line charts** (or slope charts) and **bar charts**.
- The two datasets share the **same scale and units**.

## Exceptions

- **High data density or overplotting:** If the two series are very dense or cross over frequently, an overlaid chart can become a tangled mess, often called a "spaghetti plot." In this case, small multiples may provide more clarity despite the comparison cost.
- **Different scales or units:** If the two series have vastly different scales, overlaying them on the same axis would render one of them unreadably small. They must be plotted separately.
- **Task is to see each dataset individually:** If the primary goal is to understand the independent pattern of each series, and comparison is secondary, small multiples can be better as they present each series without interference.

## Trade-offs

- **Potential for Clutter:** Overlaid charts risk becoming cluttered and hard to read if the data series overlap significantly.
- **Loss of Individual Clarity:** It can be harder to focus on the shape and pattern of a single series when it's intertwined with another.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Back-and-Forth Eye Movements:** You observe viewers repeatedly shifting their gaze between two separate charts to make a single comparison.
- **"Spaghetti Plot":** The overlaid chart is so cluttered with crossing lines or overlapping marks that it's impossible to distinguish the individual series.
- **Memory Errors:** Viewers make mistakes when trying to recall a value from one chart to compare it with another.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Use Color and Opacity.** If your overlaid chart is cluttered, use distinct, perceptually separated colors for each series. You can also reduce the opacity of one or both series to make overlapping areas more legible.
- **Moderate Approach: Switch from Small Multiples to Overlaid.** If you currently have two separate charts for comparison, combine them into a single chart with two series distinguished by color.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Offer Both Views.** In an interactive setting, provide a control that allows the user to switch between a small multiple view (for individual clarity) and an overlaid view (for direct comparison), letting them choose the best layout for their current task.

## Guidance

For tasks requiring precise comparison, do not arrange small multiples in a vertical stack. Instead, place them side-by-side (horizontally adjacent).

## Why

Humans are far more accurate at comparing lengths or positions when the objects are aligned along a common baseline. When two charts are placed side-by-side (adjacent), their corresponding categories are vertically aligned, allowing for easy and accurate comparison. When they are stacked vertically, this crucial alignment is lost. The viewer must mentally project the length of a bar from the top chart down to the bottom chart, a task that is slow, cognitively demanding, and highly inaccurate.

### Core Principle

Judgments of position and length are most accurate along a common, aligned scale. Effective chart design facilitates this by aligning the elements that need to be compared.

## When it applies

- **Any task that requires precise comparison** of values, ranks, or patterns between two or more charts.
- You are using **small multiples** to show different datasets or categories.
- This is especially critical for **bar charts**, where length is the primary visual encoding.

## Exceptions

- **Layout Constraints:** If you are forced into a tall, narrow aspect ratio (like a mobile phone screen), a vertical stack may be the only feasible layout. Acknowledge that this will make precise comparison difficult.
- **Gist-level Comparison:** If the goal is simply to get a rough sense of the overall pattern in each chart and precise comparison is not important, a stacked layout may be acceptable.

## Trade-offs

- **Space Usage:** A side-by-side layout requires more horizontal space, which may not be available. A stacked layout is more compact vertically. However, this space efficiency comes at a severe cost to perceptual accuracy.

## Signs of Trouble

- **No Common Baseline:** Corresponding bars in the charts you want to compare do not start at the same vertical or horizontal position.
- **Gross Inaccuracy:** Viewers consistently make large errors when asked to compare values between the charts.
- **High Floor Effect in Experiments:** In the Ondov et al. study, participants performed so poorly with stacked charts that the task became too difficult to measure their true performance, indicating a fundamentally flawed layout for comparison.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Re-arrange the Layout.** The simplest fix is to change the arrangement from a vertical stack to a horizontal row. This immediately provides the common baseline needed for accurate comparison.
- **Moderate Approach: Use a Connecting Line or Shading.** If you must use a stacked layout, you can add faint guidelines that connect corresponding points across the charts. This helps guide the eye but does not fully resolve the lack of a common baseline.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Switch to an Overlaid Chart.** If you are only comparing two series, overlaying them onto a single chart is an even more effective way to facilitate comparison, as it minimizes the distance between corresponding marks.
