## Guidance

To support accurate lookup of specific values on a continuous map, use a colormap with a wide range of hue variation, such as a rainbow or spectral scheme.

## Why

When viewers estimate a quantity at a specific point on a color-coded map, their perception is affected by the colors of surrounding areas (an effect called simultaneous contrast). The study's results suggest that colormaps with substantial, non-monotonic changes in hue are most effective at counteracting these contrast effects, leading to more accurate value lookups. In contrast, colormaps designed to have monotonically increasing lightness (like spiral or sequential schemes) were found to be significantly less accurate for this specific task.

### Core Principle

Visual encodings for value lookup should prioritize minimizing perceptual errors from simultaneous contrast over maintaining monotonic luminance.

## When it applies

- The primary task for the viewer is quantity estimation (e.g., "What is the temperature at this specific location?" or "Find a place where the elevation is 750 feet").
- This guidance applies to continuous quantitative maps (e.g., weather maps, elevation maps) regardless of whether the underlying data is simple (low spatial frequency) or complex (high spatial frequency).

## Exceptions

- **Pattern Perception Task:** If the main goal is for viewers to perceive shapes, gradients, or fine-grained patterns, this type of colormap performs poorly. Use a diverging colormap with uniform luminance steps instead (see related guidelines).
- **Accessibility:** Standard rainbow colormaps are not accessible to users with common forms of color vision deficiency (CVD) because they rely on red-green distinctions. If CVD accessibility is a priority, a different, CVD-safe colormap should be used.
- **Ordered Judgements:** For tasks requiring viewers to easily judge the order of values (e.g., quickly identifying which of two points is higher), the non-perceptually-ordered nature of rainbow hues can be problematic.

## Trade-offs

- **False Boundaries:** High-hue-variation colormaps like rainbow can introduce sharp, artificial boundaries (especially in the yellow-green region) that viewers may misinterpret as abrupt changes in the data where none exist.
- **Poor Pattern/Shape Cues:** These colormaps are not effective for revealing the form, shape, or texture of the underlying data field because they lack a consistent luminance gradient, which is a primary cue for shape perception.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Inaccurate Lookups:** When asked to find a specific value on the map, users are consistently inaccurate.
- **Sub-optimal Colormap:** The map uses a greyscale, single-hue, or spiral colormap (e.g., cubehelix) when the primary task is to read specific values.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Switch the Colormap.** Change the existing colormap to one with greater hue variation. For example, switch from `greyscale` or `viridis` to a `spectral` or `rainbow` scheme.
- **Moderate Approach: Use a Perceptually-Uniform Rainbow.** Instead of a standard rainbow, use a perceptually-uniform variant that still covers a wide hue range but corrects for some of the perceptual distortions.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Provide Task-Based Controls.** If the visualization must support both value lookup and pattern perception, provide an interactive control that allows users to switch between a high-hue-variation colormap (for quantity estimation) and a diverging colormap (for pattern perception), with clear labels explaining the purpose of each.

## Guidance

When the task is to compare the rate of change (gradient) in complex, high-frequency spatial data, use a diverging colormap with chroma variation or a fully saturated hue-varying colormap.

## Why

On maps with simple, smooth data, viewers can perceive gradients equally well with almost any colormap, including greyscale. However, as data becomes more complex (i.e., has more fine-grained features), the task of judging steepness becomes more difficult. The study found that certain colormaps—specifically those with significant changes in saturation (diverging chroma) or hue (saturated rainbow)—provide stronger perceptual cues that help viewers distinguish gradients more accurately in these complex scenarios. Sequential and spiral colormaps did not offer this benefit.

## When it applies

- The analytical task is judging gradients, such as "Which of these two areas is steeper?" or "Where is the pressure changing most rapidly?".
- The underlying spatial data is complex or has high spatial frequency, meaning it contains many small, varied features rather than broad, smooth surfaces.

## Exceptions

- **Simple Data:** For maps with low spatial frequency (smooth, simple data), the choice of colormap has no significant effect on gradient perception. In this case, other factors like aesthetics or task suitability can take priority.
- **Other Tasks:** If the primary task is quantity estimation or detailed pattern matching, other colormaps are more effective.

## Trade-offs

- **Quantity Estimation:** These colormaps may not be the most accurate for reading specific values from the map. A full-spectrum rainbow is better for that task, but a diverging map is significantly worse.
- **Pattern Perception:** While helpful for gradients, these colormaps may not be optimal for perceiving the overall shape of longitudinal patterns. Diverging maps with uniform luminance steps are better for that specific task.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Poor Gradient Judgment:** On a map with complex, detailed data, users struggle to determine where the values are changing most quickly.
- **Wrong Colormap for the Job:** A sequential (e.g., single-hue) or spiral (e.g., cubehelix) colormap is used on a complex map where gradient perception is a key task.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Switch to a Better Colormap.** If the map is complex and gradient perception is important, replace the current colormap with a diverging one like `coolwarm` or a fully saturated one like `rainbow`.
- **Moderate Approach: Evaluate Data Complexity.** Analyze the spatial frequency of your data. If it is high, apply a `coolwarm` or `rainbow` colormap. If it is low, you can stick with a simpler sequential or greyscale map if it serves other goals better.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Offer User Controls.** In an interactive system, allow users to switch between a colormap optimized for quantity lookup (e.g., rainbow) and one optimized for structural analysis (e.g., coolwarm), as different tasks may be performed on the same data.

## Guidance

To support the perception of fine-grained, longitudinal patterns in complex spatial data, use a diverging colormap with equidistant luminance steps.

## Why

Perceiving the shape of a pattern, like a terrain profile, relies heavily on cues from shading and luminance. The study suggests that diverging colormaps with uniform luminance steps (which get darker toward both ends from a bright center) provide the "maximal perceptual resolution" needed to resolve fine-grained features in complex data. In contrast, colormaps without this structured luminance, such as rainbow, or those with only one direction of luminance change, like sequential and spiral ramps, failed to help viewers perceive patterns accurately in complex maps.

### Core Principle

Visualizing form and structure in complex data requires a colormap with a perceptually ordered and varied luminance gradient to provide clear shading cues.

## When it applies

- The primary task is to extract, identify, or match a fine-grained longitudinal pattern (e.g., "Which of these profiles matches the transect from A to B?").
- The underlying spatial data is complex, with high spatial frequency (many detailed features).

## Exceptions

- **Simple Data:** For simple, low-frequency data, the choice of colormap had little impact on pattern perception.
- **Quantity Estimation:** This type of colormap performs poorly for tasks that require reading specific numeric values. Use a high-hue-variation colormap for that.

## Trade-offs

- **Poor Quantity Estimation:** Diverging colormaps are significantly less accurate for quantity estimation tasks compared to colormaps with maximum hue variation (like rainbow). Viewers will struggle to read exact values.
- **May Be Less Intuitive for Magnitude:** A diverging scale is not ideal for representing continuous data that does not have a meaningful midpoint or zero.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Patterns Are Obscured:** Viewers cannot accurately identify or match patterns in a complex map, describing it as "noisy" or "hard to read."
- **Wrong Tool for the Job:** The map uses a rainbow, spiral (e.g., cubehelix), or sequential (e.g., single-hue) colormap for a pattern-matching task on complex, high-frequency data.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Change the Colormap.** Replace the current colormap with a diverging one that has uniform luminance steps, such as `coolwarm` or `spectral`.
- **Moderate Approach: Add a Profile Tool.** If changing the map's colormap is not feasible, add an interactive tool that lets users draw a line across the map and generates a separate 2D line chart of the profile. This offloads the pattern perception task to a more suitable chart type.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Provide Task-Oriented Views.** Design the system to offer multiple, coordinated views. Show the main map with a colormap optimized for quantity lookup, and provide a secondary view or lens that uses a diverging colormap to highlight structural patterns.

## Guidance

Recognize that there is no single 'best' colormap for all tasks on continuous quantitative maps. Instead, choose a colormap that is optimized for the viewer's primary analytical task, or provide multiple options if several tasks are critical.

## Why

Different analytical tasks rely on different perceptual processes, which are supported by different colormap characteristics.
1.  **Quantity Estimation** (reading a value) is hindered by simultaneous contrast effects, which are best counteracted by high hue variation (e.g., rainbow).
2.  **Pattern Perception** (seeing shape in complex data) relies on shading cues, which are best provided by structured luminance gradients (e.g., a diverging coolwarm scheme).

Using a single, general-purpose colormap (like a sequential or spiral ramp) results in sub-optimal performance for both of these critical tasks. Therefore, the colormap should be treated as a task-dependent tool, not a fixed data property.

### Core Principle

Visual encodings should be optimized for the specific perceptual task the viewer is performing, as different tasks have different perceptual requirements.

## When it applies

- When designing a visualization or system for analyzing continuous quantitative maps (e.g., weather, climate, or other scientific data).
- When the audience needs to perform multiple distinct types of analysis on the same map, such as reading exact values at some times and identifying structural features at others.

## Exceptions

- **Single-Task Visualizations:** If a visualization is designed to support only one specific task (e.g., it is always used for quantity lookup), then a single, optimized colormap is sufficient and appropriate.
- **Strict Brand/Style Guides:** If organizational standards rigidly enforce a single color palette, it may not be possible to offer multiple task-based options.

## Trade-offs

- **Increased Interface Complexity:** Providing users with controls to switch colormaps adds an element to the UI that must be designed, taught, and maintained.
- **Violates Simplicity:** This approach moves away from the simple "one size fits all" ideal and requires the designer and user to be more thoughtful about the analytical process.

## Signs of Trouble

- **One-Size-Fits-All Design:** A single, all-purpose colormap (e.g., greyscale, viridis) is used for an application that needs to support both accurate value lookups and fine-grained pattern analysis.
- **User Complaints:** Users express frustration that they either can't read exact values accurately or that they can't see the important details and patterns in the data.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Prioritize the Primary Task.** Identify the single most critical and frequent task your users perform. Choose a colormap optimized for that task and accept the trade-offs for other, less critical tasks. Clearly document this choice and its reasoning.
- **Moderate Approach: Provide Separate, Labeled Views.** If an interactive control is too complex, provide two static views of the same map side-by-side: one with a colormap for quantity estimation (labeled "For Reading Values") and one for pattern perception (labeled "For Seeing Patterns").
- **Comprehensive Approach: Implement Task-Based Controls.** In an interactive visualization, add a simple control (e.g., a dropdown or radio buttons) that allows the user to switch between task-optimized colormaps. Label the options by their purpose, not just their name (e.g., "Mode: Read Values" vs. "Mode: See Patterns").
