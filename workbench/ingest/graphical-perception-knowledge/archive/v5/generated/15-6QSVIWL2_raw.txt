## Guidance

For tasks requiring accurate comparison or ranking of quantitative values, encode data using position along a common, aligned scale. This is the most perceptually accurate visual encoding channel for this purpose.

## Why

Humans perceive and judge differences in position more accurately and with less error than differences in length, angle, or area. Using position for quantitative data leverages this innate ability, leading to more reliable and faster interpretation of the chart.

### Core Principle

Humans judge quantities more accurately by comparing positions along a common scale than by comparing unaligned lengths, angles, or areas.

## When it applies

- When the primary goal is for the viewer to **compare** values, **rank** categories, or **look up** a specific value.
- This is especially critical when the differences between values are small but meaningful.
- Applies to chart types like bar charts, dot plots, line charts, and scatter plots, which are based on this principle.

## Exceptions

- When showing **part-to-whole relationships** is the absolute primary goal and precise comparison accuracy is secondary, an area-based chart like a pie or donut chart might be considered. However, a bar chart often remains a more accurate alternative even for this task.
- When physical space is extremely limited and only a high-level overview is sufficient, less accurate encodings might be used as a compromise.

## Trade-offs

- **Space:** Charts using position (like bar charts) often require more linear space (either horizontal or vertical) than more compact charts (like pie charts or treemaps).
- **Part-to-Whole:** While bar charts can show parts of a whole, they may be less effective at quickly communicating the "part-of" relationship compared to a pie chart, even if the pie chart is less accurate for comparisons between slices.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Inaccurate Comparisons:** Viewers struggle to determine which of two similar-sized pie slices or bubbles is larger without checking the legend or labels.
- **Misleading Rankings:** A visual ranking of elements (e.g., bubbles by size) does not match the actual data ranking due to perceptual error.
- **High Cognitive Load:** Viewers must read data labels to make basic comparisons because the visual encoding itself is not precise enough.
- **Chart Choice:** You have used a pie chart, donut chart, or bubble chart for a task that requires precise comparisons.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** If you must keep a less effective chart (e.g., a pie chart), add direct labels with the exact values to each element (e.g., labeling pie slices with percentages). This provides an "escape hatch" for accuracy by allowing viewers to read the numbers instead of interpreting the visuals.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Switch to a Position-Based Chart.** For the most effective improvement, replace the chart with one that uses position on a common scale.
  - Replace a **pie chart** or **donut chart** with a **bar chart** or **dot plot**.
  - Replace a **bubble chart** with a **bar chart** or a **dot plot**. This ensures that comparisons are anchored to a common, reliable scale.

## Guidance

When designing treemaps or other visualizations that use rectangular area for comparison, avoid forcing all rectangles to have a 1:1 (square) aspect ratio. Allowing for a mix of aspect ratios can improve perceptual accuracy.

## Why

Experiments show that people make larger judgment errors when comparing the areas of perfect squares versus rectangles with varied aspect ratios. It is hypothesized that viewers use the 1D lengths of the sides as a visual shortcut to help estimate 2D area. This strategy is least effective when comparing squares, where a small change in side length produces a larger, squared change in area, leading to greater perceptual error.

### Core Principle

Human perception of 2D area is imperfect and often relies on 1D heuristics (like edge length). Design choices that allow these heuristics to function, even if unintentionally, can sometimes improve accuracy over a geometrically "perfect" layout.

## When it applies

- When using **treemaps**, **unit charts**, or **waffle charts** where quantitative values are encoded as the area of rectangles.
- When the primary goal is for users to **compare** the sizes of the different rectangles.

## Exceptions

- If the primary goal of the treemap is to serve as a compact, tile-like index and precise area comparison is not a priority.
- If the aesthetic goal of a perfectly tiled, grid-like appearance outweighs the need for perceptual accuracy in comparisons.

## Trade-offs

- Allowing for more varied aspect ratios might lead to layouts with very long, thin rectangles. These can be difficult to label and their own area can be hard to judge. The goal is to avoid an over-optimization for perfect squares, not to create extremely elongated shapes.
- A "squarified" layout is often more compact and aesthetically regular. Relaxing this constraint may lead to a more "jagged" or inefficient use of space.

## Signs of Trouble

- **A Grid of Squares:** Your treemap looks like a near-perfect grid of squares, indicating the layout algorithm was aggressively optimized for a 1:1 aspect ratio.
- **Comparison Difficulty:** Users struggle to accurately rank the sizes of two similarly-sized square-ish rectangles without reading their tooltips or labels.
- **Algorithm Default:** You are using the default "squarify" algorithm in a visualization library without considering its perceptual implications for comparison tasks.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Rich Tooltips.** If you cannot change the layout, ensure that hovering over any rectangle provides a clear tooltip with its category, exact value, and its percentage of the total. This provides a necessary escape hatch for users who cannot make the comparison visually.

- **Moderate Adjustment: Relax Layout Constraints.** When using a treemap layout algorithm (e.g., in a library like D3.js), adjust its parameters to relax the "squarify" constraint. Allow the algorithm to produce a mix of rectangular aspect ratios rather than strictly optimizing for squares.

- **Comprehensive Re-evaluation: Consider a Different Chart Type.** If precise and accurate comparison is the most critical task, a treemap may not be the best choice. A **bar chart** or **dot plot**, while using more space, would provide far greater perceptual accuracy for ranking and comparison tasks by using position instead of area.
