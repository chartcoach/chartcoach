## Guidance

Do not use horizon graphs for similarity comparison tasks where patterns should be considered similar regardless of their amplitude (height) or vertical offset (y-position).

## Why

Horizon graphs use discrete colored bands to represent ranges of values. A small, continuous change in a signal's amplitude or offset can cause it to cross a band boundary, resulting in an abrupt, dramatic change of color. Viewers perceive this large color change as a significant difference, making the visualization highly sensitive to a signal's vertical position and scale. This conflicts directly with the goal of amplitude/offset invariance.

### Core Principle

A visual encoding should not create large, abrupt perceptual changes in response to small, continuous changes in the underlying data, especially when those data changes are meant to be analytically ignored.

## When it applies

- When the analytical goal is to find patterns with similar shapes, regardless of their absolute values or vertical position.
- When working with similarity measures that are invariant to amplitude and offset, such as those that use z-normalization.

## Exceptions

- This guidance should be ignored when amplitude and vertical offset **are** meaningful and critical features for determining similarity. In that case, the sensitivity of horizon graphs becomes a desirable feature, not a bug.

## Trade-offs

- **Amplitude Sensitivity vs. Temporal Insensitivity:** While horizon graphs are poor for amplitude-invariant tasks, they are perceptually more robust to temporal warping (shifts in time) than line charts or colorfields. You must choose which invariance is more important for your task.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Banding Cliff:** Two patterns with nearly identical shapes and values are perceived as completely different because one lies just above a band threshold (e.g., blue) and the other lies just below it (e.g., red).
- **Flatline Exaggeration:** Signals with low amplitude (close to the baseline) are shown in different colors, exaggerating their differences, while high-amplitude signals might all fall into the same dark band, obscuring their differences.
- **Algorithm Mismatch:** When using a z-normalized similarity algorithm, users consistently reject the top results because the visualization makes them look different in amplitude.

## How to Improve

- **Switch to a Line Chart or Colorfield:** If amplitude and offset invariance is the primary goal, switch to a standard line chart or a colorfield (heatmap). These visualizations are perceptually more robust to these transformations, as they rely on relative shape and continuous color gradients, respectively, rather than discrete color bands.
- **Re-evaluate the Analytical Goal:** If a horizon graph is required for other reasons (e.g., space constraints), consider whether the analytical goal should be changed to one where amplitude and offset are indeed meaningful.
