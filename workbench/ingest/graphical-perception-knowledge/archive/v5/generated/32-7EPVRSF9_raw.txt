## Guidance

To make comparisons between bars easier and more accurate, place the bars you want users to compare as close to each other as possible, ideally adjacent.

## Why

The human ability to perceive small differences in bar heights—the Just Noticeable Difference (JND)—decreases as the distance between the bars increases. When bars are close, viewers can accurately judge their heights by comparing their top positions along a common scale. As the distance grows, this comparison becomes more difficult and requires a larger difference to be noticed, increasing the risk of misinterpretation.

### Core Principle

Make the most important comparisons the easiest to see. In bar charts, physical proximity directly aids the perceptual accuracy of length and position comparisons.

## When it applies

- When the primary user task is to compare or rank the values of different categories in a bar chart.
- When you have control over the ordering of the categories and can group them logically.
- When precise comparison is more important than maintaining a pre-existing (e.g., alphabetical) order.

## Exceptions

- When the categories have an intrinsic, meaningful order (e.g., time periods, age groups, or a pre-defined scale) that should not be broken. In this case, the logical order of the data takes precedence over facilitating arbitrary comparisons.

## Trade-offs

- **Breaks logical order:** Reordering bars to facilitate specific comparisons may disrupt a familiar or logical sequence (like alphabetical or chronological), which could confuse users who expect that order.
- **Can't optimize for all comparisons:** Optimizing the layout for one comparison (e.g., placing Bar A next to Bar B) may make another comparison harder (e.g., Bar A vs. Bar Z).

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Gap Problem:** The key bars that need to be compared are separated by many other bars, forcing viewers to scan back and forth.
- **User Indecision:** Viewers report difficulty telling which of two distant bars is taller or express low confidence in their judgment.
- **"Looks the Same":** Two bars with slightly different values appear to be the same height because they are too far apart.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Use Highlighting.** If you cannot reorder the bars, use color or a connecting line to visually link the bars being compared, helping the eye track across the distance.
- **Moderate Redesign: Reorder the Bars.** If the category order is not fixed, sort the chart to bring the bars of interest adjacent to one another. For example, you could sort the entire chart by value.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Small Multiples or a Slope Chart.** If the goal is to compare a few specific pairs of values, a more direct visualization may be better. A slope chart (or dot plot) can explicitly connect the values, making the comparison trivial and removing the distance problem entirely.

## Guidance

When a chart contains elements with very similar values (e.g., two bars of nearly the same height), add secondary visual cues like data labels, annotations, or ordinal icons to make the small differences explicit and perceptible.

## Why

Human perception has limits; we cannot reliably distinguish very small differences in length, angle, or area. This threshold is known as the Just Noticeable Difference (JND). Relying on perception alone for these "below-JND" comparisons leads to high error rates and misinterpretation. Explicitly annotating these differences offloads the work from fallible perception to reliable cognitive reading, ensuring accuracy.

### Core Principle

Don't force users to perceive what is imperceptible. When visual discrimination is unreliable, provide a direct textual or symbolic escape hatch to ensure clarity.

## When it applies

- When a dataset contains quantitative values that are very close to one another.
- When the precise comparison or ranking of these close values is important for the user's task.
- In charts where perceptual accuracy is known to be low (e.g., pie charts, bubble charts).

## Exceptions

- **Overview Visualizations:** In charts designed to show a high-level "gist" or overall pattern, where the exact ranking of similar items is unimportant.
- **Clutter Risk:** When adding annotations would create excessive visual clutter that harms the overall readability and aesthetic appeal of the chart more than it helps.

## Trade-offs

- **Increased Clutter:** Adding annotations, especially text labels, increases the amount of visual information on the chart, which can make it feel cluttered or overwhelming.
- **Shift from Perception to Reading:** Annotations shift the user's task from rapid perceptual judgment ("which is bigger?") to slower, deliberate reading. This increases accuracy but can reduce the speed of overall chart interpretation.

## Signs of Trouble

- **"Identical Twins":** Two or more chart elements (bars, pie slices, bubbles) look identical or nearly identical, making ranking impossible without a ruler.
- **User Hesitation:** Viewers express uncertainty, squint, or take a long time when asked to compare two specific elements.
- **High Error Rate:** When tested, users frequently make mistakes when ranking or comparing elements with small differences.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Data Labels.** For the few elements that are difficult to distinguish, add direct data labels (e.g., "42.1%" vs. "42.3%"). This is the most direct way to resolve ambiguity.

- **Moderate Approach: Use Interactive Tooltips.** If the chart is interactive, use tooltips that appear on hover to reveal the exact value. This keeps the chart clean while providing accuracy on demand.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Change Chart Type or Add a Table.** If precise comparison of many items is crucial, the chosen chart type may be inappropriate. Switch to a visualization that excels at precise comparison (like a sorted bar chart or dot plot) or supplement the chart with a sorted table that lists the exact values.

## Guidance

Recognize that comparing the size of marks (bubbles) is perceptually difficult. Use bubble charts to show general patterns and outliers, not for tasks requiring precise ranking or comparison of the values encoded by size.

## Why

Humans are poor at accurately comparing 2D areas, and this perceptual weakness is exaggerated by both the absolute size of the shapes and the distance between them. Unlike bar charts which share a common baseline for easy comparison, bubbles are free-floating, forcing viewers to make difficult area judgments. This leads to high error rates and low confidence when comparing similarly-sized bubbles.

### Core Principle

Visual encodings that rely on area or size are less accurate for quantitative comparison than those that rely on position along a common scale.

## When it applies

- When using a bubble chart or any scatterplot where a quantitative value is encoded by the size/area of the marks.
- When users need to compare or rank the values represented by the bubbles.

## Exceptions

- **Showing General Patterns:** When the goal is to show a general pattern, correlation, or clustering, and precise comparison between individual bubbles is not required.
- **Finding Extreme Outliers:** Bubble charts are effective at making the very largest or smallest bubbles pop out, which is useful for identifying extreme values in a dataset.

## Trade-offs

- **Information Density:** Bubble charts are visually engaging and can effectively show three dimensions of data on a 2D plane (x-position, y-position, size). Sacrificing them for a more perceptually accurate chart type (like a bar chart) for the 'size' variable may mean losing the ability to show one of the other data dimensions or the relationship between them.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Ambiguous Blobs:** It is impossible to tell which of two similarly-sized bubbles is larger, especially if they are on opposite sides of the chart.
- **Ranking Failure:** A user cannot confidently rank the top 5 largest bubbles by size without reading tooltips or labels.
- **Cluttered Overlap:** In dense areas of the chart, bubbles overlap so much that their individual sizes cannot be judged.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Interactive Tooltips.** In an interactive chart, provide tooltips on hover that reveal the exact value represented by the bubble's size. This provides an accuracy "escape hatch" on demand.
- **Moderate Redesign: Create a Companion Chart.** Supplement the bubble chart with a simple, sorted bar chart or table that shows the values from the 'size' dimension. This allows for both the overview (bubble chart) and precise comparison (bar chart).
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Choose a Different Chart Type.** If precise comparison and ranking are the primary tasks for the 'size' variable, do not use a bubble chart. Use a bar chart, dot plot, or a simple sorted list instead. This prioritizes perceptual accuracy over information density.
