## Guidance

To help viewers quickly and accurately identify the single greatest change between two datasets, use an animated transition that morphs one chart into the other.

## Why

The human visual system processes motion as a primitive feature. During an animated transition where all items move for the same duration, the velocity of a mark becomes an emergent and powerful visual signal that directly encodes the magnitude of its change. The fastest-moving object is the one with the largest change, allowing for rapid, pre-attentive identification without requiring conscious calculation or memory.

### Core Principle

Make the most important comparison the easiest to see. By mapping the data "delta" directly to the perceptual feature of "velocity," you offload a complex cognitive task (scan, compare, remember) into a simple perceptual one (spot the fastest object).

## When it applies

- **The primary task is to find the "biggest mover"** or identify the single data point with the maximum change (increase or decrease) between two states.
- You are comparing **exactly two datasets** or two points in time.
- The visualization is presented in an **interactive medium** (screen) that supports animation.
- This applies to chart types like **bar charts** and **donut/pie charts**.

## Exceptions

- **The task is to judge overall similarity or correlation.** Animation was found to be ineffective for this task, as velocity only highlights individual changes, not the collective pattern.
- **You are comparing more than 2-4 datasets simultaneously.** The cognitive load of tracking many moving objects at once quickly becomes overwhelming.
- **The medium is static (print, PDF).** Animation is not possible in these formats.
- **For slope charts (or line charts with few points),** overlaid static charts were found to be more effective than animation for this task.

## Trade-offs

- **Ephemeral:** The information is only available during the transition. It cannot be scrutinized in a static state.
- **Requires Interaction:** The user must trigger the animation to see the comparison.
- **Can be Disorienting:** If not designed carefully (e.g., without using easing or if changes are massive and uncontrolled), large movements can be jarring or difficult to follow.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Scanning Hell:** Viewers have to slowly scan back and forth between two static charts, trying to mentally measure and compare each corresponding pair of marks.
- **"Did anything change?":** Viewers struggle to notice or quantify changes between two states, especially when the changes are subtle.
- **Verbal requests for the "biggest mover":** If users have to ask "which one changed the most?", it's a sign that the visualization isn't making this comparison easy.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Use an Overlaid Chart.** If animation is not feasible, superimposing the two datasets (e.g., using different colors or opacities for the two series) is the next best static alternative. This co-locates the marks and reduces eye movement.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Implement Animated Transitions.** Introduce a control (like a toggle button or slider) that triggers a smooth, animated transition between the two chart states.
  - Use "cubic in-out" easing for the transition to make the acceleration and deceleration feel natural.
  - Ensure the transition duration is long enough to perceive movement but short enough to feel responsive (e.g., 1.5 seconds).
