## Guidance

When visualizing time-series data for similarity comparison tasks, choose a visualization technique whose perceptual properties match the desired analytical "invariance." For example, if local shifts in time should be ignored, use a visualization that de-emphasizes precise temporal alignment.

## Why

Different visualizations cause people to perceive similarity differently. The visual encoding can either highlight or mask specific types of data deformations, such as temporal shifts (warping), amplitude scaling, or vertical offsets. Aligning the visualization with the analytical goal (e.g., the invariance of a chosen similarity algorithm like DTW or z-normalized Euclidean distance) ensures that what viewers see as "similar" matches what the algorithm calculates as "similar," reducing confusion and improving trust in the results.

### Core Principle

The notion of similarity is not an absolute; it is dependent on the visualization used to display the data. An effective visualization for similarity search is one that makes the desired invariances easy to perceive and the undesired variations salient.

## When it applies

- When using automated or semi-automated tools to find similar patterns in time-series data (e.g., in EEG analysis, financial data, or sensor logs).
- When asking users to visually validate or rank the results of a similarity search algorithm.
- When designing interactive systems for querying time-series data by example.

## Exceptions

- For elementary tasks like looking up a single value at a specific point in time, this principle is less critical, as the goal is not pattern comparison.

## Trade-offs

- **Performance vs. Perceptual Alignment:** The visualization that best matches the desired invariance may not be the fastest or easiest to read. For example, horizon graphs align well with temporal warping but can have a higher cognitive load and lead to slower task times than line charts.
- **Specificity vs. Generality:** A visualization chosen for a specific invariance (e.g., horizon graph for temporal warping) may perform poorly for other invariances (e.g., amplitude scaling).

## Signs of Trouble

- **Algorithm-Human Mismatch:** Users consistently disagree with the rankings provided by a similarity search algorithm, indicating a mismatch between what they perceive as similar and what the algorithm is designed to find.
- **Feature Fixation:** Users are getting hung up on features that should be ignored for the task at hand (e.g., complaining that two otherwise identical patterns are "different" because one is slightly shifted in time).
- **Low Inter-Rater Agreement:** When multiple users are asked to judge similarity, their answers are inconsistent, suggesting the visualization is ambiguous or does not guide them to a common interpretation of similarity.

## How to Improve

- **For Temporal Warping Invariance (e.g., using DTW):** If patterns should be considered similar even if they are slightly stretched, compressed, or shifted in time, **use a horizon graph.** Its dual encoding helps users see high-level patterns while being less sensitive to precise x-axis alignment.
- **For Amplitude/Offset Invariance (e.g., using z-normalization):** If patterns should be considered similar regardless of their height or vertical position, **use a line chart or colorfield.** Avoid horizon graphs, as their rigid color bands make them highly sensitive to vertical changes.
- **For Precise Matching (e.g., using Euclidean Distance):** If patterns must match point-by-point in both time and value, **a standard line chart** is often most effective, as it accurately represents both dimensions without the abstractions of other techniques.
