## Guidance

Do not use stacked area, stacked bar, or stacked line charts when the primary task is for a viewer to judge the presence or strength of a positive correlation between the component series.

## Why

In stacked charts, only the bottom-most series is aligned to a flat, common baseline. All other series are placed on top of a fluctuating, irregular baseline created by the series below them. This "floating baseline" problem makes it extremely difficult to judge the values and trends of the upper segments, as their perceived shape is distorted. This distortion introduces significant perceptual error, making it nearly impossible to accurately assess if the components are correlated.

### Core Principle

Humans judge quantities and trends most accurately when they are aligned to a common, straight baseline. Stacked charts violate this principle for all but the bottom-most series, impeding accurate comparison and correlation judgment.

## When it applies

- When you have two or more quantitative series and want to show if they are positively correlated with each other.
- When comparing the trends or patterns of individual components within a total.

## Exceptions

- Stacked charts are primarily intended for showing **part-to-whole composition** and the **trend of the total sum**. If showing the changing composition or the total is far more important than comparing the components to each other, a stacked chart may be acceptable. However, it is the wrong tool for the correlation task.

## Trade-offs

- While poor for correlation between parts, stacked charts are effective at showing the total value and its general composition. Switching to a different chart type (like a line chart) means losing the ability to easily see the sum of the components.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Floating Baseline Illusion:** Viewers cannot tell if the component series are moving together or in opposition. The chart seems to suggest a relationship that isn't actually in the data (or vice-versa) because of the misleading effect of the shifting baselines.
- **Incorrect Task-Tool Fit:** The chart is used to support a claim about the relationship between two of the *parts*, rather than the relationship of the parts *to the whole*.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Acknowledge the Limitation.** If you must use a stacked chart, add a note of caution that it is not suitable for comparing the trends of the individual components, only for seeing their contribution to the total.

- **Moderate Approach: Unstack the Chart.** Switch from a stacked chart to a grouped bar chart or a standard line chart with multiple lines. This places all series on a common baseline, enabling accurate comparison and correlation judgment, but sacrifices the part-to-whole view.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Use Small Multiples.** To show both the total and the individual trends accurately, use a "small multiples" approach. Show the total in a main stacked chart, and then show each individual component as a separate, smaller line or area chart below it. This provides the best of both worlds: a clear view of the whole and an accurate, un-distorted view of each part.
