## Guidance

When the primary goal is to help viewers accurately judge the correlation between two quantitative variables, use a scatterplot.

## Why

Scatterplots encode two variables using position on the X and Y axes. The resulting pattern's shape—specifically, the "width" of the cloud of points—provides a direct and consistent visual cue for correlation strength. This encoding aligns well with fundamental human perceptual abilities for judging spatial properties. Unlike other chart types, this visual cue is symmetric, meaning it works equally well for both positive and negative correlations.

### Core Principle

Humans judge quantities more accurately by comparing positions along a common scale than by comparing other visual attributes. The shape of a scatterplot effectively translates the statistical concept of correlation into a perceivable "width" or "elongation" of the point cloud, which our visual system is adept at judging.

## When it applies

- When your main message is about the relationship (strength and direction) between two quantitative variables.
- When you want to enable viewers to accurately perceive and compare correlation values.

## Exceptions

- When you have a very large number of data points, a standard scatterplot may suffer from overplotting, obscuring the density and true relationship. In this case, a density-based variation is preferable.
- If the task is to show composition (part-to-whole) or a time-series trend for a single variable, other chart types like a bar chart or line chart are more appropriate.

## Trade-offs

- **Overplotting:** With large datasets, individual points can merge into an indistinguishable blob, hiding density and patterns.
- **Limited to two variables:** A standard scatterplot can only show the relationship between two variables at a time. A scatterplot matrix (SPLOM) is needed for more variables, which can become unwieldy.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Overplotting:** Your chart is a solid mass of color, and you can't distinguish areas of high or low density.
- **Misinterpretation:** Viewers consistently under- or over-estimate the strength of the correlation.
- **Task Mismatch:** The chart is being used to show something other than a relationship, such as a ranking or a trend over time, for which it is not optimized.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Adjust Opacity.** If overplotting is an issue, reduce the opacity of the points. This allows overlapping points to create darker areas, revealing the density of the data.

- **Moderate Approach: Use a Density Plot.** For very large datasets, switch to a binned scatterplot (often shown as a 2D histogram or a heatmap) or a contour plot. These charts explicitly visualize the density of points, resolving the overplotting issue.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Use a Scatterplot Matrix (SPLOM).** If you need to show correlations among several variables, a SPLOM presents a grid of scatterplots for every pair of variables, providing a comprehensive overview.
