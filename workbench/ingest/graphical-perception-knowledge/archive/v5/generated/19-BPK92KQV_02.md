## Guidance

For tasks that require comparing specific corresponding values between two datasets, prefer an overlaid (superimposed) layout over separate small multiples.

## Why

Placing data in the same visual space (co-location) makes comparison significantly more efficient. When charts are overlaid, corresponding data points are close together, allowing for direct perceptual judgment of their difference with minimal eye movement. In contrast, comparing values across separate small multiples requires the viewer to shift their gaze, encode the first value into working memory, shift their gaze again, find the corresponding value, and then perform a mental comparison. This process is slower, more cognitively demanding, and more prone to error.

### Core Principle

Reduce cognitive load by minimizing the distance—both physical and mental—required for comparison. The closer two things are in space, the easier they are to compare.

## When it applies

- The task involves **direct comparison of specific values** between two series (e.g., "Which product had higher sales in June?", "Find the biggest gap between Series A and Series B").
- This is particularly effective for **line charts** (or slope charts) and **bar charts**.
- The two datasets share the **same scale and units**.

## Exceptions

- **High data density or overplotting:** If the two series are very dense or cross over frequently, an overlaid chart can become a tangled mess, often called a "spaghetti plot." In this case, small multiples may provide more clarity despite the comparison cost.
- **Different scales or units:** If the two series have vastly different scales, overlaying them on the same axis would render one of them unreadably small. They must be plotted separately.
- **Task is to see each dataset individually:** If the primary goal is to understand the independent pattern of each series, and comparison is secondary, small multiples can be better as they present each series without interference.

## Trade-offs

- **Potential for Clutter:** Overlaid charts risk becoming cluttered and hard to read if the data series overlap significantly.
- **Loss of Individual Clarity:** It can be harder to focus on the shape and pattern of a single series when it's intertwined with another.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Back-and-Forth Eye Movements:** You observe viewers repeatedly shifting their gaze between two separate charts to make a single comparison.
- **"Spaghetti Plot":** The overlaid chart is so cluttered with crossing lines or overlapping marks that it's impossible to distinguish the individual series.
- **Memory Errors:** Viewers make mistakes when trying to recall a value from one chart to compare it with another.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Use Color and Opacity.** If your overlaid chart is cluttered, use distinct, perceptually separated colors for each series. You can also reduce the opacity of one or both series to make overlapping areas more legible.
- **Moderate Approach: Switch from Small Multiples to Overlaid.** If you currently have two separate charts for comparison, combine them into a single chart with two series distinguished by color.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Offer Both Views.** In an interactive setting, provide a control that allows the user to switch between a small multiple view (for individual clarity) and an overlaid view (for direct comparison), letting them choose the best layout for their current task.
