## Guidance

When visualizing scalar fields (e.g., heatmaps, geographic maps) where the primary task is for viewers to compare the overall "steepness" or average gradient, prefer a diverging colormap (like "cool-warm") over a smooth, sequential one (like "viridis").

## Why

Diverging colormaps create apparent discontinuities or "hue bands" in the visualization. Viewers subconsciously use these bands as a perceptual shortcut, estimating the size and frequency of the distinct color patches to judge the net gradient more effectively. A smooth, continuous palette lacks these perceptual "anchors," making the same summary task more difficult.

### Core Principle

Visual 'artifacts' like hue bands, often considered harmful, can sometimes serve as beneficial perceptual aids for complex summary or ensemble tasks by providing features for the visual system to latch onto.

## When it applies

- When the main goal is comparing the magnitude of first-order derivatives (gradients, rates of change) across 2D quantitative fields.
- For summary-level or holistic "gist" comparisons of the data's structure, rather than precise value lookups.
- This applies even at high spatial frequencies, where details in the scalar field are fine and dense.

## Exceptions

- If the primary task is reading exact values at specific points, a smooth, perceptually uniform sequential colormap is generally safer as it avoids creating artificial boundaries that could be misinterpreted.
- If the data is purely sequential and has no meaningful central anchor point (e.g., zero, an average, a baseline), a diverging colormap is semantically inappropriate and can be misleading.

## Trade-offs

- This approach prioritizes perceptual sensitivity for a specific summary task (gradient perception) at the potential cost of clarity for other tasks (like value lookup).
- Choosing a diverging colormap for purely sequential data may confuse viewers who expect the color scheme to reflect the data's structure.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Looks the Same" Problem:** Your scalar fields contain important differences in gradient, but to the naked eye they appear nearly identical when rendered with a smooth, monotonic colormap.
- **Failed Comparison:** Users have difficulty describing which of two maps looks "busier," "steeper," or has more "activity," even when there are known underlying differences.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** If you must use a sequential colormap, add explicit contour lines. This manually introduces the perceptual edges that a diverging colormap provides automatically, helping to segment the visual field and aid gradient perception.

- **Moderate approach:** Switch from a smooth sequential colormap to a well-designed diverging colormap, such as "cool-warm" or a blue-to-red scheme from ColorBrewer. Ensure the central, neutral color is mapped to a meaningful midpoint in your data.

- **Comprehensive approach:** For a repeated expert task, analyze the data distribution and design a custom colormap that intentionally introduces sharp hue transitions or discrete color steps at specific, perceptually meaningful thresholds. This transforms the "artifact" of hue banding into a calibrated analytical instrument.

## Guidance

For specific expert analysis tasks like comparing the average gradient in scalar fields, acknowledge that a spectral (rainbow) colormap can be surprisingly effective. This does not mean it should be a default, but that its blanket prohibition may overlook its utility in narrow, specialized contexts.

## Why

The very 'flaw' that makes rainbow colormaps problematic for many tasks—their numerous, sharp, and unevenly spaced hue bands—can become an asset for gradient perception. These bands create strong perceptual artifacts, segmenting the image into many small patches. Experts can subconsciously use the perceived texture, size, and frequency of these patches as a powerful heuristic to judge the overall 'activity' or 'steepness' of the data field.

### Core Principle

The effectiveness of a visual encoding is highly task-dependent. A feature that is a flaw in one context (e.g., an artificial boundary for value lookup) can be a feature in another (e.g., a perceptual proxy for gradient).

## When it applies

- In expert-driven, scientific visualization contexts where the users are highly trained and the task is very specific (e.g., comparing structural differences in simulation outputs).
- When the primary goal is to maximize sensitivity to small changes in gradient, and other tasks like value lookup are secondary.
- When the visualization is intended for a controlled audience for which accessibility issues have been addressed or are not a primary constraint.

## Exceptions

- **Never** use a rainbow colormap as a general-purpose default. Its flaws are numerous and well-documented.
- Do not use for any visualization intended for a general audience, publication, or presentation where accessibility cannot be guaranteed.
- Avoid for tasks that require perceiving order, judging magnitude, or looking up precise values, as the non-uniform perceptual steps will distort the data.

## Trade-offs

- **Clarity for Sensitivity:** You gain sensitivity for gradient perception at a significant cost to data fidelity, perceptual uniformity, and order.
- **Accessibility:** Using a rainbow colormap inherently excludes users with common forms of color vision deficiency. This is a major ethical and practical trade-off.
- **Interpretability:** The chart becomes a specialized instrument for one task, but is highly likely to be misinterpreted if used for any other purpose.

## Signs of Trouble

- **False Boundaries:** Viewers point to sharp color changes (e.g., from yellow to green) and interpret them as significant events in the data, when they are merely artifacts of the colormap.
- **Lost Detail:** In a colorblindness simulation, large sections of the colormap collapse into indistinguishable bands of yellow and brown, rendering the chart useless.
- **Misjudged Magnitude:** A user incorrectly assumes that the perceptual distance between red and orange is the same as between green and cyan.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** If you find rainbow is effective for your task but need to mitigate its harms, add a secondary visualization with a perceptually uniform grayscale or sequential colormap. This provides a 'ground truth' reference for users.
- **Moderate approach:** Replace the rainbow colormap with a well-designed diverging scheme (e.g., 'cool-warm'). The 2019 study showed it offers the same performance benefits for gradient perception without as many of the severe downsides of a full rainbow.
- **Comprehensive approach:** Deconstruct why the rainbow colormap is effective for your task (e.g., the number and location of hue bands) and design a custom, perceptually-principled colormap that replicates those specific features without the extra baggage of a full, un-ordered spectrum.
