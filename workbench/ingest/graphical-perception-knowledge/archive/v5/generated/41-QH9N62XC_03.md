## Guidance

When using a line chart to visualize a correlation between two quantitative variables, ensure the data points are sorted along the horizontal axis according to that x-axis variable. Do not plot them in an arbitrary or nominal order.

## Why

A standard line chart connects points in a given sequence. If this sequence is arbitrary (e.g., alphabetical by category), the resulting line can be a tangled, jagged mess that completely obscures any underlying correlation. Sorting the points by the x-axis variable transforms this chaos into a coherent visual pattern. A positive correlation will appear as a generally upward-trending line, and a negative correlation as a downward-trending line, making the relationship much easier for the visual system to perceive.

### Core Principle

Reducing visual noise and creating coherent, monotonic shapes enhances perceptual accuracy. Sorting transforms a potentially chaotic series of line segments into a more meaningful shape that directly maps to the statistical concept of correlation.

## When it applies

- When you are using a line chart to visualize the relationship between two quantitative variables (e.g., height vs. weight).
- When the order of the points on the x-axis is not otherwise meaningful or fixed (e.g., it is not a time series).

## Exceptions

- This guidance is **not** for time-series data. For time-series data, the chronological order of the x-axis is fundamentally meaningful and must be preserved. Re-sorting a time series by the y-axis value would destroy its meaning.

## Trade-offs

- Sorting by the x-axis variable means you lose the original ordering of the data points (if any), which might be important context. This technique is only suitable when showing the correlation is the primary message.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Jagged Scribble:** A line chart intended to show a relationship between two variables looks like a chaotic, jagged scribble with no clear direction.
- **Categorical X-Axis:** The x-axis consists of unordered categories (e.g., countries, products), but a line is used to connect the points, incorrectly implying a continuous relationship between adjacent categories. In this case, a bar chart is usually more appropriate.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Re-order the Data.** Before plotting, sort your source data by the column you will place on the x-axis. This simple data preparation step will produce a much clearer, "ordered" line chart.

- **Moderate Approach: Switch to a Bar Chart.** If the x-axis is categorical, a bar chart is often a better choice. Sorting the bars by their value (the y-axis) will create a clear pattern for ranking and comparison, which is a more appropriate task than correlation for categorical data.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Switch to a Scatterplot.** The standard and most effective chart type for showing correlation between two quantitative variables is a scatterplot. It does not depend on point order and is purpose-built for this task. Use a scatterplot to avoid any ambiguity.
