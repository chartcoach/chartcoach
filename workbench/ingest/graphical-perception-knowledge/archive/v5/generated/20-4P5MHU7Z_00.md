## Guidance

Do not ask viewers to compare the sizes of two segments that are stacked directly on top of each other. Such comparisons are highly inaccurate and prone to systematic underestimation.

## Why

When two bar segments are adjacent in a stack, viewers exhibit a strong perceptual bias to perform a "part-to-whole" comparison. Instead of comparing the size of one segment to the other, they subconsciously compare one segment to the combined total of both. For example, when comparing a segment of size 30 to an adjacent segment of size 60, viewers tend to perceive the ratio closer to 33% (30 / 90) rather than the correct 50% (30 / 60).

### Core Principle

Visual context can trigger unintended perceptual strategies. Forcing a length comparison in a context that strongly implies composition (stacking) leads to predictable errors.

## When it applies

- When creating stacked bar charts where a primary analysis task is to compare the magnitude of adjacent segments within the same bar.
- This applies to both 100% stacked bar charts and standard stacked bar charts.

## Exceptions

- If the primary goal of the chart is to show the total value of the stack and the general composition of that total, a stacked bar is appropriate. This guidance is specifically for the task of *comparing adjacent segments*. If that comparison is secondary, the bias may be an acceptable trade-off.

## Trade-offs

- Following this guidance may require changing the chart type, which could use more screen space (e.g., a grouped bar chart) or make it harder to see the total of the stack.
- Adding gaps to mitigate the bias can slightly distort the visual perception of the total length of the bar.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Task Mismatch:** The chart is a stacked bar, but the key insight or caption requires comparing two adjacent segments.
- **The 33-vs-50 Test:** When presented with two adjacent segments with a 1:2 ratio, do viewers estimate the ratio closer to 33% than 50%? This indicates the part-to-whole bias is active.
- **Misleading Annotations:** The visualization uses callouts or arrows to prompt a comparison between two adjacent stacked segments.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach: Add a Gap.** Introduce a small, visible gap between the segments you want to compare. This visually breaks the "part-to-whole" gestalt and encourages a more direct and accurate length-to-length comparison.

- **Moderate approach: Use a Grouped Bar Chart.** Switch the chart type to a grouped bar chart. This places all bars on a common baseline, which is the most perceptually accurate way to facilitate comparisons of magnitude.

- **Comprehensive approach: Use Dot Plots or Dumbbell Plots.** For pure comparison tasks, consider a dot plot. If the main goal is to show the difference between two values in a pair, a dumbbell plot is even better, as it is explicitly designed to highlight the gap between two points.
