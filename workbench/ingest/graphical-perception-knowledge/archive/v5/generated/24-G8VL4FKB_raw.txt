## Guidance

For tasks requiring the comparison or ranking of proportions, encode quantitative values using position along a common scale (e.g., bar charts, dot plots) rather than using angle (e.g., pie charts, donut charts).

## Why

Humans are significantly more accurate at perceiving and comparing quantities represented by position along a common axis than by angle. Experiments show that angle judgments have nearly double the error rate of position judgments, making it difficult for viewers to accurately compare, rank, or judge part-to-whole relationships in pie charts.

### Core Principle

Humans judge quantities more accurately by comparing positions along a common scale than by comparing angles, areas, or colors. Choosing a visual encoding that is higher on this "perceptual hierarchy" leads to more accurate and effective visualizations.

## When it applies

- When a primary user task is to accurately compare the magnitude of different categories or parts of a whole.
- When you need to rank categories by value.
- When there are more than 2-3 categories to display.

## Exceptions

- If the primary goal is simply to emphasize that values are parts of a single whole, and there are very few slices (2-3), a pie chart can be acceptable.
- When precise comparison is less important than a quick, general impression of proportions (e.g., "about half" or "a small fraction").

## Trade-offs

- Bar charts or dot plots may take up more screen or page space than a single, compact pie chart.
- A bar chart may be less effective at communicating the "part-of-a-whole" relationship at a single glance, as it doesn't form a complete visual circle.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Ambiguous Slices:** Viewers cannot reliably tell which of two similar-sized slices in a pie chart is larger.
- **Hidden Differences:** Small but important differences between categories are not visually apparent.
- **Difficult Ranking:** It's hard to confidently rank all slices from smallest to largest without reading labels.
- **Inaccurate Sums:** It's difficult to judge if two or more slices combined are larger or smaller than another slice.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** If you must use a pie chart, add direct percentage and/or value labels to each slice. This provides an "escape hatch" for users, allowing them to read the exact values instead of relying on flawed angle perception.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Switch to a Bar or Dot Plot.** Replace the pie chart with a bar chart or dot plot. This encodes the data using position on a common scale, which is perceptually superior. For added clarity, sort the bars or dots by value (descending or ascending) to make ranking effortless.

## Guidance

When the primary task is to compare the magnitudes of individual categories, use chart types that place all values on a common baseline (e.g., grouped bar charts, dot plots). Avoid relying on comparisons of unaligned lengths, as is required for most segments in a stacked bar chart.

## Why

Humans are poor at accurately judging and comparing the lengths of objects that are not aligned to a common baseline. In a stacked bar chart, only the bottom-most series of segments shares a common baseline (the axis). Comparing any other segments requires a difficult and error-prone mental judgment of length, which is a less accurate perceptual task than judging position.

### Core Principle

Comparing positions along a common, straight scale is one of the most accurate elementary perceptual tasks. Designs that enable this task will be more effective than designs that require judging length, angle, or area.

## When it applies

- When the primary task is to compare the values of individual components across different parent categories (e.g., comparing product sales for 'Product X' across all regions).
- When ranking a specific component's value across all parent categories is an important task.

## Exceptions

- When the primary task is to compare the total of the stacks and to see the approximate composition of each total. Stacked bar charts are effective for showing part-to-whole relationships across categories.
- When using a 100% stacked bar chart, where the task is to compare proportions rather than absolute values. In this case, the top and bottom of the chart provide a common baseline.

## Trade-offs

- Grouped bar charts use more horizontal space than stacked bar charts.
- Grouped bar charts make it harder to see and compare the total sum of the components for each parent category.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Difficult Comparisons:** It's hard to tell which of two segments in the middle of different stacks is larger.
- **Impossible Ranking:** Trying to rank the size of a specific component (e.g., the "blue" segment) across all the main bars is difficult or impossible.
- **Distorted Trends:** In a stacked area chart (the time-series equivalent), the perceived trend of an inner series is distorted by the series below it.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** Add direct data labels to each segment in the stacked bar chart. This allows viewers to read and compare exact numbers, bypassing the flawed perceptual task.

- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Grouped Bar Chart.** Change the chart from a stacked to a grouped format. This places every single bar on the same zero-baseline, making all comparisons direct and accurate.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Small Multiples.** Create a separate, simple bar chart for each component you want to compare. Arrange these charts in a grid. This provides maximum clarity for comparing a specific component across all parent categories, though it uses the most space.

## Guidance

When encoding quantitative data, choose a visual variable from as high up the perceptual hierarchy as possible. The general order from most to least accurate is:

1.  Position on a common scale
2.  Position on non-aligned scales
3.  Length / Direction / Angle
4.  Area
5.  Volume / Curvature
6.  Shading / Color Saturation

## Why

Not all visual encodings are created equal. The human visual system decodes some (like position) with high accuracy and little systematic bias, while others (like area or color saturation) are judged less accurately and are more prone to error. Aligning your most important data with the most accurate encodings makes your chart more effective and reduces the chance of misinterpretation.

### Core Principle

A graphical form that involves elementary perceptual tasks that lead to more accurate judgments will result in better organization and increase the chances of a correct perception of patterns and behavior in the data.

## When it applies

- When designing any visualization where the accurate perception of quantitative data is important for the user's task.
- When deciding which of several possible chart types is best for a given comparison or ranking task.

## Exceptions

- Less accurate encodings are often necessary and acceptable for secondary variables in a multivariate visualization. For example, using position for the two main variables on a scatter plot and area or color (less accurate) for a third or fourth variable is a standard and effective technique.
- Sometimes the visual metaphor requires a lower-ranked encoding. For example, a choropleth map must use area and/or color to show a geographic pattern.

## Trade-offs

- Higher-ranked encodings (especially position) can take up more space. A bar chart is typically less compact than a treemap (which uses area).
- Overloading a chart with too many high-priority encodings can lead to clutter. A key part of design is deciding which variables are most important and deserve the best encodings.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Imprecise Impressions:** The visualization gives a general impression (e.g., "this is big") but fails to reveal more specific relationships or rankings.
- **Struggling to Compare:** Users find it difficult to make precise comparisons (e.g., "Is A about 1.5x or 2x bigger than B?").
- **Hidden Patterns:** The chosen encoding obscures small but important differences in the data that would be visible with a more accurate encoding.

## How to Improve

- **Perform a Channel Audit:** Identify the primary task you want users to perform (e.g., "compare sales across regions"). Then, identify which visual channel (position, length, area, color, etc.) is encoding that data.

- **Upgrade the Channel:** If the primary task is supported by a low-ranked channel (like area in a bubble chart), consider if a different chart type could use a higher-ranked channel (like position in a bar chart) to accomplish the same task more effectively.

## Guidance

When designing layouts where comparison is a key task, minimize the spatial distance between the graphical elements being compared.

## Why

The human visual system can make more accurate comparisons between adjacent or nearby objects. As the distance between two objects increases, viewers must scan their eyes back and forth and rely more on working memory to hold one value while viewing the other. This process is slower and introduces perceptual and cognitive errors.

### Core Principle

Reducing the distance for visual comparisons reduces the cognitive load required to perform the task, leading to faster and more accurate insights.

## When it applies

- When choosing the sort order for categories in a bar chart, dot plot, or list. Sorting by value places similar-sized items next to each other.
- When laying out small multiples in a grid. A compact grid (e.g., 3x4) is often better for comparison than a long, linear one (e.g., 1x12).
- When designing grouped bar charts or dot plots.

## Exceptions

- When a specific, non-value-based ordering is more important for the task. For example, sorting categories alphabetically is better for lookup tasks. Sorting by time (chronologically) is necessary to see trends.
- When the layout is constrained by other factors, such as a geographical map.

## Trade-offs

- Sorting by value to improve comparison will disrupt any "natural" order (like alphabetical or chronological), which can make lookup tasks more difficult. The optimal sort order depends on the primary task.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Back-and-Forth Scanning:** You notice viewers have to scan their eyes repeatedly across a wide area of the chart to make a single comparison.
- **Ruler Use:** Viewers try to use their finger or a piece of paper as a straight-edge to compare two distant elements.
- **Difficulty with Extremes:** It is hard to compare the first and last bars in a long, alphabetically sorted bar chart.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Highlighting or Guide Lines.** If you cannot change the layout, use color or faint background lines to visually connect the distant items you want the user to compare. This guides the eye and reduces the scanning effort.

- **Moderate Redesign: Re-sort the Data.** If the primary task is comparison or ranking, sort your chart by the quantitative value (descending or ascending). This is the single most effective way to improve comparisons in a bar or dot chart.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Re-think the Layout.** For small multiples, reconsider the grid arrangement. Could a more compact grid bring key comparisons closer? Could you overlay two key plots instead of placing them side-by-side?
