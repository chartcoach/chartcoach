## Guidance

Always visualize your data as a primary step in analysis. Do not rely solely on summary statistics to understand the relationships and distributions within a dataset.

## Why

Summary statistics like mean, standard deviation, and correlation coefficients can be dangerously misleading. They can be identical for datasets that are qualitatively very different, masking important features like non-linear relationships, outliers, or distinct subgroups. Visual inspection is often the only way to discover these critical patterns, validate the appropriateness of a statistical model, and avoid making false assumptions.

### Core Principle

Summary statistics abstract data; visualization reveals it. Trusting abstraction without revelation risks serious misinterpretation.

## When it applies

- During any form of exploratory data analysis.
- Before committing to a statistical model (e.g., before running a linear regression).
- When communicating findings to an audience, to ensure they understand the underlying data shape, not just a single summary number.

## Exceptions

- In highly automated, large-scale systems where individual visual inspection of every datapoint is impossible. Even in these cases, however, visualization of data samples and model diagnostic plots is crucial for quality assurance.

## Trade-offs

- Visualization takes more time and effort than simply computing summary statistics.
- The interpretation of visualizations can be subjective, whereas statistics are objective (though, as noted, potentially misleading without visual context).

## Signs of Trouble

- **Blind Analysis:** Conclusions are drawn or models are built based purely on statistical outputs (e.g., R-squared values, p-values) without ever plotting the data.
- **Hidden Patterns:** A summary statistic (like the mean) is heavily skewed by one or two outliers that are not seen or acknowledged.
- **Model Mismatch:** A linear model is applied to a clearly non-linear relationship because the correlation coefficient alone seemed "high enough".
- **Over-reliance on Tables:** Reports or dashboards consist entirely of tables of numbers, with no graphical representations.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Generate a Scatterplot or Histogram.** For any two variables you are correlating, create a simple scatterplot. For any single variable, create a histogram or density plot. This is the fastest way to spot non-linearities, outliers, or multi-modal distributions.
- **Moderate Approach: Create a Scatterplot Matrix (SPLOM) or Pairs Plot.** For multivariate datasets, use a scatterplot matrix to quickly visualize the pairwise relationships and distributions between all variables in one grid.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Integrate Visualization into Your Default Workflow.** Adopt an exploratory data analysis (EDA) mindset where visualization is the first step, not an afterthought. Use tools that automatically suggest relevant visualizations to lower the barrier to routine visual inspection.
