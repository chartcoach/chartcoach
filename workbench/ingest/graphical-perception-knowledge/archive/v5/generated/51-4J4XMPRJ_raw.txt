## Guidance

Do not use mark size to encode a quantitative value in a scatterplot (i.e., a bubble chart) if viewers need to accurately estimate the average position of the points.

## Why

Humans have a perceptual bias called the "Weighted Average Illusion." Our brains give more weight to larger, more salient objects when mentally calculating an average position. This pulls the perceived mean away from the true statistical mean and towards the location of the larger marks, leading to inaccurate conclusions about the data's central tendency.

### Core Principle

Visual encodings can interfere with each other, and channels that create more salient marks (like size) can disproportionately influence the perception of other channels (like position).

## When it applies

-   When creating scatterplots where a third quantitative variable is encoded using mark size (bubble charts).
-   When a primary task for the viewer is to judge the average value (mean) of the primary X and Y variables.
-   When comparing the mean position of different groups or classes within a scatterplot.
-   The risk of bias is highest when there is a strong correlation between mark position and mark size (e.g., larger bubbles are clustered in one corner).

## Exceptions

-   If the primary task is to look up individual values or compare the relative sizes of specific marks, and estimating the overall mean is not important.
-   If the size encoding represents a statistical weight that *should* influence the viewer's judgment of the mean (e.g., a population-weighted average). In this case, the "illusion" aligns with the intended interpretation, but you should be explicit about this.

## Trade-offs

-   By not using size, you lose a perceptually precise and intuitive channel for showing quantitative differences between individual points.
-   You may need to use a different, potentially less biasing channel (like color lightness) or a different chart type (like small multiples), which might increase complexity or take up more space.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Clustered Bubbles:** Larger bubbles are systematically located in one area of the plot (e.g., top-right corner), indicating a high correlation and a high risk of bias.
-   **High Size Variance:** The chart contains both very small and very large bubbles. This wide encoding range amplifies the bias.
-   **Mean Misjudgment:** When you ask viewers where the "average" point is, their answer is consistently skewed towards the cluster of larger points.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Add an Explicit Mean.** If you must use a bubble chart, add a visible marker for the true mean. Drawing explicit cross-hairs or a distinct point that represents the calculated average provides a correct anchor for viewers and helps counteract the bias.

-   **Moderate Redesign: Switch the Encoding.** Use a less biasing channel like color lightness instead of size to encode the third variable. The bias effect is still present for lightness but is significantly weaker.

-   **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Small Multiples.** Instead of encoding a third variable with size, facet the data into a grid of standard scatterplots (small multiples). For example, bin the third variable into categories (e.g., "Small," "Medium," "Large") and show a separate, un-biased scatterplot for each. This allows for accurate mean estimation within each facet without interference.

## Guidance

When using color lightness (e.g., a sequential color scale) to encode a quantitative value in a scatterplot, be aware that it can bias the viewer's perception of the average position.

## Why

Similar to the bias from mark size, our brains give more perceptual weight to darker, more salient marks. This "Weighted Average Illusion" pulls the perceived mean away from the true statistical mean and towards the location of the darker marks. While the effect is weaker than with size, it can still lead to misinterpretations of the data's center.

### Core Principle

Visual salience influences summary perception. Marks that stand out more (like darker points) are given more weight in subconscious mental calculations.

## When it applies

-   When designing scatterplots where a quantitative variable is encoded using a sequential color scheme (e.g., light gray to dark blue).
-   When judging the overall average (mean) position of the points is a key analytical task.
-   The bias is most pronounced when there is a high correlation between position and lightness (i.e., darker points are clustered in one region of the plot).

## Exceptions

-   If the primary task is looking up individual values or identifying clusters of dark/light points, and mean estimation is secondary.
-   The bias from lightness is weaker than from size, so it may be an acceptable trade-off if a third visual channel is necessary and accuracy of the mean is not critical.

## Trade-offs

-   Lightness is an intuitive way to show ordered data. Mitigating the bias might require using a narrower color range, which reduces the dynamic range for distinguishing values.
-   To eliminate the bias, you may have to remove the color encoding, losing a dimension of information.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Clustered Darkness:** Darker-colored points are systematically grouped in one area of the plot.
-   **Wide Lightness Range:** The color scale spans a very large range from near-white to pure black. This high contrast can amplify the bias.
-   **Subjective Feedback:** Viewers describe the "center" of the data as being located where the darkest points are.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Use a Narrower Lightness Range.** Avoid color ramps that go from extremely light (near-white) to extremely dark (near-black). Choosing a narrower range (e.g., from 75% to 25% lightness) reduces the perceptual difference between marks and mitigates the bias.

-   **Moderate Redesign: Add an Explicit Mean.** If you must use a wide lightness range, add a visible, distinct marker (e.g., cross-hairs) at the true mean's location. This provides a correct visual anchor for viewers to compare against.

-   **Comprehensive Redesign: Use a Different Channel or Chart.** If mean estimation is critical, do not encode a third variable in the same plot. Use faceting (small multiples) instead. Alternatively, if the third variable is categorical, use a qualitative color palette (based on hue) which does not have the same ordering bias.

## Guidance

If you must encode a third quantitative variable in a scatterplot and the task involves judging the mean position, use color lightness (a sequential color scale) instead of mark size (a bubble chart).

## Why

Both size and lightness can bias the perceived mean position, pulling it toward larger or darker marks (the "Weighted Average Illusion"). However, experimental evidence shows this biasing effect is significantly more pronounced for size than for lightness. Choosing lightness is a "lesser of two evils" that minimizes this perceptual distortion while still allowing a third variable to be encoded.

### Core Principle

Different visual channels have different perceptual properties and potential for interference. Choose the channel that minimizes perceptual bias for the primary analytical task.

## When it applies

-   When designing a trivariate scatterplot and you need to choose between using size or color lightness for the third variable.
-   This is most critical when a primary goal for the user is to accurately estimate or compare the average position of all points in the plot.

## Exceptions

-   If the primary task is comparing the magnitude of individual marks. Size is a more perceptually precise channel than lightness for making one-to-one comparisons of quantity.
-   If the audience has color vision deficiencies, size may be a more accessible option than a sequential lightness ramp, as it does not rely on color perception. In this case, be sure to add an explicit marker for the mean to counteract the bias.

## Trade-offs

-   You are deliberately choosing a channel with lower perceptual bias (lightness) at the cost of one with higher precision for individual value lookups (size). This prioritizes the accuracy of summary statistics over the accuracy of individual mark comparisons.

## Signs of Trouble

-   You are currently using a bubble chart (size encoding) for a task where the data's central tendency is a key takeaway (e.g., comparing the "average" patient profile across two treatments). Viewers' interpretation of the "average" is likely biased.

## How to Improve

-   **Direct Swap:** The most direct way to apply this guidance is to change the encoding for the third variable from `size` to `color`. In declarative visualization tools (like Vega-Lite, ggplot2, etc.), this is often a one-line change in the specification. Ensure you use a sequential, single-hue color scheme.
-   **Combined Approach:** Use color lightness and also add an explicit marker for the true mean position to provide the most accurate possible representation for this task.
