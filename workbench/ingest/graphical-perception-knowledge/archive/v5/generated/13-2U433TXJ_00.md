## Guidance

Use bar charts, which encode quantitative values by position and length along a common scale, for tasks that require comparing or ranking values. Avoid using pie charts, which encode values using angles and areas.

## Why

Humans are significantly more accurate and faster at judging differences in length along a common baseline than they are at judging differences in angles or areas. This fundamental principle of graphical perception means that bar charts lead to fewer errors and less ambiguity when comparing values.

### Core Principle

Humans judge quantities more accurately by comparing positions along a common scale than by comparing angles, areas, or colors. Visual encodings should be chosen based on their ranking in this perceptual hierarchy to maximize accuracy for the given analytical task.

## When it applies

- When the primary task is to compare or rank the magnitude of different categories.
- When you need to show precise differences between values.
- When you have more than 2-3 categories to compare.

## Exceptions

- When showing a part-to-whole relationship for a very small number of categories (e.g., 2 or 3 slices) and precise comparison is **not** the primary goal, a pie chart can be acceptable. Its primary function in this case is to communicate a simple composition, like "Category A is about half of the total."
- When the data adds up to a meaningful total (100% or a whole number) and you want to emphasize the part-to-whole relationship.

## Trade-offs

- **Clarity vs. Familiarity:** While bar charts are perceptually superior, some audiences may have a strong (though often misguided) expectation for pie charts when seeing part-to-whole data.
- **Space:** A bar chart typically requires more rectangular screen space than a compact, circular pie chart.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Similar Slices:** The chart has multiple slices that are close in value (e.g., 22%, 24%, 25%), making them nearly impossible to rank visually.
- **The "Over 5 Slices" Rule:** If your pie chart has more than 5-7 slices, it almost certainly becomes an unreadable "color wheel" where comparisons are meaningless.
- **3D or Exploded Pies:** The use of 3D perspective or "exploding" slices distorts the angles and areas, further degrading perceptual accuracy.
- **Frequent Legend Lookups:** Viewers have to constantly look back and forth between the chart and the legend to understand which slice represents which category and what its value is.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** If you must use a pie chart, add direct labels with the category name and exact value (e.g., "Category A: 42%") to each slice. This provides an escape hatch, allowing users to read the values instead of relying on flawed angle/area perception.
- **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Bar Chart.** The most effective improvement is to convert the pie chart into a bar chart. This immediately leverages a more accurate visual encoding (length on a common scale).
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Sort the Bar Chart.** After converting to a bar chart, sort the bars in ascending or descending order. This further reduces cognitive load by making the ranking of categories immediately obvious without requiring multiple comparisons.
