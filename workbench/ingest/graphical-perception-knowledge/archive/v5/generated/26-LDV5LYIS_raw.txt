## Guidance

For judging the correlation between two quantitative variables, use a scatterplot instead of a parallel coordinate plot (PCP).

## Why

Scatterplots lead to significantly more accurate and faster perception of correlation compared to parallel coordinate plots. A controlled study found that viewers could reliably distinguish twice as many different correlation levels when using scatterplots. The same study also found that parallel coordinate plots can introduce a bias toward perceiving negative correlations, even when none exist.

### Core Principle

Humans perceive correlation more effectively through the 2D spatial arrangement of points (as in a scatterplot) than by tracking the angles and crossovers of lines between parallel axes.

## When it applies

- When the primary goal for the viewer is to assess the strength and direction (positive, negative, or none) of the relationship between two continuous variables.
- When choosing between a scatterplot and a parallel coordinate plot for displaying a bivariate relationship.

## Exceptions

- **Multivariate Analysis:** When the goal is to visualize relationships between *more than two* variables simultaneously. Parallel coordinate plots are designed for multivariate data and can reveal complex patterns that a simple scatterplot cannot. However, for any specific pair of variables, a scatterplot is still better for judging correlation.
- **Other Tasks:** When the primary task is not correlation but rather identifying clusters, outliers, or ranges across many variables. Parallel coordinate plots can be effective for these exploratory tasks.

## Trade-offs

- **Variable Limitation:** While superior for bivariate correlation, a single scatterplot is limited to two variables. To show correlations across many variables, you must use a scatterplot matrix (SPLOM), which can become cluttered.
- **Dimensionality vs. Clarity:** A parallel coordinate plot can display many variables in a single, compact view, but sacrifices the perceptual clarity needed to accurately judge the correlation between any single pair of them. A scatterplot prioritizes this clarity for just one pair.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Inaccurate Judgements:** Viewers consistently over- or under-estimate the strength of a correlation when using a parallel coordinate plot.
- **Negative Bias:** Viewers tend to report negative correlations more often than actually exist when using a PCP.
- **Slow Interpretation:** It takes viewers a long time to determine the relationship between two variables on a parallel coordinate plot, often requiring them to trace many individual lines.

## How to Improve

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use a Scatterplot.** If the primary task is to show the correlation between two specific variables, replace the parallel coordinate plot with a scatterplot. Encode the two variables on the x and y axes respectively.

- **Hybrid Approach: Supplement with a Scatterplot.** If you must use a parallel coordinate plot for a broader multivariate analysis, consider supplementing it with a linked scatterplot. This allows users to select two axes on the PCP to see the corresponding relationship displayed clearly in a scatterplot, giving them the best of both worlds.
