## Guidance

To improve the legibility of complex scatterplots, assign colors with the highest luminance contrast against the background to the classes that are most spatially intermingled or overlapping with other classes.

## Why

Classes that are spatially mixed with others have low spatial separability and are inherently difficult to perceive as distinct groups. Boosting their color contrast against the background provides a strong perceptual cue that compensates for the spatial ambiguity, making these "messy" classes more visible and easier to distinguish. This reduces the cognitive load required to parse the chart.

### Core Principle

Make the hardest-to-see elements the easiest to distinguish by giving them the strongest perceptual cues. In this case, color contrast is used to compensate for a lack of spatial separation.

## When it applies

- When creating multiclass scatterplots where two or more class distributions overlap significantly.
- When the number of classes or data points is high, increasing the likelihood of visual clutter and overlap.
- When the primary task is to identify, count, or compare the different classes present in the visualization.

## Exceptions

- If all classes in the scatterplot are well-separated with no spatial overlap, this guidance is less critical, and color assignment can be based on other factors like semantic meaning or aesthetic preference.
- When specific colors are required for certain classes due to domain conventions (e.g., branding, established standards), and these required colors have low contrast.

## Trade-offs

- You may need to assign a perceptually high-contrast (but perhaps less aesthetically pleasing or semantically relevant) color to a class simply because it is spatially messy.
- This might conflict with the goal of assigning similar colors to semantically related groups if one of those groups is highly overlapped. Visibility must be prioritized.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Disappearing Class:** One or more classes are very difficult to see because their color (e.g., light yellow on a white background) blends in with the chart background, especially in dense or overlapping regions.
- **Ambiguous Clouds:** You can see a dense cloud of points but cannot easily tell if it's one class or multiple overlapping classes because the colors are not distinct enough from each other or the background.
- **The Squint Test:** Squinting at the chart causes one or more of the classes to completely vanish.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Manual Swap.** Identify the most intermingled or "lost" class and manually swap its color with the highest-contrast color available in your palette (e.g., swap yellow for dark blue).

- **Moderate Approach: Luminance Sort.** When assigning colors from your palette, sort them by luminance contrast against your background. Assign the highest-contrast colors to the classes you identify as most overlapped, and work your way down.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Algorithmic Optimization.** Use a tool or algorithm that models class overlap (e.g., based on nearest neighbors) to systematically find a color assignment that maximizes an objective function balancing background contrast for overlapping classes and distinctness between adjacent ones, as described in the source paper.

## Guidance

When assigning categorical colors in a multiclass scatterplot, avoid assigning perceptually similar colors (e.g., blue and purple) to distinct clusters that are spatially next to each other. Instead, maximize the color difference between adjacent groups.

## Why

The human visual system is sensitive to boundaries. When two adjacent clusters have similar colors, the boundary between them becomes less salient. This can cause viewers to misinterpret them as a single, larger cluster or struggle to determine where one group ends and the other begins. Using highly dissimilar colors for neighboring groups creates a strong visual border, reinforcing their separateness.

### Core Principle

Visual encoding differences should be at least as large as the data differences you want to show. If two groups of data are distinct, their visual representations should also be clearly distinct.

## When it applies

- When distinct (non-overlapping) clusters in a scatterplot are located very close to one another.
- When the task requires viewers to accurately judge the shape, size, or number of clusters.

## Exceptions

- If all clusters are spatially far apart from each other, the color similarity of non-adjacent clusters is not an issue.
- This guideline may be overridden by the need to assign high-contrast colors to *other* clusters that are highly overlapping (see `prioritize-contrast-for-overlapping-clusters`). The source paper suggests that ensuring visibility of overlapping clusters is often a higher priority.

## Trade-offs

- You may have to break a semantic or ordered color grouping to ensure adjacent clusters are visually distinct. For example, you might not be able to place two shades of blue next to each other, even if they represent related categories.
- In a palette with a limited number of highly distinct colors, you may be forced to place two less-distinct colors adjacent to each other.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Blurry Boundaries:** When looking at the chart, it is difficult to tell where one cluster ends and an adjacent one begins.
- **Accidental Merges:** Two or more distinct clusters appear to merge into a single, larger shape because their colors are too similar (e.g., a teal cluster next to a light green one).
- **Back-and-Forth:** A viewer has to look back and forth between the legend and the chart to confirm that two adjacent, similarly-colored groups are, in fact, different classes.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Identify and Swap.** Find the two adjacent clusters with the most similar colors. In your color palette, swap the color of one of them with a color that is most different from the other.

- **Moderate Approach: Pairwise Check.** Before finalizing color assignments, create a quick visualization of your palette where each color is shown next to every other color. Avoid assigning any two colors that look too similar in this test to clusters that are adjacent in your scatterplot.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Spatial-Aware Assignment.** Use an optimization tool that considers the spatial location of each class. The algorithm should penalize solutions that place perceptually similar colors on spatially adjacent clusters, as demonstrated in the source paper.
