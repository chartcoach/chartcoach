## Guidance

When visualizing correlation with a parallel coordinates plot, it is a highly effective choice for showing **negative** correlation. However, avoid it for visualizing **positive** correlation, as viewer perception is significantly less accurate.

## Why

The visual representation of correlation is fundamentally different for positive and negative relationships in a parallel coordinates plot.
- **Negative Correlation:** A strong negative correlation creates a clear, easily perceived focal point where all the lines cross over between the two axes.
- **Positive Correlation:** A strong positive correlation creates a series of roughly parallel lines. This pattern is harder to distinguish from the visual noise of weaker correlations.

This asymmetry in visual cues leads to a dramatic asymmetry in perceptual performance. The "crossover" is a salient, unique pattern, while "parallel-ness" is not.

### Core Principle

The effectiveness of a visualization depends on the salience and consistency of its visual cues. A chart that provides a clear, distinct visual pattern for a specific task (like the crossover point for negative correlation) will be far more effective for that task.

## When it applies

- When visualizing relationships between multiple quantitative variables and you want to highlight or enable the discovery of strong negative correlations.
- When evaluating the effectiveness of a parallel coordinates plot for a correlation task.

## Exceptions

- If you must show both positive and negative correlations in the same multi-axis view and cannot change the chart type or invert axes, this asymmetry is an inherent limitation you must accept. The primary purpose may be something other than correlation judgment, such as identifying clusters or outliers.

## Trade-offs

- Prioritizing negative correlation with this chart comes at the cost of accurately representing positive correlations.
- Parallel coordinates plots can become cluttered and unreadable with many data points (overplotting).

## Signs of Trouble

- **Asymmetric Insight:** Viewers are able to spot negative correlations but consistently misjudge or miss obvious positive ones.
- **Spaghetti Chart:** The plot is a spaghetti-like mess of overlapping lines, making it impossible to see any pattern at all, whether positive or negative.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Invert an Axis.** If you want to highlight a strong positive correlation, invert one of the corresponding axes. A positive correlation will now appear as a strong negative correlation (a clear crossover point), making it much easier to perceive. Be sure to clearly label the inverted axis to prevent misinterpretation.

- **Moderate Approach: Use Brushing and Linking.** Implement interactivity that allows users to select a subset of lines (a "brush"). Highlighting this subset and fading out the rest can reduce clutter and reveal patterns that were previously hidden.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Use a Symmetric Chart Type.** For a general-purpose correlation visualization tool, use a scatterplot matrix (SPLOM) or a correlation matrix heatmap. These provide a symmetric and consistent representation for all pairs of variables, regardless of the correlation's direction.
