## Guidance

When using color lightness (e.g., a sequential color scale) to encode a quantitative value in a scatterplot, be aware that it can bias the viewer's perception of the average position.

## Why

Similar to the bias from mark size, our brains give more perceptual weight to darker, more salient marks. This "Weighted Average Illusion" pulls the perceived mean away from the true statistical mean and towards the location of the darker marks. While the effect is weaker than with size, it can still lead to misinterpretations of the data's center.

### Core Principle

Visual salience influences summary perception. Marks that stand out more (like darker points) are given more weight in subconscious mental calculations.

## When it applies

-   When designing scatterplots where a quantitative variable is encoded using a sequential color scheme (e.g., light gray to dark blue).
-   When judging the overall average (mean) position of the points is a key analytical task.
-   The bias is most pronounced when there is a high correlation between position and lightness (i.e., darker points are clustered in one region of the plot).

## Exceptions

-   If the primary task is looking up individual values or identifying clusters of dark/light points, and mean estimation is secondary.
-   The bias from lightness is weaker than from size, so it may be an acceptable trade-off if a third visual channel is necessary and accuracy of the mean is not critical.

## Trade-offs

-   Lightness is an intuitive way to show ordered data. Mitigating the bias might require using a narrower color range, which reduces the dynamic range for distinguishing values.
-   To eliminate the bias, you may have to remove the color encoding, losing a dimension of information.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Clustered Darkness:** Darker-colored points are systematically grouped in one area of the plot.
-   **Wide Lightness Range:** The color scale spans a very large range from near-white to pure black. This high contrast can amplify the bias.
-   **Subjective Feedback:** Viewers describe the "center" of the data as being located where the darkest points are.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Use a Narrower Lightness Range.** Avoid color ramps that go from extremely light (near-white) to extremely dark (near-black). Choosing a narrower range (e.g., from 75% to 25% lightness) reduces the perceptual difference between marks and mitigates the bias.

-   **Moderate Redesign: Add an Explicit Mean.** If you must use a wide lightness range, add a visible, distinct marker (e.g., cross-hairs) at the true mean's location. This provides a correct visual anchor for viewers to compare against.

-   **Comprehensive Redesign: Use a Different Channel or Chart.** If mean estimation is critical, do not encode a third variable in the same plot. Use faceting (small multiples) instead. Alternatively, if the third variable is categorical, use a qualitative color palette (based on hue) which does not have the same ordering bias.
