## Guidance

When choosing between a pie chart and a treemap to show a simple part-to-whole relationship with a small number of categories, prefer the pie chart.

## Why

Despite common criticism of pie charts, empirical evidence suggests they are more effective than treemaps for simple composition tasks. A 2019 study found that people can judge the proportions in pie charts more accurately than in treemaps. The varied aspect ratios of the rectangles in a treemap make direct area comparison perceptually difficult, leading to higher error rates.

### Core Principle

Visual encodings that are easier for the human brain to decode lead to more accurate and faster data interpretation. For simple part-to-whole comparisons, the combination of angle and area in a pie chart appears to be more decodable than the variable-aspect-ratio areas of a treemap.

## When it applies

- When your primary task is to have the audience compare the proportions of a few (e.g., fewer than 10) non-hierarchical categories.
- When you are considering a treemap as a "modern" alternative to a pie chart for showing simple composition.

## Exceptions

- If the data is hierarchical (i.e., has nested sub-categories), a treemap is the appropriate choice. Treemaps are specifically designed to visualize nested structures, a task which pie charts cannot perform.
- If you have a very large number of categories, neither chart may be effective, but a treemap might handle the complexity better than a pie chart with dozens of tiny slivers.

## Trade-offs

- **Familiarity vs. Performance:** While treemaps may seem more novel or space-efficient, you sacrifice perceptual accuracy for these aesthetics in a simple composition context.
- **Limited Scope:** This guideline only states a preference between a pie chart and a treemap. It does not imply a pie chart is the *overall best* choice. A bar chart often outperforms both for comparison tasks.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Aspect Ratio Confusion:** In your treemap, you have two segments that are similar in area but have very different shapes (e.g., one is a wide rectangle, the other is a tall, thin one). It's nearly impossible for a viewer to tell which is larger without reading labels.
- **You're using a treemap for non-hierarchical data**, such as the market share of five competing companies.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** If you have a treemap showing simple composition, replace it with a pie chart. This is a direct swap that the evidence suggests will improve accuracy.
- **Moderate Redesign:** If you must use a treemap, ensure every rectangle is clearly labeled with its value and/or percentage. This provides a "cognitive escape hatch," allowing users to read the values instead of relying on flawed area perception.
- **Comprehensive Approach:** For the highest accuracy in part-to-whole comparisons, replace either chart with a simple bar chart. Bar charts use length on a common, aligned scale, which is one of the most accurately perceived visual encodings.

## Guidance

For simple part-to-whole comparisons involving a small number of non-hierarchical categories, avoid using a treemap.

## Why

Treemaps are designed to display hierarchical data in a space-filling manner. When used for simple, non-hierarchical composition tasks, their primary visual encoding—area—is difficult for people to compare accurately, especially when the rectangles have different aspect ratios. Research shows this leads to higher error rates and longer reading times compared to more suitable charts like bar charts or even pie charts.

## When it applies

- When showing the composition of a whole with a small number of parts (e.g., 5-10 categories).
- When the data has no underlying hierarchical structure.
- When the primary goal is for the audience to accurately compare or rank the parts.

## Exceptions

- The primary exception is when the data is hierarchical. A treemap is an excellent choice for showing nested data, such as a breakdown of a company's budget by department, then by sub-team, then by expense type.
- If you have an extremely large number of categories and the goal is to get a general impression of the distribution rather than precise comparison, a treemap might be a compact way to visualize the data.

## Trade-offs

- **Aesthetics vs. Clarity:** Treemaps can be visually dense and aesthetically interesting, filling a rectangular space completely. By choosing a more effective chart like a bar chart, you may sacrifice this compact, "filled-in" look for a chart that is perceptually more accurate but may take up more linear space.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Long and Skinny vs. Short and Fat" Problem:** Your treemap contains two rectangles of similar area but very different shapes. Viewers are unable to confidently say which one is larger.
- **No Hierarchy:** You are using a treemap to visualize a single-level list of categories, such as poll results for several candidates.
- **Slow Interpretation:** Viewers take a long time to answer simple questions like "Which category is the third largest?"

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** If you must keep the treemap, add large, clear labels with the absolute value or percentage to every rectangle. This allows users to read and compare numbers directly, bypassing the flawed perceptual task of comparing areas.
- **Moderate Redesign:** Replace the treemap with a pie chart or donut chart. While not as accurate as a bar chart, a 2019 study showed it was significantly more accurate than a treemap for this task.
- **Comprehensive Approach:** Replace the treemap with a bar chart. Sorting the bars from largest to smallest makes comparison and ranking tasks trivial and highly accurate, as it relies on comparing lengths from a common baseline.

## Guidance

For part-to-whole comparisons, consider using a circular chart that encodes data purely as area (such as a 'circular slice' chart) as a potentially more effective alternative to a standard pie chart.

## Why

Research suggests that isolating the area encoding in a circular format might improve perceptual performance. A 2019 study tested a novel chart that used overlapping circles to represent part-to-whole relationships and found it was both more accurate and faster to read than a traditional pie chart. This indicates that the mixed encodings of a pie chart (angle, arc length, area) might be less effective than a chart that relies on a single, clearer visual cue.

## When it applies

- When creating a visualization for a part-to-whole comparison task.
- When you are open to using a less conventional chart type to potentially improve accuracy and reading speed.
- When you are designing a custom visualization and exploring alternatives to the standard pie chart.

## Exceptions

- When audience familiarity is the top priority. A novel chart type may require additional explanation or could confuse viewers who are only accustomed to standard charts. The immediate recognizability of a pie chart may be more valuable than the potential perceptual gains of a novel chart.
- When implementation time is a major constraint. A custom chart type will require more development effort than a standard pie chart available in any charting library.

## Trade-offs

- **Novelty vs. Familiarity:** Using a novel chart sacrifices immediate audience recognition for potential gains in perceptual accuracy and speed. Viewers may need a moment to understand the new chart's mechanics.
- **Implementation Effort:** Standard charts are easy to create, while custom charts require more design and development work.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Pie Chart Ambiguity:** Viewers are having difficulty accurately comparing slices in your pie chart, especially when the slices are similarly sized.
- **Seeking Higher Precision:** Your use case requires more precise part-to-whole comparisons than a standard pie chart can offer, but you wish to retain a circular form factor.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** If using a standard pie chart, ensure it is well-designed: sort the slices by size, use a limited number of categories, and add direct data labels with values or percentages.
- **Moderate Redesign:** If you must use a circular chart, consider a donut chart. While perceptually similar to a pie chart, some designers prefer it for its cleaner aesthetic and the ability to place a total value or summary in the center.
- **Comprehensive Approach:** Experiment with implementing a custom circular area-based chart like the 'circular slice' chart from the 2019 study. Alternatively, for maximum accuracy, switch to a non-circular format like a bar chart, which uses the highly effective encoding of length on a common scale.
