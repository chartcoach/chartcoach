## Guidance

When the primary user task is to compare, rank, or find extreme values (e.g., "which category is 3rd highest?"), sort the bars in your bar chart by their value (ascending or descending).

## Why

Sorting bars by value transforms a difficult perceptual estimation task into a simple positional lookup. Viewers can quickly and accurately determine a bar's rank by its position in the sequence, rather than having to mentally compare its height against all other bars. This reduces cognitive load, decreases error rates, and speeds up comprehension.

### Core Principle

Make the most important comparisons the easiest to see. If rank is the key message, the visual ordering should directly encode that rank.

## When it applies

- The primary task is to understand the ranking or relative order of categories.
- You want to highlight the highest or lowest performing items.
- The categorical data has no inherent, meaningful order (e.g., a list of products, countries, or people).

## Exceptions

- **When a different sort order is more meaningful for the task.** If the primary task is to look up a specific category by name (e.g., finding "Switzerland" in a list of countries), an alphabetical sort may be more effective.
- **When there is an inherent logical order.** For categories with a natural sequence, such as age groups, Likert scale responses ("Strongly Disagree" to "Strongly Agree"), or temporal units, that order should be preserved.

## Trade-offs

- **Loses alphabetical lookup:** Sorting by value makes it harder to find a specific category by name if the list is long.
- **Can feel less 'neutral':** A value-based sort inherently emphasizes the ranking, which may not be the primary goal if you intend a more neutral presentation.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Alphabetical order for non-lookup tasks:** The bars are sorted alphabetically, but the user's question is about rank (e.g., "Who are the top 5?").
- **Mental gymnastics:** Viewers have to scan back and forth across the entire chart to figure out which bar is the third-largest or fifth-smallest.
- **User questions about rank:** If viewers frequently ask "Which one is ranked #X?", it's a sign that the chart isn't effectively communicating rank.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** If you cannot change the chart's sort order, add annotations. Place rank numbers (e.g., "1st", "2nd", "3rd") as labels on the most important bars to provide a direct lookup for rank.
- **Comprehensive approach:** Reorder the bars based on their value, either ascending or descending. This is the most effective way to support rank-based tasks and is a standard best practice in data visualization.

## Guidance

Be aware that in an unsorted bar chart, viewers are likely to misjudge the rank of individual bars, especially those at the extremes of the distribution. They tend to perceive ranks as being closer to the middle (median) than they actually are.

## Why

This bias is rooted in a well-documented cognitive heuristic called "anchoring and adjustment," as well as the statistical phenomenon of "regression to the mean." When asked to estimate a value (like rank), people often anchor on an obvious middle point (the 50th percentile) and then adjust insufficiently towards the true value. This systematically pulls their perception of high-ranking bars down and low-ranking bars up.

### Core Principle

Human perception is not a perfect mirror of the data; it is shaped by powerful cognitive shortcuts and biases. Effective visualization design anticipates and mitigates these biases.

## When it applies

- When presenting an unsorted bar chart (e.g., alphabetical or random order).
- The user's task involves estimating the rank of one or more bars within the overall distribution.
- Accuracy of rank perception is important for the audience's understanding.

## Exceptions

- When the task does not involve rank estimation at all (e.g., only looking up absolute values for specific, named categories).
- When the bar chart is sorted by value, which makes rank explicit and removes the need for estimation.

## Trade-offs

- Mitigating this bias often requires adding more information (labels, annotations) or changing the structure of the chart (sorting), which may conflict with other design goals like minimalism or preserving an alphabetical order for lookup.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Unsorted Bars + Rank Task:** Your chart is unsorted, but you expect viewers to accurately gauge which items are in the top 10% or bottom 25%.
- **Over-reliance on Gestalt:** Assuming viewers can accurately "eyeball" the rank of a bar in a sea of dozens of other unsorted bars.
- **Misleading conclusions:** An audience member concludes an item is "about average" when it is actually in the 85th percentile.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** If you must keep the chart unsorted, add direct text labels to key bars of interest indicating their value or, even better, their actual rank (e.g., "Rank: 3 out of 50"). This provides an "escape hatch" from perceptual bias by allowing for direct reading.
- **Moderate Approach: Use Color to Highlight.** Use a distinct color to highlight specific bars of interest (e.g., "your region," the top 5, the bottom 5) to draw attention and separate them from the "middle" that viewers anchor on.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Sort the Chart.** The most effective way to eliminate this estimation bias is to sort the bar chart by value. This makes the rank of every bar visually explicit by its position, removing the need for perceptual judgment.

## Guidance

Be aware that the perceived height and rank of a bar are slightly influenced by the height of its immediate neighbors. This is known as a "neighborhood effect" or "length contrast."

- **Tall neighbors** can make a target bar seem slightly shorter.
- **Short neighbors** can make a target bar seem slightly taller.

## Why

This perceptual artifact is a form of the **Parallel Line Illusion**, a well-known visual bias where the perceived length of a line is distorted by the length of adjacent parallel lines. In a bar chart, the bars act as parallel lines, and their heights create a local context that subtly biases our perception of any single bar within that context.

However, research shows this effect is **small** in magnitude for typical bar charts and is usually not a primary concern for designers.

## When it applies

- This effect is most likely to occur in unsorted bar charts where bar heights vary and create distinct local groupings.
- It is technically present in all bar charts but is most relevant when making precise judgments about a bar that is surrounded by extreme values (very tall or very short bars).

## Exceptions

- **The effect size is small.** The authors of the primary study conclude that "designers do not need to worry too much about neighborhood effects." Other factors, like the bar's true rank in the overall distribution and whether the chart is sorted, have a much larger impact on perception.
- **In sorted charts, the effect is irrelevant.** When a chart is sorted by value, the rank is determined by position, not by a subtle perceptual judgment of height, rendering the neighborhood effect moot.

## Trade-offs

- **Over-optimization:** Trying to "fix" this small effect by reordering bars could disrupt a more meaningful sort order (like alphabetical or temporal) and potentially introduce more confusion than it solves. Prioritize the primary task and overall clarity over correcting for this minor bias.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Focusing on minor details:** Spending significant time arranging bars to mitigate a small perceptual contrast effect at the expense of a clear, logical sort order.
- **Inconsistent groupings:** In an unsorted chart, a bar that should be perceived as "high" is placed next to the absolute highest bars, potentially causing a viewer to slightly underestimate its importance.

## How to Improve

- **Acknowledge and Ignore:** For most use cases, the best approach is to be aware that this minor effect exists but to not let it drive design decisions. Focus on more impactful improvements.
- **Sort the Chart:** If rank accuracy is critical, sorting the bar chart by value is the most effective solution. This makes rank explicit and completely bypasses any need for the viewer to make subtle perceptual judgments that could be biased by neighborhood effects.
