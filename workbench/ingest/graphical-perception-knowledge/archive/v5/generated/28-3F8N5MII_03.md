## Guidance

To help viewers identify and assess the correlation between two quantitative variables, use a scatterplot.

## Why

Scatterplots map two quantitative variables to the x and y axes, allowing the relationship between them to emerge as a visual pattern. The shape, density, and direction of the cloud of points provide powerful, direct perceptual cues about the strength and nature (positive, negative, or non-existent) of the correlation. Other chart types, like line charts or bar charts, require viewers to perform mental calculations to infer the same relationship, increasing cognitive load and the risk of error.

### Core Principle

Choose the visualization that makes the primary task the most direct perceptual judgment. For correlation, this means letting the relationship manifest as a single, holistic visual pattern.

## When it applies

- The primary goal is to show the relationship or correlation between two continuous variables.
- You want to identify outliers that do not fit the general pattern of the relationship.
- You are performing exploratory data analysis to look for potential relationships in the data.

## Exceptions

- **Time-Series Data:** If one of the variables is time and the primary goal is to show the trend of each variable over that time, two side-by-side line charts might be more effective. A scatterplot would obscure the temporal sequence.
- **Categorical Data:** A scatterplot requires two quantitative variables. If one variable is categorical, a box plot or violin plot is more appropriate for showing the distribution of the quantitative variable across the categories.
- **Overplotting:** If you have a very large number of data points, a standard scatterplot can become a dense, unreadable blob. In this case, use techniques like transparency, smaller points, or a 2D density plot (heatmap) to reveal the underlying structure.

## Trade-offs

- A scatterplot is an abstract representation that may be less familiar to some audiences than bar or line charts.
- It can be difficult to look up the exact value of a single point, especially in a dense plot (though tooltips can mitigate this in interactive versions).

## Signs of Trouble

- **Zig-Zag Lines:** A line chart is used to connect points in a scatterplot where the x-axis is not time. This creates meaningless connections and obscures the correlation.
- **"Mental Gymnastics":** You present two bar charts and ask the audience to "notice how the pattern is similar," forcing them to compare the shapes of the two charts in their head.
- **Dual-Axis Disaster:** A dual-axis line chart is used to imply a correlation, but the scaling of the two axes is arbitrary and can create a misleading impression of a strong or weak relationship.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Connect the Dots.** If you are already looking at two variables, simply plot them against each other on X and Y axes to create a scatterplot. Add a trend line to make the correlation more explicit.
- **Moderate Approach: From Separate to Single.** If you have two separate charts (e.g., line charts over time for two variables), create an additional scatterplot of the two variables against each other. This allows you to show both the individual trends and their direct relationship.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Use a Scatterplot Matrix (SPLOM).** If you are exploring relationships among multiple (3+) variables, use a scatterplot matrix. This grid of scatterplots shows the relationship between every pair of variables in your dataset, providing a powerful tool for exploratory analysis.
