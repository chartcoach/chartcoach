## Guidance

Do not assume a single, universal ranking of visual channels (e.g., that position is always better than length, which is always better than angle). The effectiveness of an encoding is highly dependent on the viewer's task, the number of data points, and the specific performance goal (e.g., minimizing bias vs. maximizing precision).

## Why

Classic rankings are based on the narrow task of judging the ratio between two values. Research shows these rankings break down in more realistic scenarios that involve remembering and comparing multiple values. The "best" channel for judging two values is often not the best for recalling or comparing eight values, as memory limitations and data context become the dominant factors.

### Core Principle

The effectiveness of a visual encoding is context-dependent, not universal.

## When it applies

- When choosing a chart type or visual encoding for any task that involves more than a simple, direct comparison of two values. This applies to nearly all practical data visualizations where viewers need to see patterns, find outliers, or remember information.

## Exceptions

- For the specific, narrow task of judging the ratio between two *adjacent* values that share a common baseline (as in a simple bar chart), the classic rankings (position > length > angle) generally hold.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Dogmatic Design:** A chart is chosen based on a generic "best practice" rule (e.g., "never use bubble charts") without considering if it's a good fit for the specific data volume, user task, or communication goal.
- **Poor Performance on Complex Charts:** A chart type that works well for simple data becomes difficult to read when more data is added, suggesting the initial design choice was based on a rule that didn't scale.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Question the Default.** If a chart chosen by a standard "rule of thumb" feels hard to read, consider if the encoding is mismatched for the number of data points or the primary task. For example, if a bar chart with 20 bars is hard to summarize, see if a heatmap (luminance) makes patterns clearer.
- **Moderate Redesign: Test Alternatives.** Create alternative versions of the chart using different encodings and informally test which one makes it easiest to answer key questions about the data.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Optimize for the Goal.** Explicitly decide whether your primary goal is to minimize systematic error (bias), improve consistency (precision), or reduce overall error. Choose the visual channel that best supports that specific goal for your given data volume (e.g., position/length for low bias; area for high precision).

## Guidance

Select your visual encoding based on whether it is more important to avoid systematic error (bias) or to ensure consistent judgments (precision). For minimizing bias, prefer length or position (e.g., bar charts, dot plots). For maximizing precision, area (e.g., bubble charts, treemaps) can be an effective choice, but you must account for its potential to introduce bias.

## Why

Different visual channels force trade-offs between perceptual bias and precision; no single channel is best on all metrics. Encodings like position and length lead to less systematic over- or under-estimation. In contrast, area can lead to more consistent (precise) judgments, but viewers are more likely to be systematically inaccurate (e.g., consistently underestimating the size of large circles).

## When it applies

- When designing a visualization where the consequences of different types of error vary.
- In scientific or financial contexts where minimizing systematic bias is critical for accurate interpretation and decision-making.
- In general-audience communications where ensuring viewers are at least consistent in their interpretation (high precision) might be prioritized, as long as the potential for bias is mitigated.

## Trade-offs

- **Prioritizing low bias** with a bar chart may result in slightly less consistent (less precise) judgments compared to an alternative like a bubble chart.
- **Prioritizing high precision** with a bubble chart may lead to viewers systematically misjudging the data's true magnitude, which can be misleading if not corrected.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Bias:** Viewers consistently misjudge values in the same direction. For example, they always underestimate the values represented by large bubbles relative to small ones.
- **Imprecision:** When asked to interpret the same chart, different viewers give widely different answers, or the same viewer is inconsistent.

## How to Improve

- **To Reduce Bias:** If you suspect bias in an area-based chart (like a bubble chart or treemap), switch to a position-based or length-based encoding. A dot plot or a bar chart will make judgments of magnitude less susceptible to systematic error.
- **To Increase Precision:** If viewers struggle with inconsistent judgments on a bar or line chart, using an area-based encoding might, counterintuitively, improve their consistency. However, you **must** mitigate the accompanying risk of bias by adding direct data labels to each shape.

## Guidance

Recognize that a viewer's ability to accurately perceive, remember, and compare values in a static chart degrades dramatically as the number of items increases beyond 3-4. The cognitive load imposed by data volume is often the single biggest barrier to a chart's effectiveness.

## Why

Human visual working memory has a strict capacity limit of roughly 3-4 items. Exceeding this limit forces viewers to use less reliable strategies like getting a rough "gist" of the data or performing slow, sequential scans. This cognitive overload leads to more errors, increased bias, and lower precision, and its negative impact often outweighs any benefit from choosing a "perceptually optimal" visual encoding.

### Core Principle

Design for the limits of human attention and memory.

## When it applies

- When designing any static visualization, especially dashboards, reports, or presentations.
- When deciding how much data to show in a single chart versus breaking it into multiple charts or adding interactivity.

## Exceptions

- **Interactive visualizations** can manage high-cardinality data effectively by allowing users to filter, highlight, or search, thereby controlling how many items are in focus at once.
- **Small multiples** are a powerful exception, as they strategically break a large, complex dataset into a series of smaller charts, each of which respects memory limits.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The "Wall of Data" Effect:** The chart feels cluttered, dense, and overwhelming at first glance.
- **Slow Interpretation:** Viewers take a long time to find a specific piece of information or understand the main message.
- **Gist Over Details:** Viewers can only describe the overall trend or "average" but cannot accurately recall or compare specific data points.
- **The "Where's Waldo" Problem:** Finding a specific category or value requires a slow, effortful visual search.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Highlight and Annotate.** If you must show all the data points, use color, size, or callout-boxes to draw immediate attention to the 1-3 most important points. This guides the viewer and reduces the initial cognitive load.
- **Moderate Redesign: Use Small Multiples.** Instead of one complex chart (e.g., a line chart with 12 series), create a grid of smaller, simpler charts (e.g., a 3x4 grid of line charts). This keeps the detail while making each individual comparison manageable.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Introduce Interactivity.** For digital platforms, implement controls like filters, sorters, or a search bar. This empowers users to narrow down the data to a subset that is relevant to them and respects their cognitive limits.
