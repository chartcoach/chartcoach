## Guidance

When encoding quantitative data, choose a visual variable from as high up the perceptual hierarchy as possible. The general order from most to least accurate is:

1.  Position on a common scale
2.  Position on non-aligned scales
3.  Length / Direction / Angle
4.  Area
5.  Volume / Curvature
6.  Shading / Color Saturation

## Why

Not all visual encodings are created equal. The human visual system decodes some (like position) with high accuracy and little systematic bias, while others (like area or color saturation) are judged less accurately and are more prone to error. Aligning your most important data with the most accurate encodings makes your chart more effective and reduces the chance of misinterpretation.

### Core Principle

A graphical form that involves elementary perceptual tasks that lead to more accurate judgments will result in better organization and increase the chances of a correct perception of patterns and behavior in the data.

## When it applies

- When designing any visualization where the accurate perception of quantitative data is important for the user's task.
- When deciding which of several possible chart types is best for a given comparison or ranking task.

## Exceptions

- Less accurate encodings are often necessary and acceptable for secondary variables in a multivariate visualization. For example, using position for the two main variables on a scatter plot and area or color (less accurate) for a third or fourth variable is a standard and effective technique.
- Sometimes the visual metaphor requires a lower-ranked encoding. For example, a choropleth map must use area and/or color to show a geographic pattern.

## Trade-offs

- Higher-ranked encodings (especially position) can take up more space. A bar chart is typically less compact than a treemap (which uses area).
- Overloading a chart with too many high-priority encodings can lead to clutter. A key part of design is deciding which variables are most important and deserve the best encodings.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Imprecise Impressions:** The visualization gives a general impression (e.g., "this is big") but fails to reveal more specific relationships or rankings.
- **Struggling to Compare:** Users find it difficult to make precise comparisons (e.g., "Is A about 1.5x or 2x bigger than B?").
- **Hidden Patterns:** The chosen encoding obscures small but important differences in the data that would be visible with a more accurate encoding.

## How to Improve

- **Perform a Channel Audit:** Identify the primary task you want users to perform (e.g., "compare sales across regions"). Then, identify which visual channel (position, length, area, color, etc.) is encoding that data.

- **Upgrade the Channel:** If the primary task is supported by a low-ranked channel (like area in a bubble chart), consider if a different chart type could use a higher-ranked channel (like position in a bar chart) to accomplish the same task more effectively.
