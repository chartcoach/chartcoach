## Guidance

For tasks requiring accurate judgment of quantitative values, encode data using position on a common scale (e.g., bar charts, scatter plots) or length. These are perceptually more accurate than angle (pie charts), area (bubble charts), or color.

## Why

Viewers are significantly more accurate and faster at judging differences in position along an aligned scale than they are at judging differences in length, angle, or area. Using a less accurate channel increases the risk of misinterpretation and reduces the precision with which viewers can read the data.

### Core Principle

Humans judge quantities more accurately by comparing positions along a common scale than by comparing other visual attributes like angle, areas, or colors.

## When it applies

-   When the primary task is to compare, rank, or look up precise quantitative values.
-   When creating standard charts like bar charts, line charts, or scatter plots.
-   When data integrity and accurate interpretation are more important than compact-ness or novel aesthetics.

## Exceptions

-   When the goal is to show a part-to-whole relationship and the number of slices is very small (2-3), a pie chart can be acceptable, though a bar chart is still often clearer.
-   When showing hierarchical data or packing many values into a small space, area-based charts like treemaps can be effective for showing general magnitude, even if they sacrifice precision.
-   When visualizing geographic data, where position is already used to encode location, other channels like color or size must be used.

## Trade-offs

-   **Space:** Bar charts and dot plots (which use position) can take up more screen or page space than more compact representations like pie charts or treemaps (which use angle or area).
-   **Part-to-whole:** While a stacked bar chart can show part-to-whole relationships, it is often harder to compare the individual components that are not on the baseline. A pie chart makes the part-to-whole relationship more explicit, at the cost of comparison accuracy.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Difficult Comparisons:** Viewers find it hard to confidently say which of two non-adjacent pie slices or bubbles is larger.
-   **Inaccurate Takeaways:** When asked to estimate the ratio between two values (e.g., "how many times bigger is A than B?"), users' answers are consistently wrong.
-   **Reliance on Labels:** The chart is unreadable without direct data labels on every element. The visual encoding itself is failing.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** If you must use a pie or bubble chart, add direct data labels (e.g., "42%") to each element. This provides an "escape hatch" for the user to read exact values instead of relying on flawed perceptual judgments.
-   **Moderate Redesign: Switch to a Better Chart.** Convert a pie chart or bubble chart into a bar chart or a dot plot. This directly replaces the less accurate encoding (angle/area) with a more accurate one (position/length).
-   **Comprehensive Approach: Align the Scales.** If comparing multiple series with grouped bar charts, ensure all bars share a common, aligned baseline (the y-axis at zero) to maximize the effectiveness of position and length judgments.
