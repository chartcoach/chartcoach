## Guidance

For tasks that require comparing or ranking quantitative values across categories, use a bar chart or dot plot instead of a pie chart or donut chart.

## Why

Humans are significantly better at accurately judging differences in length from a common baseline (as in a bar chart) than they are at judging differences in angles, areas, or arc lengths (as in a pie or donut chart). This perceptual advantage makes comparisons in bar charts faster, easier, and more precise.

### Core Principle

Make the most important comparisons the easiest to see. Visual encodings should be chosen based on their perceptual accuracy for the intended task. Position along a common scale is the most accurate visual channel for comparing quantities.

## When it applies

- When the primary goal is to have the viewer **compare** the sizes of different categories (e.g., "Which region has the highest sales?").
- When the viewer needs to **rank** categories from smallest to largest.
- When there are more than 2-3 categories to display.

## Exceptions

- **Simple Part-to-Whole Judgment:** If the only goal is to show a single category's share of the total (e.g., "What percentage of the budget is spent on marketing?") and high precision is not required, a pie chart can be acceptable, especially with 2-3 slices.
- **Retrieve Value Task:** Some research suggests that for the specific task of retrieving a single value (e.g., reading a percentage label), pie charts can perform as well as bar charts, provided the values are directly labeled.
- **Binary Choice:** For showing a simple binary split (e.g., 75% Yes / 25% No), a pie chart can be intuitively understood.

## Trade-offs

- **Familiarity:** Some audiences are very familiar with pie charts for part-to-whole relationships. Using a bar chart might require a slight cognitive adjustment for viewers accustomed to seeing compositions represented as a circle.
- **Space:** A pie chart can be more compact than a horizontal bar chart, but it is generally less space-efficient than a vertical bar chart for the information it conveys.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Multiple Pies:** You are using multiple pie charts side-by-side to compare compositions. This is extremely difficult to do accurately.
- **Many Slices:** The pie chart has more than 5-7 slices, creating a cluttered "rainbow" of thin slivers that are impossible to distinguish, label, or compare.
- **Similar Values:** Categories have very similar values (e.g., 22%, 24%, 26%), which look almost identical as angles in a pie chart but are clearly distinguishable as bars.
- **Legend-Reliant:** Viewers must constantly look back and forth between the chart and a color-coded legend to understand which slice is which.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Direct Labels.** If you must use a pie chart, add direct labels with the category name and exact value (percentage or total) to each slice. This provides an "escape hatch" by allowing users to read the data instead of relying on flawed angle perception.

- **Moderate Redesign: Sort the Slices.** If using a pie chart, sort the slices from largest to smallest, starting at the 12 o'clock position. This makes it slightly easier to do coarse comparisons of adjacent slices.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Switch to a Bar Chart.** The most effective improvement is to convert the pie chart into a bar chart. Sort the bars from largest to smallest (or in another logical order) to make ranking and comparison effortless. This directly leverages the perceptual superiority of length judgments.

## Guidance

When creating or using a categorical color or shape palette, reorder the items to maximize the perceptual distance between them, especially for items that will be assigned sequentially or appear near each other. Do not rely on default or alphabetical sorting.

## Why

Default palettes (e.g., in software) or alphabetically sorted ones often place perceptually similar items next to each other (e.g., "blue" and "cyan", or a circle and an oval). This makes it difficult for viewers to distinguish between the categories they represent. By reordering the palette to maximize the perceptual "gap" between items, you make each category more distinct and reduce the cognitive load of telling them apart.

### Core Principle

The ability to distinguish between visual marks is fundamental to decoding a chart. The design of a palette should prioritize perceptual discriminability, not arbitrary orders like software defaults or alphabetical lists.

## When it applies

- When assigning colors or shapes to unordered categorical data.
- When creating a shareable palette or design system standard for your organization.
- When you have more categories than can be handled by a small, hand-picked, highly distinct palette (i.e., > 5-7 categories).

## Exceptions

- **Ordered Data:** If your data has an inherent order (e.g., "low," "medium," "high"), you should use a sequential or diverging palette that reflects that order, not a categorical one reordered for maximum contrast.
- **Semantic Association:** If certain colors or shapes have strong semantic meanings tied to your categories (e.g., using a red cross for "medical"), that association may be more important than maximizing perceptual distance.

## Trade-offs

- **Aesthetic Harmony:** A palette designed purely for maximum discriminability might not be as aesthetically harmonious as a hand-tuned one. The reordered palette may contain high-contrast, potentially jarring combinations.
- **Effort:** Creating an empirically-derived, perceptually-ordered palette requires more effort than using a default palette.

## Signs of Trouble

- **"Is that blue or green?":** Viewers express confusion about which category a mark belongs to because two colors in the palette are too similar.
- **The Squint Test:** When you squint at the visualization, marks from different categories blend together into a single color.
- **Subtle Neighbors:** The palette legend contains two or more colors/shapes that are neighbors in perceptual space (e.g., red and orange, circle and ellipse) and are hard to tell apart, especially when marks are small or overlapping.
- **Default Order:** Your color legend is sorted alphabetically (e.g., "Aqua," "Blue," "Cerulean") or follows a software's default rainbow-like progression.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Approach: Manual Reordering.** Look at your chosen palette. Identify the most similar pairs of colors/shapes and manually re-sort the legend to separate them as much as possible. For example, if you have blue and teal, place an unrelated color like orange between them.

- **Moderate Approach: Use a Well-Designed Tool.** Use a tool like Colorgorical or Viz-Palette that is designed to generate discriminable palettes. These tools often have perceptual models built-in to ensure the colors they suggest are easy to tell apart.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Algorithmic Reordering.** Implement the method from Demiralp et al. (2014). Start with the two most perceptually distant items in your palette. Then, iteratively add the item that has the greatest minimum distance to the items already selected (i.e., maximize the Hausdorff distance). This builds a palette where each new item is as distinct as possible from all previous items.

## Guidance

When the primary task is to understand the relationship (correlation) between two quantitative variables, a scatter plot is the most effective choice.

## Why

Scatter plots map the two variables directly to the X and Y axes, leveraging the highly accurate visual channel of position. The resulting pattern of points—its direction, tightness, and shape—provides a powerful and immediate visual cue about the strength and nature of the correlation. The human visual system is adept at detecting patterns in these 2D point clouds.

### Core Principle

The visualization should directly represent the data relationship being investigated. For correlation, which is a bivariate relationship, a chart that uses two positional axes is the most direct and effective representation.

## When it applies

- When you have two continuous or discrete quantitative variables.
- When you want to see if variables are positively correlated, negatively correlated, or not correlated.
- When you want to identify the strength of a relationship (e.g., tight and linear vs. weak and scattered).
- When looking for outliers that do not fit the general pattern.

## Exceptions

- **Time-Series Data:** If one of the variables is time, a line chart with two lines can also be used to show correlation over that time period. However, a scatter plot may still be better for judging the strength of the correlation itself, independent of the time trend.
- **Many Variables:** For exploring correlations across many variables at once, a scatter plot matrix (SPLOM) or a correlation heatmap may be more space-efficient, though individual scatter plots are still best for deep-diving into a specific pair.

## Trade-offs

- **Overplotting:** In datasets with a large number of data points, a standard scatter plot can suffer from overplotting, where points are drawn on top of each other, obscuring the true data density and potentially misleading the viewer about the correlation.
- **Categorical Data:** Scatter plots are not suitable for showing relationships between two categorical variables. Use a heatmap or mosaic plot instead.

## Signs of Trouble

- **A "Blob" of Points:** The plot is so dense with overlapping points that it's impossible to discern any pattern or density variations.
- **Incorrect Chart Choice:** You are trying to infer correlation from a chart not suited for the task, like a dual-axis line chart (which can be misleading) or two separate bar charts.
- **Underestimation in Parallel Coordinates:** You are using a parallel coordinates plot to judge correlation; research shows this often leads to underestimating the strength of the relationship.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Adjust Transparency.** If facing overplotting, reduce the opacity of the points. This allows overlapping points to create darker areas, revealing the density of the data and giving a better sense of the correlation.

- **Moderate Approach: Use Sampling or Jittering.** For very large datasets, plot a random sample of the data to reduce clutter. If one variable is discrete, apply a small amount of random "jitter" to the points to separate them and prevent them from forming perfect lines.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Use Density Plots.** For severe overplotting, switch from plotting individual points to visualizing their 2D density. This can be done using 2D histogram binning (similar to a heatmap) or a contour plot, which clearly shows where the data is most concentrated and thus the nature of the correlation.
```

    url: "https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2109.01271"
    note: "This review paper summarizes the finding from Smart & Szafir [92] that 'CH [color hue] are generally more discriminable with filled shapes than with unfilled ones'."

## Guidance

When using color to encode categorical data with point marks (like in a scatter plot), use filled shapes instead of outlined (unfilled) shapes to make the colors easier to tell apart.

## Why

A filled shape provides a larger surface area of color than a thin outline. This larger area provides a stronger, more stable signal to the visual system, making it easier to perceive and distinguish between different hues. This is especially important for small marks, where the color in a thin outline can be difficult to see clearly.

## When it applies

- In scatter plots where point color represents a category.
- In bubble charts where bubble color represents a category.
- In any chart using small, colored glyphs or symbols to represent categorical data.

## Exceptions

- **Severe Overplotting:** In very dense plots, filled shapes can completely obscure underlying marks. Using outlined shapes or adjusting opacity might be necessary to get a sense of the underlying data density, even if it slightly reduces color discriminability.
- **Highlighting a Subset:** Outlined shapes can be effectively used to distinguish a specific subset of data from a background of filled shapes, or vice-versa.

## Trade-offs

- **Overplotting vs. Discriminability:** Using filled shapes increases the risk of overplotting in dense charts. You are trading a better view of the data's density for better discriminability of individual categories.
- **Perceived Size:** Filled shapes are often perceived as being larger than unfilled shapes of the same dimensions, which could introduce a slight bias if size is also a meaningful visual variable.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Indistinguishable Outlines:** When looking at the chart, it is difficult to tell the color of the outlines, especially for small or overlapping marks.
- **"Empty" Appearance:** The chart looks sparse or "empty" because the colored marks are just thin lines, reducing the visual impact of the color encoding.
- **User Confusion:** Viewers have to zoom in or squint to determine the color of a specific point.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Increase Stroke Width.** If you must use outlined shapes, increase the thickness (stroke width) of the outline to provide more color area. However, this will also make the marks larger and can worsen overplotting.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Switch to Filled Shapes.** Change the mark property from outlined to filled. If overplotting becomes an issue, you can then apply transparency (alpha) to the fill color. A semi-transparent fill is often the best solution, as it improves color discriminability while still revealing the density of underlying points.
```
