## Guidance

When viewers need to compare values of multiple time series at a specific point in time (a local comparison), use a shared-space technique where all series are plotted in the same coordinate system. Standard line charts or braided graphs are effective choices.

## Why

Placing all series on a common set of axes allows for direct visual comparison without requiring eye movement between different charts. This superposition minimizes the cognitive and perceptual load for local tasks, leading to faster performance.

### Core Principle
Make the most important comparisons the easiest to see. For local comparisons, this means putting the data points in the same visual space.

## When it applies

- The primary task is a **local comparison**, such as "Which series has the highest value at this specific time?" or "Rank the series' values on this particular date."
- You are visualizing two to eight time series.
- The values of the time series are frequently crossing and changing order.

## Exceptions

- When the number of time series is very high (e.g., more than 8-10), the visual clutter from overlapping lines (a "spaghetti plot") can make even local comparisons difficult or impossible. In such cases, a split-space technique like small multiples may be necessary, even if it's less efficient for the task.
- If the lines are so dense that they form an illegible mass, this guidance does not apply.

## Trade-offs

- **Clarity vs. Efficiency:** While faster for local tasks, shared-space charts can become cluttered, making it difficult to follow a single line's trend across the entire chart (a dispersed task).
- **Legibility:** Requires a well-chosen, distinguishable color palette. With too many lines, colors can become hard to tell apart, undermining the chart's effectiveness.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Spaghetti Plot:** The chart is a tangled mess of overlapping lines, making it impossible to distinguish individual series.
- **Back-and-Forth:** Viewers have to repeatedly look back and forth between the legend and the chart to identify which line is which.
- **Slow Performance:** Users take a long time to answer simple questions about values at a specific point, suggesting they are struggling to decode the chart.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Highlight Key Series.** If you must use a shared-space chart with many series, de-emphasize less important series (e.g., by making them light gray) and use a distinct color to highlight the one or two series of interest for the comparison.
- **Moderate Approach: Switch to a Braided Graph.** If you are using a simple line chart, switching to a braided graph (where filled areas are depth-sorted) can improve the identifiability of each series by giving color more surface area, though it may not be faster.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Use Small Multiples (If Clutter is the Issue).** If the core problem is clutter from too many series, switch to a split-space approach like small multiples. Acknowledge that this will make local comparisons less efficient but will drastically improve overall clarity and the ability to perform dispersed tasks.

## Guidance

When viewers need to compare values or trends across the entire duration of multiple time series (a dispersed comparison), use a split-space technique like small multiples or horizon graphs. This involves giving each time series its own dedicated chart space.

## Why

Separating each series into its own plot (juxtaposition) eliminates the visual clutter and occlusion that occurs in shared-space charts with many overlapping lines. This makes it much easier for viewers to follow the trajectory of individual series and compare their overall shapes, peaks, and troughs across a wide visual span.

### Core Principle
Reduce cognitive work by providing clarity. For complex or dispersed tasks, separating elements is often clearer than superimposing them.

## When it applies

- The primary task is a **dispersed comparison**, such as "Which series had the highest peak value overall?" or "Compare the overall shape and volatility of these series."
- You are visualizing more than 4-5 time series, where overlap in a shared-space chart would become a significant problem.
- Clarity of individual trends is more important than the speed of direct point-in-time comparisons.

## Exceptions

- When the primary task is a purely local comparison (e.g., "Which line is highest at this single point?"). In this case, a shared-space chart is more efficient as it doesn't require eye travel between plots.
- When screen space is extremely limited and the number of series is very high. In such cases, aggregation or other summarization techniques might be better than showing all individual series.

## Trade-offs

- **Space vs. Clarity:** Split-space techniques require significantly more screen real estate than shared-space charts.
- **Local vs. Global Efficiency:** This approach makes dispersed comparisons easier at the cost of making local, point-in-time comparisons less efficient, as the viewer's eye must scan across multiple separate charts.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Excessive Scrolling:** Users have to scroll extensively to see all the charts, making comparison difficult.
- **Inconsistent Scales:** The y-axes of the small multiples are not synchronized, making visual comparison of magnitudes misleading and invalid.
- **Difficulty with Local Comparison:** Users struggle to answer questions about which series is higher at a specific point in time, as they have to visually scan and align points across separate charts.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Ensure Consistent Axes.** The single most important rule for small multiples is to use the same scale and range for the y-axis (and x-axis) across all charts. This ensures that visual comparisons of height and slope are valid.
- **Moderate Approach: Smart Ordering.** Arrange the small multiples in a logical order (e.g., sorted by average value, final value, or volatility). This can help viewers find patterns more quickly.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Switch to a Shared-Space Chart (If Task is Local).** If you find that users are primarily performing local comparisons, the choice of a split-space layout may be wrong for the task. Consider switching back to a shared-space chart and using other techniques (like highlighting or filtering) to manage clutter.

## Guidance

When the primary task is to have viewers compare the overall rate of change (slope or trend) across multiple time series, prefer using either a simple line chart or a small multiples layout. Avoid more complex representations like braided graphs or horizon graphs for this task.

## Why

Judging the overall slope of a line is a holistic, perceptual task. Simple line charts and small multiples present the data in a direct, un-abstracted way that supports this judgment. More complex techniques like braided graphs (with their changing depth order) and horizon graphs (with their layered bands) introduce additional cognitive steps to decode the chart, which interferes with and slows down the primary task of trend assessment.

### Core Principle
Match the complexity of the visualization to the complexity of the task. For holistic judgments like slope, simpler visual representations are often more effective.

## When it applies

- The primary task is to identify or rank which time series is increasing or decreasing the fastest.
- The task involves assessing the overall trend, not just local fluctuations.
- Performance (speed of judgment) is a key concern.

## Exceptions

- **Simple Line Chart Exception:** If the number of series in a simple line chart creates a "spaghetti plot," it becomes impossible to judge any slope. In this case, small multiples are the only viable option of the two.
- **Horizon Graph Exception:** If you are working with extremely limited vertical space and the audience is highly trained to read horizon graphs, their space efficiency might be an acceptable trade-off for the slower performance on this specific task.

## Trade-offs

- **Choosing between Simple Line and Small Multiples:**
    - A **simple line chart** allows direct comparison of slopes at the same point in time but can suffer from clutter.
    - **Small multiples** eliminate clutter but make it harder to compare the steepness of two lines at the exact same moment. The choice depends on whether you expect more clutter or more need for direct comparison.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Slow Task Completion:** Users take a surprisingly long time to determine which line has the steepest trend.
- **User Confusion:** Users express confusion about how to interpret the visualization, particularly with horizon or braided graphs, asking "What do the different colors mean?" or "Why are the lines changing layers?"
- **Incorrect Judgments:** Users frequently misidentify the steepest trend, suggesting the visualization is perceptually misleading for this task.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Simplify the Chart.** If using a complex chart type, switch to a simpler one. If using a braided graph or horizon graph for a slope task, change to a simple line chart (if few series) or small multiples (if many series).
- **Moderate Approach: Control for Clutter.** If using a simple line chart, reduce clutter by highlighting the most relevant lines and de-emphasizing others with a neutral color like light gray.
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Align Chart Choice with Task Priority.** Explicitly decide on the primary user task. If slope/trend analysis is most important, commit to a simple line chart or small multiples layout. If other tasks (like local comparison or space efficiency) are more critical, accept that slope judgment will be a secondary, less efficient task.

## Guidance

Do not use horizon graphs if the primary task for your viewers is to perform quick, local value lookups or comparisons (e.g., finding the value of a series at a single point in time).

## Why

Horizon graphs are a space-efficient but perceptually complex visualization. They encode value through a combination of layered bands and color saturation, and they often mirror negative values into the positive space. To read a single value, a viewer must mentally "unfold" these layers, a cognitive process that takes time and effort. For simple lookup tasks, this added decoding step makes them significantly slower than more direct representations like standard line charts.

### Core Principle
Minimize cognitive overhead. Every mental step required to decode a chart is a potential source of error and delay. For simple tasks, use simple encodings.

## When it applies

- The task is a **local lookup** or comparison ("What was the value on Tuesday?").
- Task completion **speed** is a critical metric.
- The audience is not specifically trained in interpreting horizon graphs.

## Exceptions

- When **vertical screen space is the single most important constraint**, and you must display many time series. The primary strength of horizon graphs is their extreme space efficiency. If the alternative is not showing the data at all, their slowness on lookup tasks may be an acceptable trade-off.
- For tasks involving the detection of rhythm or periodicity, where the colored bands can create a strong visual pattern.
- The audience consists of experts who use horizon graphs daily and have developed high fluency in reading them (e.g., in some financial or server monitoring dashboards).

## Trade-offs

- **Space vs. Speed:** This guideline prioritizes lookup speed over space efficiency. Choosing a simple line chart or small multiples will be faster for lookups but will consume more vertical space than a horizon graph.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Slow Lookups:** Users take a noticeably long time to answer simple questions about the value of a series at a specific point.
- **Decoding Errors:** Users frequently misinterpret the values, for example, by misjudging which band they are in or confusing a mirrored negative value for a positive one.
- **Cognitive Strain:** Users report that the charts feel "confusing," "complicated," or "hard to read." This indicates high cognitive load.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Interactive Tooltips.** If you must use horizon graphs, add interactive tooltips that display the precise value on hover. This provides an "escape hatch" for users, allowing them to bypass the complex perceptual decoding and get the exact value when needed.
- **Moderate Approach: Use a Simpler Split-Space Chart.** Replace the horizon graphs with a standard small multiples layout. This maintains the split-space approach but uses a much more direct and faster-to-read visual encoding (position).
- **Comprehensive Redesign: Re-evaluate Task vs. Space Constraints.** If local lookups are a critical and frequent task, fundamentally question whether the space savings from horizon graphs are worth the performance penalty. It may be better to use a more direct chart type and show fewer series at once, or use a different layout entirely.
