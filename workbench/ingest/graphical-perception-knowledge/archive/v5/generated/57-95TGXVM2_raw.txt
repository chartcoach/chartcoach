## Guidance

When comparing values between two data series in a bar chart, combine a grouped bar chart with a superimposed "difference overlay" (e.g., a line mark) that explicitly visualizes the difference between the two bars in each group.

## Why

This hybrid design is a versatile "all-rounder." It effectively supports multiple comparison tasks—such as finding extreme values, identifying the largest change, and measuring specific differences—within a single chart. It avoids the pitfalls of more specialized charts, which often sacrifice context (like a difference-only chart) or force mental calculations (like showing only one series). This design was overwhelmingly preferred by users for its clarity and information density.

### Core Principle

Make the most important comparisons the easiest to see. By explicitly encoding the difference, you offload the cognitive work of mental subtraction from the viewer, freeing them up for higher-level analysis.

## When it applies

- When you need to show the comparison between two series of data (e.g., this year vs. last year, forecast vs. actual).
- When the audience needs to perform multiple comparison tasks, such as finding the largest increase/decrease, looking up original values, and measuring the difference for a specific category.
- In contexts like information dashboards where a single, versatile chart is more valuable than multiple specialized charts.

## Exceptions

- If screen space is extremely limited, the width required for a grouped bar chart might be prohibitive.
- If the *only* task is to show the magnitude of the difference and the original values are irrelevant, a simpler difference chart might suffice.

## Trade-offs

- Adds some visual complexity compared to a standard grouped bar chart. The difference overlay must be designed carefully to be visually distinct from the bars without adding too much clutter.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Back-and-Forth:** Viewers' eyes are darting back and forth between two bars, trying to estimate their difference.
- **Slow Discovery:** It takes a long time for viewers to answer "Which category changed the most?"
- **User Complaints:** Viewers ask for a way to see the exact difference or express frustration with having to estimate it.

## How to Improve

- **Quick approach:** If you have a standard grouped bar chart, add a third series that represents the difference. This is less effective than a superimposed overlay but better than nothing.
- **Comprehensive approach:** Redesign the chart to be a grouped bar chart where each group contains the bars for both series. Then, add a superimposed mark (like a horizontal line or dot) aligned with the top of each bar, with a connecting line between them to explicitly show the difference. This was the `GB+D` design in the research.

## Guidance

When using a difference overlay to visualize the change between two series, ensure the chart displays the original bars for *both* series, not just one.

## Why

Showing only one of a pair of series along with the difference forces the viewer to perform mental arithmetic (addition or subtraction) to reconstruct the value of the missing series. This increases cognitive load, slows down interpretation, and leads to significant errors. Users in the study described this design as confusing and difficult. Displaying both original series allows for direct lookup and eliminates this unnecessary mental work.

### Core Principle

Minimize the cognitive work required to decode your visualization. Every mental calculation you force upon the viewer is an opportunity for error and a barrier to understanding.

## When it applies

- Any time you use a difference encoding (like an overlay) to show the change between two values.
- When it is important for the viewer to be able to look up or compare the absolute values of *both* original series, not just the change.

## Exceptions

- If there is absolutely no need for the viewer to ever know the value of the second series, and the only goal is to show how the primary series has changed from its prior value. This is a rare scenario.

## Trade-offs

- Displaying both series (e.g., in a grouped bar chart) requires more horizontal space than displaying a single series. However, the resulting gains in accuracy and comprehension usually justify the space.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Mental Math:** Viewers are pausing to "do the math in their head" to figure out a value.
- **Incorrect Lookups:** When asked for a specific value from the non-displayed series, viewers give the wrong answer.
- **User Complaints:** Viewers say "it's confusing to have one year and a difference" or that it's "difficult to imagine the previous year's value."

## How to Improve

- **Moderate approach:** If you are using a single bar chart with a difference overlay, switch to a grouped bar chart format. Keep the difference overlay, but now it will connect the two bars within each group. This ensures all original values are directly represented.

## Guidance

To help users quickly identify the largest change or measure a specific difference between two series, add a visual mark (e.g., a line overlay) that explicitly encodes the difference.

## Why

Relying on juxtaposition alone, as in a standard grouped bar chart, forces users to perform a series of slow and error-prone mental operations: visually estimate the difference for one category, hold it in working memory, estimate the difference for the next, compare the two, and repeat for all categories. By explicitly encoding the difference as a distinct visual mark, you transform this complex estimation task into a simple perceptual task of finding the largest mark, which is much faster and more accurate.

### Core Principle

Convert analytical tasks (mental calculation) into perceptual tasks (visual search) whenever possible. The human visual system is much faster and more accurate at spotting the longest line than it is at calculating and comparing a series of differences.

## When it applies

- The primary goal is to compare two series and find where the change is greatest or smallest.
- The task involves measuring the precise difference for a given category.
- In dashboards where users need to quickly spot significant changes between periods.

## Exceptions

- If the chart is already extremely dense, adding another visual element might create too much clutter. In this case, consider interaction (e.g., showing the difference on hover) as an alternative.
- If the only task is to look up individual values, and comparison is not a priority.

## Trade-offs

- Adds an extra layer of visual encoding, which can increase clutter if not designed carefully.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Slow Comparisons:** Users take a long time to answer "which category changed the most?"
- **Inaccurate Comparisons:** Users frequently pick the wrong category as having the largest change.
- **Visual Estimation:** You can observe users' eyes scanning back and forth between pairs of bars, trying to judge the gaps.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** Add a third data series to your grouped bar chart that plots the difference. This is less integrated than an overlay but is a simple way to make the difference explicit.
- **Comprehensive approach:** Add a superimposed "difference overlay" to your grouped bar chart. This could be a line segment connecting the tops of the two bars, or a line mark showing the magnitude of the difference, as seen in the `GB+D` design from the study.

## Guidance

Avoid using a chart that *only* displays the differences between two series if viewers also need to understand the original values to properly interpret the data.

## Why

A difference-only chart strips away essential context. A change of +10 units is significant if the original value was 20 (a 50% increase), but trivial if the original value was 1,000 (a 1% increase). Without the context of the original magnitudes, viewers cannot assess the relative importance or significance of the changes they see, which can lead to misinterpretation. As one study participant noted, a difference chart "doesn't really tell you much—you'd need to have an accompanying chart or table to support the visual."

### Core Principle

Data visualizations should provide sufficient context for accurate interpretation. Showing only derived values like differences without their base values can be misleading.

## When it applies

- When the magnitude of the original values is important for interpreting the magnitude of the change.
- When viewers need to perform both comparison tasks (seeing the difference) and lookup tasks (seeing the original values).
- In most general-purpose dashboards where users have diverse and unpredictable questions.

## Exceptions

- When the original values are irrelevant, and the *only* point of interest is the absolute or relative difference. For example, charting a trade surplus/deficit where the specific import/export numbers don't matter.
- When the difference chart is presented alongside another chart that clearly shows the original values.

## Trade-offs

- A difference-only chart is visually simpler and can be very effective at its one specific task: showing differences. Avoiding it in favor of a more contextual chart (like a grouped bar with overlays) means accepting more visual complexity.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Unanswerable Questions:** Viewers ask, "Is that a big change?" or "What were the sales numbers before?" and the chart cannot answer them.
- **Misinterpretation:** Viewers draw incorrect conclusions, such as assuming the category with the largest absolute change is the "most important" change.
- **Need for Second Chart:** Viewers request a second chart or table showing the original data to make sense of the difference chart.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** Add annotations to the difference chart that specify the original values for key data points.
- **Moderate approach:** Place the difference chart adjacent to a chart showing the original values (e.g., a grouped bar chart). Use interaction like brushing and linking to connect them.
- **Comprehensive approach:** Use a single, integrated design like a grouped bar chart with a difference overlay. This provides both the original values and the difference in one unified view.

## Guidance

When comparing data series, do not rely on the mere absence of a visual mark (like a missing bar or overlay) to indicate that data is missing. Instead, use an explicit and distinct visual cue.

## Why

The absence of a mark is a weak and ambiguous signal. Viewers can easily overlook it or, more dangerously, misinterpret it as a value of zero. This can lead to flawed analysis and incorrect conclusions. An explicit signal, such as a different color, a special symbol, or a text label, removes this ambiguity and clearly communicates the status of the data. The study authors found this kind of subtle, absence-based cue was not effective for helping users identify categories with missing values.

### Core Principle

Be explicit about uncertainty and missing data. Ambiguity in data representation can be more damaging than showing no data at all, as it can lead to a false sense of certainty and incorrect insights.

## When it applies

- When comparing two or more series where some categories may not have data in all series (e.g., comparing product sales year-over-year when some products are new or discontinued).
- In any chart where a value of zero is a possible and meaningful value, to distinguish it from a value that is missing, not applicable, or unknown.

## Exceptions

- None. Clearly distinguishing missing data from zero is a fundamental principle of honest data representation.

## Trade-offs

- Adding explicit cues for missing data introduces more visual elements to the chart. However, this is a necessary complexity to ensure accurate interpretation.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Zero vs. N/A Confusion:** Viewers cannot tell if an empty spot in the chart means "the value is zero" or "the value is unknown."
- **Incorrect Counts:** Viewers incorrectly count the number of items in a category because they misinterpret missing data.
- **Erroneous Analysis:** Viewers make claims based on assuming a missing value is zero (e.g., "Sales for this product dropped to zero," when the product was simply discontinued and data collection stopped).

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add a Footnote.** Add a text footnote to the chart explaining how missing data is represented (e.g., "Blank spaces indicate data not available"). This is better than nothing but still relies on the user reading the fine print.
- **Moderate approach: Use a Distinct Style.** Represent the missing data point with a visually distinct mark. For example, if you have a grouped bar chart, you could show a low-opacity, greyed-out bar in the place of the missing one.
- **Comprehensive approach: Add Direct Annotations.** For each instance of missing data, add a direct text label (e.g., "N/A," "New," "Discontinued") to the chart at the location of the missing data point. This is the most explicit and least ambiguous method.
