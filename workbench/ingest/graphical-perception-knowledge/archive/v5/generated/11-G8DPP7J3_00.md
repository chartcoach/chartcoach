## Guidance

When creating or refining a color palette for categorical data, use an optimization algorithm to systematically increase the minimum perceptual distance (e.g., CIEDE2000) between all pairs of colors in the palette.

## Why

Manually selected or brand-based color palettes often contain pairs of colors that are difficult for people to distinguish, especially when they appear as small points or thin lines in a chart. This is a significant issue for viewers with color vision deficiencies. By algorithmically maximizing the perceptual 'space' between colors, you make it easier and faster for all viewers to differentiate categories, which reduces cognitive load and the risk of misinterpretation.

### Core Principle

Make the most important visual distinctions the easiest to perceive. For categorical color, this means ensuring every color is clearly and unambiguously distinct from every other color.

## When it applies

- **Multi-category charts:** This is especially critical when using color to encode more than 3-4 distinct categories.
- **Refining existing palettes:** Use this to improve an existing palette (e.g., from a brand guide or a legacy system) that performs poorly.
- **Small marks:** The benefits are greatest when colors are applied to small marks like dots in a scatterplot, thin lines in a line chart, or small regions on a map.

## Exceptions

- **Aesthetic priority:** In situations where a specific aesthetic mood (e.g., using only muted earth tones) is more important than maximal discriminability and the risk of confusion is low or acceptable.
- **Strong conventions:** When a convention is so strong that any change would cause more confusion (though the research shows even these can be subtly improved without breaking the convention).

## Trade-offs

- **Aesthetics vs. Clarity:** A purely algorithmic optimization might produce a palette that is maximally discriminable but aesthetically jarring or unappealing. This often requires a final human touch or the use of constraints.
- **Semantic Integrity:** Without constraints, optimization can alter brand or semantic colors beyond recognition, sacrificing meaning for technical performance.

## Signs of Trouble

- **The Squint Test:** If you squint at your visualization, do different colored categories blend into one another?
- **Subtle Neighbors:** Does your palette contain two very similar colors (e.g., a light green and a teal, or two shades of blue) that are hard to tell apart, especially when they are not adjacent in the legend?
- **Legend Deception:** The colors look distinct in large swatches in the legend but become indistinguishable when used as small points or thin lines in the chart.
- **Back-and-Forth:** Do viewers have to repeatedly look between the legend and the chart to be sure which category is which?

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Manual Tweak:** Identify the single worst-performing color pair in your palette (the two most similar colors). Manually adjust the lightness or saturation of one of them until they are clearly distinct.

- **Moderate Approach: Constrained Optimization:** Use a tool like Colourmap Hospital. Import your existing palette, but apply constraints to preserve its essential character. For example, lock the hue of a "red" category but allow the tool to optimize its saturation and lightness for maximum distance from other colors.

- **Comprehensive Approach: Generate from Scratch:** For a new visualization without pre-existing color constraints, use a tool like Colorgorical to generate a new, optimized palette from scratch based on the number of categories you need.
