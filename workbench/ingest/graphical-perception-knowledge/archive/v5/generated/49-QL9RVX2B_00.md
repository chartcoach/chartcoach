## Guidance

When choosing between a pie chart and a treemap to show a simple part-to-whole relationship with a small number of categories, prefer the pie chart.

## Why

Despite common criticism of pie charts, empirical evidence suggests they are more effective than treemaps for simple composition tasks. A 2019 study found that people can judge the proportions in pie charts more accurately than in treemaps. The varied aspect ratios of the rectangles in a treemap make direct area comparison perceptually difficult, leading to higher error rates.

### Core Principle

Visual encodings that are easier for the human brain to decode lead to more accurate and faster data interpretation. For simple part-to-whole comparisons, the combination of angle and area in a pie chart appears to be more decodable than the variable-aspect-ratio areas of a treemap.

## When it applies

- When your primary task is to have the audience compare the proportions of a few (e.g., fewer than 10) non-hierarchical categories.
- When you are considering a treemap as a "modern" alternative to a pie chart for showing simple composition.

## Exceptions

- If the data is hierarchical (i.e., has nested sub-categories), a treemap is the appropriate choice. Treemaps are specifically designed to visualize nested structures, a task which pie charts cannot perform.
- If you have a very large number of categories, neither chart may be effective, but a treemap might handle the complexity better than a pie chart with dozens of tiny slivers.

## Trade-offs

- **Familiarity vs. Performance:** While treemaps may seem more novel or space-efficient, you sacrifice perceptual accuracy for these aesthetics in a simple composition context.
- **Limited Scope:** This guideline only states a preference between a pie chart and a treemap. It does not imply a pie chart is the *overall best* choice. A bar chart often outperforms both for comparison tasks.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Aspect Ratio Confusion:** In your treemap, you have two segments that are similar in area but have very different shapes (e.g., one is a wide rectangle, the other is a tall, thin one). It's nearly impossible for a viewer to tell which is larger without reading labels.
- **You're using a treemap for non-hierarchical data**, such as the market share of five competing companies.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix:** If you have a treemap showing simple composition, replace it with a pie chart. This is a direct swap that the evidence suggests will improve accuracy.
- **Moderate Redesign:** If you must use a treemap, ensure every rectangle is clearly labeled with its value and/or percentage. This provides a "cognitive escape hatch," allowing users to read the values instead of relying on flawed area perception.
- **Comprehensive Approach:** For the highest accuracy in part-to-whole comparisons, replace either chart with a simple bar chart. Bar charts use length on a common, aligned scale, which is one of the most accurately perceived visual encodings.
