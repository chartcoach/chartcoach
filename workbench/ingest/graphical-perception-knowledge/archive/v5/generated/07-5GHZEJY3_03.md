## Guidance

To distinguish between nominal (unordered) categories, the most effective visual channels to use (after position has been used for the main axes) are **color hue**, **texture**, and **shape**. These allow for quick and accurate identification of different groups without implying a false order.

## Why

According to established perceptual rankings, viewers can quickly and accurately differentiate a limited number of distinct hues or shapes. These channels are ideal for nominal data because they are not intuitively perceived as ordered, which aligns with the unordered nature of the data. Using an ordered channel like color saturation or size would violate the expressiveness principle by implying a ranking that doesn't exist.

### Core Principle

Use visual channels that are perceptually distinct but unordered to encode data that is categorically different but unordered.

## When it applies

-   When assigning colors to different categories in a legend.
-   When choosing how to represent different groups of points in a scatter plot.
-   When styling different lines in a multi-series line chart.
-   When representing categorical data on a map with glyphs or symbols.

## Exceptions

-   **High Cardinality:** The number of easily distinguishable hues is small (around 5-8). For nominal data with many categories, relying on color hue alone can lead to confusion as colors become too similar. In such cases, direct labels, interactivity (hovering), or breaking the chart into small multiples may be necessary.
-   **Brand Constraints:** If you are required to use a limited brand color palette, you may not have enough distinct hues available. You might need to supplement with other channels like shape or rely on interactivity.

## Trade-offs

-   **Accessibility:** Relying solely on color hue can make a chart unreadable for viewers with color vision deficiency. It is a best practice to use a redundant encoding (e.g., color *and* shape, or color *and* different dash styles for lines) to improve accessibility.
-   **Clutter vs. Clarity:** Using many different shapes or complex textures can add visual clutter to a chart, even if they are technically distinct. Color hue is often less cluttering than shape or texture.

## Signs of Trouble

-   **Similar Colors:** Your color palette includes two or more colors that are difficult to tell apart (e.g., a light green and a light teal).
-   **Back-and-Forth:** Viewers have to repeatedly look back and forth between the chart and the legend to identify which category is which.
-   **Accessibility Failure:** When viewed with a colorblindness simulator, two or more categories become indistinguishable.
-   **False Ordering:** Using different saturations of one color (light blue, dark blue) for nominal data, making viewers think there's an order.

## How to Improve

-   **Quick Fix: Use a Color Palette Tool.** Use a tool designed for generating qualitative (categorical) color palettes, such as ColorBrewer or Viz Palette. These tools are built to maximize perceptual distance between colors and often include colorblind-safe options.
-   **Moderate Redesign: Add Redundant Encoding.** Don't rely on color alone. Pair color with shape for scatter plots (e.g., red circles, blue squares) or with dash styles for line charts (e.g., solid red line, dashed blue line). This makes the chart more robust and accessible.
-   **Comprehensive Approach: Rethink the Chart Type.** If you have too many categories to distinguish effectively with color or shape, consider a different approach. A bar or dot plot can use position to separate the categories, with direct labels for each one. Alternatively, small multiples can be used to give each category its own small chart, eliminating the need for a complex legend.
