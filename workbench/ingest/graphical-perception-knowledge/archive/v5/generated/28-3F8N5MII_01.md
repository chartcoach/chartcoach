## Guidance

If a colormap must be displayed on different background colors (e.g., in a presentation with both light and dark slide themes), use a colormap that does not appear to vary in opacity.

## Why

Colormaps that appear to vary in opacity (typically those created by interpolating a single color to the background color) create a powerful "opaque-is-more" perceptual bias. On a light background, this reinforces the default "dark-is-more" bias, aiding comprehension. However, on a dark background, the two biases conflict: the darker color signifies "more," but the lighter, more opaque color *also* signifies "more." This conflict slows down interpretation and can lead to errors. Using a colormap that is not perceived as varying in opacity makes the "dark-is-more" principle dominant and reliable on any background.

### Core Principle

Design for perceptual consistency. A visualization's interpretation should not change dramatically when its context, such as the background color, is altered.

## When it applies

- When creating a visualization that will be used in multiple contexts, such as a report that will be printed (white background) and a presentation that will be projected (potentially dark background).
- When creating style guides or templates for visualizations that need to be robust.

## Exceptions

- If you can guarantee the visualization will *only* ever be viewed on a light background. In this specific case, you can leverage both the "dark-is-more" and "opaque-is-more" biases by using a colormap that appears to vary in opacity (e.g., a sequential single-hue palette) to create a maximally intuitive chart.

## Trade-offs

- Choosing a colormap that is robust across backgrounds may mean sacrificing the "extra" perceptual boost that comes from aligning the "dark-is-more" and "opaque-is-more" biases on a light background. The visualization may be slightly less intuitive in the light-background-only case, but it will be far more consistent overall.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Context Collapse:** A chart that was clear on your white-background report becomes confusing when you paste it into a dark-background presentation slide.
- **Conflicting Cues on Dark Mode:** On a dark background, viewers are unsure whether the lightest, most-contrasting color or the darkest color represents the highest value.
- **Opacity Illusion:** The colormap looks like a translucent layer placed over the background, where some colors look "see-through." This is a strong indicator that the "opaque-is-more" bias is in effect.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Check on Both Backgrounds.** Before finalizing your visualization, test it by placing it on both a pure white and a pure black background. If the interpretation feels different or ambiguous on one, your colormap is not robust.
- **Moderate Approach: Choose a Curving Colormap.** Select a colormap that curves significantly through color space, such as a multi-hue sequential map (e.g., MATLAB's 'Hot' or 'Viridis'). Because these palettes don't follow a straight line to white or black, they are less likely to be perceived as a simple opacity scale against either background.
- **Comprehensive Approach: Quantify Opacity Variation.** As demonstrated in Schloss et al. (2019), you can measure how much a colormap deviates from a linear interpolation between its most contrasting color and the background color. Colormaps with high deviation (a high "Opacity Variation Index") are less likely to appear to vary in opacity and are therefore more robust.
