## Guidance

When designing layouts where comparison is a key task, minimize the spatial distance between the graphical elements being compared.

## Why

The human visual system can make more accurate comparisons between adjacent or nearby objects. As the distance between two objects increases, viewers must scan their eyes back and forth and rely more on working memory to hold one value while viewing the other. This process is slower and introduces perceptual and cognitive errors.

### Core Principle

Reducing the distance for visual comparisons reduces the cognitive load required to perform the task, leading to faster and more accurate insights.

## When it applies

- When choosing the sort order for categories in a bar chart, dot plot, or list. Sorting by value places similar-sized items next to each other.
- When laying out small multiples in a grid. A compact grid (e.g., 3x4) is often better for comparison than a long, linear one (e.g., 1x12).
- When designing grouped bar charts or dot plots.

## Exceptions

- When a specific, non-value-based ordering is more important for the task. For example, sorting categories alphabetically is better for lookup tasks. Sorting by time (chronologically) is necessary to see trends.
- When the layout is constrained by other factors, such as a geographical map.

## Trade-offs

- Sorting by value to improve comparison will disrupt any "natural" order (like alphabetical or chronological), which can make lookup tasks more difficult. The optimal sort order depends on the primary task.

## Signs of Trouble

- **Back-and-Forth Scanning:** You notice viewers have to scan their eyes repeatedly across a wide area of the chart to make a single comparison.
- **Ruler Use:** Viewers try to use their finger or a piece of paper as a straight-edge to compare two distant elements.
- **Difficulty with Extremes:** It is hard to compare the first and last bars in a long, alphabetically sorted bar chart.

## How to Improve

- **Quick Fix: Add Highlighting or Guide Lines.** If you cannot change the layout, use color or faint background lines to visually connect the distant items you want the user to compare. This guides the eye and reduces the scanning effort.

- **Moderate Redesign: Re-sort the Data.** If the primary task is comparison or ranking, sort your chart by the quantitative value (descending or ascending). This is the single most effective way to improve comparisons in a bar or dot chart.

- **Comprehensive Redesign: Re-think the Layout.** For small multiples, reconsider the grid arrangement. Could a more compact grid bring key comparisons closer? Could you overlay two key plots instead of placing them side-by-side?
